## The Verses

> **27** Wherefore whosoever shall eat this bread, and drink this cup of the Lord, unworthily, shall be guilty of the body and blood of the Lord.
> **28** But let a man examine himself, and so let him eat of that bread, and drink of that cup.
> **29** For he that eateth and drinketh unworthily, eateth and drinketh damnation to himself, not discerning the Lord's body.
> **30** For this cause many are weak and sickly among you, and many sleep.
> **31** For if we would judge ourselves, we should not be judged.
> **32** But when we are judged, we are chastened of the Lord, that we should not be condemned with the world.
>
> — 1 Corinthians 11:27-32 (KJV)

## What Paul Is Doing

Movement A diagnosed the abuse. Movement B re-told the meal's meaning. Movement C draws the conclusion. Because the meal *is* what Movement B says it is, eating it the way Movement A described carries weight — *anaxiōs* (in an unworthy manner). Paul names the warning, prescribes self-examination, names the consequence already visible in the community, and frames the consequence as discipline aimed at *not* condemnation.

Three topics open here that deserve careful work: what *unworthily* means (and what it does not mean), what *discerning the body* means (the great crux of the passage), and what Paul means by *judgment* / *chastening*. The bodily-consequences question of v. 30 is treated separately in the [Health Question](/1-corinthians-11-17-34/health-question/) page, which Movement C will cross-reference rather than duplicate.

## Topic C1 — "Unworthily" (v. 27, ἀναξίως)

> "whosoever shall eat this bread, and drink this cup of the Lord, unworthily, shall be guilty of the body and blood of the Lord" (v. 27, KJV)

The Greek is ἀναξίως — an *adverb*. It modifies the *manner* of eating, not the *worth of the person*. This is one of the most consequentially mistranslated points in pastoral practice in the English-speaking church.

| Translation | Rendering |
|---|---|
| KJV | "unworthily" |
| ESV | "in an unworthy manner" |
| NASB | "in an unworthy manner" |
| NIV | "in an unworthy manner" |

Modern translations are clearer than KJV here. The English adverb "unworthily" sounds like a comment on the person's worthiness ("you're not worthy enough to come"); the Greek says no such thing. *Anaxiōs* modifies *how* one eats. The question is whether the manner of one's eating is *worthy of the meal* — not whether one is *worthy of God*.

This is decisive for pastoral application. The over-scrupulous reading — "I am not worthy, so I should not come" — drives Christians away from the Table on a misreading. No one is worthy in that sense; the meal is for the unworthy. The question is whether you are coming *worthily* — discerning what the meal is, treating the community as the body the meal makes.

### The historical mis-application

The mediaeval and Reformation practice of withholding communion from those who felt unworthy traces partly to this misreading. Cranmer's first exhortation in the 1549 Prayer Book gets the sense right: "Examine your lives and conversations… repent you of your sins… draw near with faith." The aim is preparation, not exclusion. The later Puritan and revivalist traditions sometimes drifted toward exclusionary reading — Christians who "felt unworthy" were urged not to come. That is the opposite of Paul's pastoral logic. Paul's remedy in v. 28 is *examine and then eat*, not *examine and then abstain*.

### "Guilty of the body and blood"

The phrase ἔνοχος τοῦ σώματος καὶ τοῦ αἵματος is severe. *Enochos* is a juridical term — "liable for," "accountable for." To eat the Supper unworthily is to be *answerable for* the body and blood of the Lord. The sense is something like "complicit in the crucifixion" — eating the meal that proclaims his death without discerning what one is doing places one on the side of those who did not see what they were doing when they crucified him (cf. Luke 23:34, "they know not what they do").

This is severe but not annihilating. The very next verse (v. 28) prescribes a remedy: *examine yourself, and so let him eat*. The point of the severity in v. 27 is not to exclude but to wake up. The Corinthians have been treating a momentous thing trivially; Paul names the momentousness so the triviality can stop.

## Topic C2 — Self-Examination (v. 28, δοκιμαζέτω)

> "But let a man examine himself, and so let him eat of that bread, and drink of that cup." (v. 28, KJV)

The verb is δοκιμαζέτω — "let him test/approve." Same root as δόκιμοι in v. 19 ("they which are approved"). The thread connects: in v. 19 Paul said factions reveal who is approved; in v. 28 he tells each one to do the approving on himself.

### What examination is *for*

Two related readings:

**View 1 — Examination for moral state.** The believer examines conscience for unconfessed sin, broken relationships, unrepented patterns. The Catholic and Orthodox sacrament of confession (or the Anglican exhortation) takes this reading: prepare yourself by reckoning with what is between you and God before approaching the Table.

**View 2 — Examination for discernment.** The believer examines whether she is *seeing* the body — both the broken body of Christ that the meal proclaims and the gathered body of Christ at the Table with her. The Corinthians' failure was not (only) moral but discerning: they were not *seeing* what the meal was and who was at it.

The text supports both. The flow of vv. 27-29 is: unworthy manner → examine yourself → eat → because failing to discern the body brings judgment. Self-examination ought to include both moral reckoning *and* discernment of what the meal is. The Corinthian sin was a moral-and-discernment failure at once: they were callous toward the poor (moral) and they did not see that the poor at the Table *were* the body whose meal this was (discernment).

### Examination, not exclusion

Note again the syntax of v. 28: *examine himself, and so let him eat*. Paul does not say "examine, and if you find unworthiness, abstain." He says examine, and *then eat*. Examination is *preparation for eating*, not a precondition for hesitating about whether to eat. The pastoral application matters: examination is the on-ramp to the meal, not the off-ramp.

## Topic C3 — Discerning the Body (v. 29, διακρίνων τὸ σῶμα)

> "For he that eateth and drinketh unworthily, eateth and drinketh damnation to himself, not discerning the Lord's body." (v. 29, KJV)

This is the great interpretive crux of the passage. *What body* is one failing to discern?

The textual note from the [translation comparison](/1-corinthians-11-17-34/01-text/#v-29--not-discerning-the-lords-body) is important: "the Lord's" is a later manuscript addition; the earliest texts read simply *to sōma* ("the body"). The KJV "the Lord's body" reflects the Byzantine tradition; modern critical texts read just "the body." The shorter, earlier reading is *more* ambiguous than the longer one — and the ambiguity is theologically loaded.

### View 1 — The eucharistic body of Christ

**The position:** "The body" is the body Christ gave for them — the body present in the bread (in whatever sense one's eucharistic theology holds). To fail to discern it is to treat the bread as common bread, to miss what is on the Table. The judgment falls because one has trampled the body of the Lord.

**Who holds it:** Catholic, Orthodox, Lutheran, and much of the Reformed tradition. Augustine, Aquinas, Luther, Calvin. The Byzantine textual addition of "of the Lord" reflects this reading.

**The argument:**
1. *Sōma* in v. 24 (just five verses earlier) refers to Christ's body — "this is my body." The natural antecedent for *to sōma* in v. 29 is the *sōma* of v. 24.
2. The warning of v. 27 ("guilty of the body and blood of the Lord") is explicitly Christological. *To sōma* in v. 29 picks up *tou sōmatos* from v. 27.
3. The traditional eucharistic theology of the Fathers reads *to sōma* this way nearly unanimously.

**The argument's strongest expression:** A Catholic or Orthodox theologian would say the entire pastoral logic of the passage requires this reading. The meal is the body of the Lord; not to discern that *is* the failure. Read otherwise, the passage loses its sacramental gravity and becomes mere ecclesial-ethics scolding.

### View 2 — The body of Christ as the church

**The position:** "The body" is the gathered ecclesial body — the congregation as the body of Christ. To fail to discern it is to fail to *see* the gathered church as Christ's body and to act accordingly. The Corinthians ate while the poor went hungry; that is the failure to discern the body.

**Who holds it:** Many modern Protestant commentators (Fee, Thiselton, Hays, Witherington); some Reformed and Anglican scholars; nearly all social-historical readings (Theissen, Meeks).

**The argument:**
1. Movement A's diagnosis is that the Corinthians have failed to honor the church as the body. This is the *immediate* failure; the eucharistic body has not been the explicit theme.
2. Paul's "body of Christ" ecclesiology is developed at length one chapter later (1 Cor 12:12-27) — "ye are the body of Christ, and members in particular" (12:27). The vocabulary is in Paul's hand.
3. The textually earliest reading is *to sōma* — just "the body," not "the body of the Lord." The ambiguity is genuine and Paul *could* have written *tou kyriou* if he meant only the eucharistic body.
4. The "for this cause many are weak and sickly" of v. 30 lands on bodies in the community — the bodily harm falls where Greco-Roman society distributes harm, on the poor. That is the body Paul has been concerned about throughout Movement A.

**The argument's strongest expression:** A modern social-historical reader would say this reading takes Paul at his actual word and in his actual context. The Corinthian problem is not metaphysical confusion about the bread; it is a stratified meal that humiliates the poor. Of course "the body" Paul wants them to discern is *the body they are crushing* — the body of Christ that is the gathered church.

### View 3 — Both bodies at once

**The position:** "The body" is deliberately undifferentiated. To discern the eucharistic body is *also* to discern the ecclesial body, and vice versa. One cannot rightly discern the bread without discerning the church gathered around the bread; one cannot rightly discern the church without seeing the bread that makes it. Paul is not choosing between the two; he is naming the single reality that the Corinthians have split.

**Who holds it:** Many careful patristic readers; the modern ecumenical liturgical movement; Henri de Lubac's *Corpus Mysticum*; readings influenced by his retrieval of the patristic identification of the eucharistic body and the ecclesial body. Many contemporary Catholic and Reformed theologians.

**The argument:**
1. The ambiguity of *to sōma* is not a problem to be resolved but a theological signal. Paul writes ambiguously because both readings are right and *together* are right.
2. Augustine's *Sermon 272* makes the same move: "Be what you see; receive what you are." The bread you see *is* the body of Christ; the body of Christ *is* what the church is. The two are not the same but they are inseparable.
3. The two readings produce the same pastoral judgment. If discerning the eucharistic body would lead one to revere the meal, that reverence cannot live alongside humiliating the poor at the same meal. If discerning the ecclesial body would lead one to honor the poor, that honor cannot live alongside trivializing the bread that makes them one body.

**The argument's strongest expression:** A patristic-minded theologian would say this is the *original* reading. The split between "the eucharistic body" and "the ecclesial body" is a later Latin scholastic distinction. For the early church, the bread *was* the body that made them the body. To discern *to sōma* was to discern both at once, because they are one reality.

### Comparing the three

| Claim | View 1 (Eucharistic) | View 2 (Ecclesial) | View 3 (Both) |
|---|---|---|---|
| *To sōma* refers to Christ's body in the bread | Yes | Indirectly | Yes |
| *To sōma* refers to the church | No (or secondary) | Yes | Yes |
| The Corinthian failure is sacramental | Yes | Indirectly | Yes |
| The Corinthian failure is ecclesial-ethical | Indirectly | Yes | Yes |
| The textually earliest reading is preferred | — | Yes | Yes |
| The Pauline body-of-Christ ecclesiology is in view | — | Yes | Yes |
| Both readings together best fit the text | — | — | Yes |

View 3 is the position the present writer finds most fully accountable to the text. But Views 1 and 2 each capture half of what View 3 holds together, and the long history of the church has held each separately with serious results. The right move is probably not to pick one but to hold the two together — discerning the body is discerning *both* what is on the Table *and* who is at the Table, because they are one body.

## Topic C4 — Judgment and Chastening (vv. 30-32)

> "For this cause many are weak and sickly among you, and many sleep. For if we would judge ourselves, we should not be judged. But when we are judged, we are chastened of the Lord, that we should not be condemned with the world." (vv. 30-32, KJV)

The passage shifts from warning to diagnosis: the failure to discern is *already* producing bodily consequence in the Corinthian church. Paul has not been speaking hypothetically; the judgment is here.

### v. 30 — The bodily question

The three views on v. 30 — Direct divine judgment, Natural consequence of broken community, Pentecostal healing reading — are developed in the [Health Question (v. 30)](/1-corinthians-11-17-34/health-question/) page. The summary is:

- **View 1 (Direct):** God is presently striking the Corinthians with illness and death because they are profaning the Supper. Held by patristic majority; grounds the Catholic/Orthodox preparation discipline.
- **View 2 (Natural):** The community itself is sick — the poor go hungry, the weak go unsupported, the result is bodily harm distributed along the same lines Greco-Roman society distributed it. Held by Theissen and modern social-historical readings.
- **View 3 (Healing):** If unworthy reception produces sickness, then worthy reception participates in healing — the Supper is medicinal. Held by Pentecostal and charismatic traditions.

The full development, including how the views overlap and whether they continue to apply, is in the Health Question page. Movement C does not relitigate the question here.

### vv. 31-32 — Self-judgment, divine judgment, chastening

The verbs in vv. 31-32 cluster around the *krin-* root — *diakrinō* (discern, v. 29), *diekrinomen* (we discerned, v. 31), *ekrinometha / krinomenoi* (we are judged, v. 31-32), *katakrinomen* (we are condemned, v. 32). Paul is playing on the same word in different registers:

- *Discern* the body → and you avoid being *judged*.
- *Judge* yourselves → and you avoid being *judged* (by the Lord).
- When judged by the Lord, you are *chastened* — *not condemned*.

The English vocabulary loses what the Greek holds together. *Diakrinō* (discern) and *krinō* (judge) are word-cousins. The right discernment is itself a self-judgment that pre-empts external judgment. The Lord's judgment, when it falls, is *paideia* (παιδευόμεθα — "we are being trained as a child") — corrective, not destructive.

### *Paideia* — discipline as child-training

The Greek verb παιδευόμεθα is rich. *Paideia* is the Greek word for the formation of a child into a mature person; it includes instruction, correction, and (when necessary) chastening. The Septuagint uses it for the Lord's training of Israel (Deut 8:5, "as a man chasteneth his son, so the Lord thy God chasteneth thee"); Hebrews 12:5-11 cites Proverbs to the same effect ("whom the Lord loveth he chasteneth").

The judgment on the Corinthian church is not condemnation. It is the Lord's training. The bodily harm is severe; the *purpose* of the severity is restoration. This is the theological key to vv. 30-32: the sickness, the deaths, even the failure to discern — when met with self-examination and correction, they become the Lord's *paideia*, "that we should not be condemned with the world."

The pastoral implication is decisive. The Corinthian church under the Lord's chastening is still *the Lord's church* — not handed over, not abandoned, not lost. The chastening is *because* they are his. Hebrews 12:8 makes the inverse explicit: those whom God does not chasten are not his sons.

### "Not condemned with the world"

The last clause of v. 32 lifts the eye. Paul has been speaking about a community under judgment for misuse of the Supper. But *they are not condemned with the world*. The world's path leads to *katakrima* — condemnation. The church's path, even under chastening, leads to *not-katakrima* — formation, training, eventual perfection.

This frames the Supper differently than a moralistic reading would. The Supper *is* dangerous — Paul has said so plainly. But the danger is *paideia*, not *katakrima*. The danger is the danger of a child being corrected by a father who loves him, not the danger of a defendant standing before a hostile judge. The Corinthian believer under chastening is in the same position as the prodigal whose father has run out to meet him: in trouble, yes; corrected, yes; still in the father's house.

## Reading Movement C in Light of the Whole

Movement A diagnosed the abuse. Movement B re-told the meal's meaning. Movement C draws the warning and the remedy.

- The *manner* matters (v. 27).
- The remedy is *self-examination*, not abstention (v. 28).
- The *failure* is failure to discern the body — both bodies, in the reading taken here (v. 29).
- The *consequence* is bodily, present, real — handled fully in the Health Question page (v. 30).
- The *frame* is *paideia*, not condemnation (vv. 31-32). The church under judgment is still the church.

Movement D will close the passage with Paul's practical instructions — wait for one another, eat at home, the rest will be set in order when he comes.
