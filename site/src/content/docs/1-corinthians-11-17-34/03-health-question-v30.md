## The Verse

> "For this cause many are weak and sickly among you, and many sleep." — 1 Corinthians 11:30 (KJV)

> "For this reason many are weak and sick among you, and a number sleep." — NASB

> "That is why many of you are weak and sick, and a number of you have fallen asleep." — NIV

The Greek leaves no doubt about the literalism:

- **ἀσθενεῖς** (*astheneis*) — weak, usually of physical illness (the same word Paul uses for himself in 2 Cor 12:10 of his "infirmity")
- **ἄρρωστοι** (*arrōstoi*) — sickly, infirm, diseased — stronger than *astheneis*
- **κοιμῶνται** (*koimōntai*) — sleep, the standard NT euphemism for death (cf. 1 Thess 4:13–15; John 11:11; Acts 7:60)

So Paul is saying: many are weak, many are sick, and some are dying — *and this is connected* (διὰ τοῦτο, "for this cause") to the way the Corinthians are misusing the Supper.

The interpretive question is *not* what the Greek says — it is unambiguous. The interpretive question is **what kind of connection** Paul is drawing, and **whether it still applies**.

Three live views, each with serious defenders. None can be dismissed as fringe.

---

## View 1: Direct Divine Judgment

**The position:** God is presently striking the Corinthians with illness and death because they are taking the Supper unworthily. The connection between abuse and bodily harm is a direct act of God, comparable to other instances of divine discipline in Scripture (Acts 5:1–11 with Ananias and Sapphira; 1 Cor 5:5; the OT examples of cultic transgression).

**Who holds it:** This is the patristic majority view. Chrysostom is explicit; Augustine assumes it. It carries forward into Catholic and Eastern Orthodox sacramental theology, where it grounds the practice of confession-before-communion and the church's right to "fence" the Table.

**The argument:**

1. The grammar is causal. διὰ τοῦτο at the head of v. 30 names the unworthy reception (vv. 27–29) as the *cause* of the illnesses. Paul does not write "incidentally, many are sick" — he writes "*for this cause* many are sick."
2. The verses immediately following (vv. 31–32) develop the same theme: "if we would judge ourselves, we should not be judged. But when we are judged, we are chastened of the Lord." This is the language of active divine discipline, not passive consequence. The verb παιδευόμεθα ("we are chastened/disciplined as a child") in v. 32 has God as agent.
3. The pattern is consistent with biblical precedent. Cultic transgression has always carried bodily consequences (Lev 10:1–3; 2 Sam 6:6–7; Acts 5).
4. The pastoral implication is real: the church must take care that the Table is not profaned, because actual harm follows from profaning it.

**The argument's strongest expression:**
A Catholic or Orthodox writer would point out that this view takes Paul seriously *as Paul wrote*. The text does not say "be careful or you might feel bad about yourself" — it says you are weak, sick, and dying, *because of this*. To soften that is to read modern sensibilities back into a text that does not have them. The cup of the new covenant is not casually approached; the holy God of Sinai has not become the indulgent grandfather of modern devotion.

**Implications for practice:**
- Confession (formal or informal) before receiving
- "Fencing the Table" (excluding those not in good standing)
- Preparation rites, fasts before communion
- Pastoral oversight of who receives

## View 2: Natural Consequence of Broken Community

**The position:** When the body of Christ at Corinth eats in a way that humiliates the poor and reinforces social hierarchy, the community is *itself* sick — and that sickness manifests literally because the community is no longer caring for its weakest members. The poor go hungry; the weak go unsupported; the sick are not visited; the result is real bodily harm distributed exactly where Greco-Roman society always distributed harm: on the bodies of the poor.

**Who holds it:** Many modern Protestant commentators, especially those reading through the social-historical lens (Theissen and his successors). Strong in mainline Reformed and Anglican exegesis; common in liberation-theology readings; increasingly common in evangelical scholarship since the 1980s.

**The argument:**

1. Paul has just spent vv. 17–22 diagnosing a *specific* social abuse: the wealthy gorging while the poor go hungry. The natural reading of v. 30 — *"for this cause"* — points back to that specific abuse, not to a generalized unworthiness.
2. "Discerning the body" in v. 29 has chapter 12 within reach. The body of Christ — both eucharistic and ecclesial — is one. To eat the bread while the body of Christ goes hungry at the same table is to misread what the meal *is*.
3. The verb παιδευόμεθα in v. 32 ("we are being disciplined") does not require a direct interventionist mechanism. God's discipline can work through the natural consequences of broken community. The agent is still God; the mechanism is the breakdown the community has chosen.
4. This view honors the Lukan parallel: the rich man and Lazarus (Luke 16:19–31) shows what happens when a brother goes hungry at your gate. Paul is saying: that is what you are doing at the Table. The bodily consequences fall on the same kind of people.

**The argument's strongest expression:**
Theissen and those after him have shown that Paul's correction makes more pastoral sense if it is *specific*. Paul is not making a general theological claim about cultic transgression. He is naming a particular abuse with particular consequences. The poor are hungry, weak, and dying *because the rich are not sharing the meal with them*. That is a "natural consequence" only in the sense that God built the world such that withholding food from the hungry causes them to die. The "judgment" is the judgment built into reality, which is also the judgment of God.

**Implications for practice:**
- Attention to economic justice within the church
- The Table as a site of class-equalization, not class-display
- Hospitality — especially toward "those who have not" — as a sacramental discipline
- Pastoral care that traces bodily harm in the congregation back to communal failures

## View 3: The Pentecostal / Charismatic Healing Reading

**The position:** If failing to discern the body produces sickness, then *rightly* discerning the body should produce wholeness. The Supper is not only memorial — it is medicinal. The body broken "for you" includes the work of the cross by which Christ bore "our griefs" and "carried our sorrows" (Isa 53:4); rightly received, the Supper participates in that healing work. Many Christians are weak and sick because they are not discerning the body as a healing reality.

**Who holds it:** Common in Pentecostal, charismatic, Word of Faith, and renewal traditions. Has antecedents in earlier holiness teaching (the "fourfold gospel" of Jesus as Savior, Sanctifier, Healer, and Coming King — A. B. Simpson, late 19th century). Currently held in various forms by many global Pentecostal communities.

**The argument:**

1. Paul connects worthy reception to bodily health directly. If the failure mode is sickness, the success mode is health. Otherwise the connection is asymmetric in a way Paul does not signal.
2. The "body broken for you" (v. 24) language is the same logic as Isaiah 53 — the suffering servant whose stripes bring healing (Isa 53:5; 1 Pet 2:24). The Supper, as the proclamation of the Lord's death (v. 26), participates in *what that death accomplished* — which includes bodily healing.
3. "Not discerning the body" (v. 29) is read as failing to recognize the Supper as the medicine of immortality (Ignatius's phrase, c. 110 AD). Right discernment receives the healing the Supper carries.
4. The early church's healing practices were closely connected to the Eucharist (oil consecrated at the eucharistic table, the sick brought forward). This view continues that tradition.

**The argument's strongest expression:**
A serious Pentecostal writer would argue that the Reformed and Catholic traditions stopped reading Paul at v. 30 because the church had largely lost the expectation of bodily healing. Read forward from v. 30: if Paul says "*for this cause* many are sick," and we ask what the alternative is, the answer cannot be "well, then they would be sick for a different reason." The alternative is that they would *not be sick* — because rightly discerning the body is a means of grace that includes wholeness.

**Implications for practice:**
- Anointing for healing within or alongside the Supper
- Specific prayer for the sick at communion
- Teaching that connects the Table to bodily restoration
- Anticipation that participation in the Supper carries real benefit, including physical

## Where the Views Overlap and Where They Diverge

| Claim | View 1 (Direct) | View 2 (Natural) | View 3 (Healing) |
|-------|----------------|-----------------|------------------|
| v. 30 names real bodily harm | Yes | Yes | Yes |
| The harm is caused by misuse | Yes (directly) | Yes (through broken community) | Yes |
| God is the agent | Yes (direct intervention) | Yes (through built-in consequence) | Yes |
| Right reception brings positive bodily effect | Implicit (protection from this kind of harm) | Implicit (a healthy community has fewer such cases) | Explicit (healing is given) |
| The dynamic still operates today | Yes | Yes (wherever a community misuses the Table) | Yes |
| The Catholic/Orthodox preparation discipline is warranted | Yes | Partly (for different reasons) | Often yes |
| Pentecostal healing claims at the Table are warranted | Skeptical | Generally skeptical | Yes |

Views 1 and 2 can be held together without contradiction. Many careful Reformed and Catholic exegetes hold a hybrid: God's discipline is real, and it often works *through* natural consequences in the community. The "God acted directly" vs. "the broken community produced the harm" distinction is, on this hybrid, a distinction without a sharp difference. Both are God's judgment; the second is the form the first usually takes.

View 3 is harder to combine with views that do not read healing as guaranteed-in-the-atonement. It can stand on its own theological ground, but it requires a different account of how the Supper relates to Isaiah 53 than most non-Pentecostal traditions hold.

## "But Does This Still Apply Today?"

Even granting one of the three views for Corinth specifically, there is a separate question: is this an apostolic-era diagnosis of a specific local situation, or a continuing dynamic?

Three positions on continuation:

1. **Fully continuing.** Catholic, Eastern Orthodox, and Pentecostal traditions all (for different reasons) read v. 30 as continuing to apply. Misuse of the Supper continues to bring bodily consequence; right use continues to carry bodily benefit. The pastoral discipline of preparation, examination, and (for Pentecostals) expectation of healing all assume this.
2. **Pastoral principle continues, specific dynamic uncertain.** Many Reformed and Anglican readings: take the principle (do not profane the Table) with full seriousness, without insisting that the specific bodily consequences of v. 30 continue. The church should always examine itself; whether God presently strikes communicants with illness for misuse is a question the text does not settle for our day.
3. **Apostolic-era only.** Some modern readings (often dispensationalist, sometimes higher-critical) take v. 30 as Paul's specific diagnosis of Corinth — applicable then, not necessarily a pattern for now. The pastoral lesson is that *Paul's* church saw bodily consequences of misuse; ours may not see them in the same way.

This is a place where your own theological priors will substantially shape the answer. The text does not foreclose any of the three. What it does foreclose is the *casual* approach to the Supper — the assumption that nothing is at stake, that no real body is on the table, that the way we eat together has no bearing on the way we live together.

## Honest Pastoral Notes

A few observations that do not belong to any single view but matter for application:

- **Paul is not asking the Corinthians to be worthy.** The adverb in v. 27 is ἀναξίως — it modifies the *manner* of eating, not the *worth of the person*. No one is worthy; the question is whether you are eating *worthily*. The pastoral implication is significant: an over-scrupulous reading that drives Christians away from the Table because they feel unworthy is reading the verse backwards.
- **The diagnosis came with a remedy, in the same passage.** Paul does not say "stop having the Supper" — he says "wait for one another," "if any man hunger let him eat at home," "tarry" (v. 33-34). The pastoral response to v. 30 is not avoidance of the Table; it is correction of the abuse that brought the harm.
- **The "body" question (v. 29) is genuinely undecided.** Movement C will work through whether "the body" is the eucharistic body, the church body, or both. Whichever of the three v. 30 views you hold, you will still need to decide what "the body" means in v. 29 — and your decision will affect what counts as "discerning" rightly.
- **All three views agree that the Lord's Supper is not safe.** It is not casual, not optional, not merely sentimental. Whether the consequence is divine intervention, broken community, or absent healing, the consequence is real. That is the part the modern church most needs to hear, and the part where all three traditions converge.
