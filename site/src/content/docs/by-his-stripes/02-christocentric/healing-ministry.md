Jesus healed people. This is one of the least disputed facts about his earthly ministry. The four Gospels record individual healing narratives, summary statements of mass healings, and Jesus's own explanations of why he healed. The sheer volume of healing material in the Gospels -- by some counts, nearly one-fifth of the Gospel narratives -- demands that any serious study of healing in the atonement reckon with what Jesus actually did.

But recording that Jesus healed is not the same as explaining what the healings mean for the atonement question. The same evidence has been read in fundamentally different ways by faithful interpreters. This section catalogs the biblical data and examines what it does and does not demonstrate.

---

## The Scope and Frequency of Jesus's Healings

The Gospels present Jesus's healing ministry not as occasional or incidental but as central and pervasive. Several summary passages describe the breadth of his healing work:

> **Matthew 4:23-24 (KJV)**
>
> <sup>23</sup> And Jesus went about all Galilee, teaching in their synagogues, and preaching the gospel of the kingdom, and healing all manner of sickness and all manner of disease among the people. <sup>24</sup> And his fame went throughout all Syria: and they brought unto him all sick people that were taken with divers diseases and torments, and those which were possessed with devils, and those which were lunatick, and those that had the palsy; and he healed them.

> **Matthew 9:35 (KJV)**
>
> And Jesus went about all the cities and villages, teaching in their synagogues, and preaching the gospel of the kingdom, and healing every sickness and every disease among the people.

> **Matthew 12:15 (KJV)**
>
> But when Jesus knew it, he withdrew himself from thence: and great multitudes followed him, and he healed them all.

> **Luke 4:40 (KJV)**
>
> Now when the sun was setting, all they that had any sick with divers diseases brought them unto him; and he laid his hands on every one of them, and healed them.

> **Mark 1:32-34 (KJV)**
>
> <sup>32</sup> And at even, when the sun did set, they brought unto him all that were diseased, and them that were possessed with devils. <sup>33</sup> And all the city was gathered together at the door. <sup>34</sup> And he healed many that were sick of divers diseases, and cast out many devils; and suffered not the devils to speak, because they knew him.

Several observations emerge from these summary passages:

**1. The healings were comprehensive in scope.** Matthew repeatedly uses the phrase "all manner of sickness and all manner of disease" (4:23) and "every sickness and every disease" (9:35). The Greek words here are πᾶσαν νόσον καὶ πᾶσαν μαλακίαν -- "every disease and every weakness." No category of physical illness was beyond Jesus's healing power.

**2. The healings were public and numerous.** These were not private, hidden acts. "Great multitudes followed him, and he healed them all" (Matt 12:15). "All the city was gathered together at the door" (Mark 1:33). The healing ministry was visible, verifiable, and known throughout the region.

**3. The healings were connected to the kingdom.** Both Matthew 4:23 and 9:35 place healing in a triad alongside teaching and preaching "the gospel of the kingdom." The healings were not separate from the kingdom announcement -- they were part of it.

**4. Mark's account introduces a subtle qualification.** Where Matthew says Jesus "healed them all" (12:15), Mark 1:34 says he "healed many that were sick." This is not necessarily a contradiction -- Mark may simply be narrating the same events with different emphasis -- but it has led some interpreters to note that even the Gospel writers do not always describe universal healing in every scene.

## Jesus's Stated Reasons for Healing

Why did Jesus heal? The Gospels record multiple motivations, and they are not always the same. This diversity matters: if Jesus healed for a single, consistent reason -- such as demonstrating what the atonement provides -- the healing-in-atonement case would be simpler. But the Gospels present a more complex picture.

### Compassion

> **Matthew 14:14 (KJV)**
>
> And Jesus went forth, and saw a great multitude, and was moved with compassion toward them, and he healed their sick.

> **Matthew 20:34 (KJV)**
>
> So Jesus had compassion on them, and touched their eyes: and immediately their eyes received sight, and they followed him.

> **Mark 1:41 (KJV)**
>
> And Jesus, moved with compassion, put forth his hand, and touched him, and saith unto him, I will; be thou clean.

The Greek word σπλαγχνίζομαι (splanchnizomai) -- "to be moved with compassion" -- appears repeatedly in connection with Jesus's healings. This is not a theological abstraction; it is a visceral, gut-level response to human suffering. Jesus saw sick people and was moved to act. Whatever theological significance the healings carry, they began in compassion.

### Faith

> **Matthew 9:22 (KJV)**
>
> But Jesus turned him about, and when he saw her, he said, Daughter, be of good comfort; thy faith hath made thee whole. And the woman was made whole from that hour.

> **Matthew 9:29 (KJV)**
>
> Then touched he their eyes, saying, According to your faith be it unto you.

> **Mark 10:52 (KJV)**
>
> And Jesus said unto him, Go thy way; thy faith hath made thee whole. And immediately he received his sight, and followed Jesus in the way.

> **Luke 7:50 (KJV)**
>
> And he said to the woman, Thy faith hath saved thee; go in peace.

> **Luke 17:19 (KJV)**
>
> And he said unto him, Arise, go thy way: thy faith hath made thee whole.

In these passages, Jesus explicitly connects healing to the faith of the person healed. The phrase "thy faith hath made thee whole" (ἡ πίστις σου σέσωκέν σε) appears in multiple Gospels and in both physical-healing and spiritual-salvation contexts -- a connection explored in detail below under the sozo discussion.

### The Glory of God

> **John 9:3 (KJV)**
>
> Jesus answered, Neither hath this man sinned, nor his parents: but that the works of God should be made manifest in him.

> **John 11:4 (KJV)**
>
> When Jesus heard that, he said, This sickness is not unto death, but for the glory of God, that the Son of God might be glorified thereby.

In these Johannine passages, Jesus identifies a purpose for sickness and healing that transcends the individual: the manifestation of God's works and the glorification of the Son. The sickness itself serves a divine purpose. The healing reveals God's power. Neither the sickness nor the healing is primarily about the sick person's faith or sin.

### Messianic Sign and Authentication

> **Matthew 11:2-6 (KJV)**
>
> <sup>2</sup> Now when John had heard in the prison the works of Christ, he sent two of his disciples, <sup>3</sup> And said unto him, Art thou he that should come, or do we look for another? <sup>4</sup> Jesus answered and said unto them, Go and shew John again those things which ye do hear and see: <sup>5</sup> The blind receive their sight, and the lame walk, the lepers are cleansed, and the deaf hear, the dead are raised up, and the poor have the gospel preached to them. <sup>6</sup> And blessed is he, whosoever shall not be offended in me.

> **Luke 7:22 (KJV)**
>
> Then Jesus answering said unto them, Go your way, and tell John what things ye have seen and heard; how that the blind see, the lame walk, the lepers are cleansed, the deaf hear, the dead are raised, to the poor the gospel is preached.

This passage is critical. When John the Baptist asks whether Jesus is the Messiah, Jesus does not answer with a theological argument. He points to the healings. The healings are evidence of who he is. They echo the messianic vision of Isaiah 35:5-6 ("Then the eyes of the blind shall be opened, and the ears of the deaf shall be unstopped," KJV) and Isaiah 61:1 ("The Spirit of the Lord GOD is upon me; because the LORD hath anointed me to preach good tidings unto the meek," KJV).

The question for the atonement debate is whether these healings are *only* authenticating signs -- proof of Jesus's identity -- or whether they also demonstrate the *scope* of his redemptive work. Both readings are defensible from this text alone.

### Kingdom Demonstration

> **Matthew 12:28 (KJV)**
>
> But if I cast out devils by the Spirit of God, then the kingdom of God is come unto you.

> **Luke 10:9 (KJV)**
>
> And heal the sick that are therein, and say unto them, The kingdom of God is come nigh unto you.

Jesus explicitly links healing and deliverance to the arrival of the kingdom of God. When demons are cast out, the kingdom has "come unto you." When the sick are healed, the kingdom has "come nigh." The healings are not merely displays of power; they are inaugurations of the kingdom.

This kingdom-framing is significant for the already/not yet discussion. If healing is a sign of the kingdom's arrival, and the kingdom is both present and future, then healing may belong to both the "already" and the "not yet" -- genuinely available now, but not universally guaranteed until the kingdom is consummated.

## The Sozo Connection: Saving and Healing in One Word

One of the most striking features of Jesus's healing vocabulary is his use of the Greek word σῴζω (sozo). This single word means both "to save" (in the spiritual, eternal sense) and "to heal" or "to make whole" (in the physical sense). Jesus uses it in both ways, and in some passages the two meanings appear to overlap.

### Sozo in Physical Healing Contexts

> **Matthew 9:21-22 (KJV)**
>
> <sup>21</sup> For she said within herself, If I may but touch his garment, I shall be whole [σωθήσομαι, sothēsomai -- "I shall be saved/made whole"]. <sup>22</sup> But Jesus turned him about, and when he saw her, he said, Daughter, be of good comfort; thy faith hath made thee whole [σέσωκέν σε, sesōken se -- "has saved/healed you"]. And the woman was made whole [ἐσώθη, esōthē -- "was saved/healed"] from that hour.

> **Mark 5:28, 34 (KJV)**
>
> <sup>28</sup> For she said, If I may touch but his clothes, I shall be whole [σωθήσομαι]. ... <sup>34</sup> And he said unto her, Daughter, thy faith hath made thee whole [σέσωκέν σε]; go in peace, and be whole of thy plague.

> **Luke 8:48 (KJV)**
>
> And he said unto her, Daughter, be of good comfort: thy faith hath made thee whole [σέσωκέν σε]; go in peace.

> **Luke 18:42 (KJV)**
>
> And Jesus said unto him, Receive thy sight: thy faith hath saved thee [σέσωκέν σε].

In each of these passages, the context is unambiguously physical: a woman with an issue of blood is healed, a blind man receives sight. Yet the word Jesus uses is sozo -- the same word used elsewhere for eternal salvation.

### Sozo in Spiritual Salvation Contexts

> **Luke 19:10 (KJV)**
>
> For the Son of man is come to seek and to save [σῶσαι, sōsai] that which was lost.

> **John 3:17 (KJV)**
>
> For God sent not his Son into the world to condemn the world; but that the world through him might be saved [σωθῇ, sōthē].

> **John 12:47 (KJV)**
>
> And if any man hear my words, and believe not, I judge him not: for I came not to judge the world, but to save [σώσω, sōsō] the world.

Here sozo refers to spiritual salvation -- rescue from sin and judgment. The context makes the spiritual meaning clear.

### Sozo in Ambiguous Contexts

> **Luke 7:50 (KJV)**
>
> And he said to the woman, Thy faith hath saved thee [σέσωκέν σε]; go in peace.

This passage is particularly interesting because it follows the account of the sinful woman who anoints Jesus's feet (Luke 7:36-49). There is no physical illness in the narrative. Yet Jesus uses the exact same phrase -- "thy faith hath saved thee" -- that he uses in the physical healing contexts. Is the "saving" here spiritual only? Or does sozo carry a comprehensive meaning -- rescue, restoration, wholeness -- that transcends the spiritual/physical boundary?

> **Luke 17:19 (KJV)**
>
> And he said unto him, Arise, go thy way: thy faith hath made thee whole [σέσωκέν σε].

This is said to the Samaritan leper who returns to give thanks. He has already been physically cleansed of leprosy (Luke 17:14). What does sozo add? Some interpreters argue that the Samaritan received not only physical healing (which all ten lepers received) but also spiritual salvation -- and that sozo captures both dimensions in a single word.

### What the Sozo Evidence Means

Two interpretive possibilities emerge:

**Possibility 1: Sozo reveals a unified redemptive reality.** The fact that Jesus uses the same word for saving and healing is theologically significant. It suggests that salvation and healing are not two separate categories but two dimensions of one comprehensive redemptive work. When Jesus "saves," he saves the whole person -- body and soul. The dual meaning of sozo is not an accident of language but a window into the nature of redemption itself. This reading supports the view that physical healing is part of the atonement.

**Possibility 2: Sozo is simply a word with a broad semantic range.** Greek words, like English words, can have multiple meanings depending on context. The English word "save" can mean "to rescue from danger," "to preserve," "to store up," or "to redeem spiritually." Nobody concludes that saving money and saving souls are two dimensions of the same reality. Similarly, sozo may simply be a versatile Greek word that means "heal" in medical contexts and "save" in spiritual contexts, with no necessary theological bridge between the two uses.

Both readings have merit. The sozo evidence is suggestive but not conclusive. It raises a question that the rest of the Christocentric analysis must help answer: did Jesus understand his saving work as comprehensive -- addressing body and soul -- or did he use common vocabulary in distinct ways for distinct situations?

## Cases Where Jesus Did Not Heal Everyone

Any honest examination of Jesus's healing ministry must account for the passages where Jesus did not heal everyone present, or where his healing power was limited by circumstances. These passages are essential for balance and for the pastoral dimensions of the debate.

### John 5:1-9 -- One Man at the Pool of Bethesda

> **John 5:1-9 (KJV)**
>
> <sup>1</sup> After this there was a feast of the Jews; and Jesus went up to Jerusalem. <sup>2</sup> Now there is at Jerusalem by the sheep market a pool, which is called in the Hebrew tongue Bethesda, having five porches. <sup>3</sup> In these lay a great multitude of impotent folk, of blind, halt, and withered, waiting for the moving of the water. <sup>4</sup> For an angel went down at a certain season into the pool, and troubled the water: whosoever then first after the troubling of the water stepped in was made whole of whatsoever disease he had. <sup>5</sup> And a certain man was there, which had an infirmity thirty and eight years. <sup>6</sup> When Jesus saw him lie, and knew that he had been now a long time in that case, he saith unto him, Wilt thou be made whole? <sup>7</sup> The impotent man answered him, Sir, I have no man, when the water is troubled, to put me into the pool: but while I am coming, another steppeth down before me. <sup>8</sup> Jesus saith unto him, Rise, take up thy bed, and walk. <sup>9</sup> And immediately the man was made whole, and took up his bed, and walked: and on the same day was the sabbath.

The detail that matters is in verse 3: a "great multitude of impotent folk, of blind, halt, and withered" lay at the pool. Jesus healed one man. The text does not record him healing the others. He walked past a multitude of sick people and healed a single individual.

This passage challenges any view that Jesus healed universally or that faith is always the prerequisite (this man does not express faith before being healed -- he does not even know who Jesus is until later, John 5:13). It also challenges the view that Jesus's compassion compelled him to heal every sick person he encountered. He chose one. The reasons for the choice are not explained.

### Mark 6:5-6 -- Unbelief in Nazareth

> **Mark 6:5-6 (KJV)**
>
> <sup>5</sup> And he could there do no mighty work, save that he laid his hands upon a few sick folk, and healed them. <sup>6</sup> And he marvelled because of their unbelief. And he went round about the villages, teaching.

This passage is startling. Mark says Jesus "could there do no mighty work" -- and connects this limitation to unbelief. The Greek is ἐδύνατο (edynato) -- "he was not able." This is not "he chose not to" but "he could not."

Interpreters handle this differently. Some argue that Jesus's power was genuinely constrained by the environment of unbelief -- that faith creates a context in which God's power operates. Others argue that "could not" means "could not appropriately" or "would not, given the circumstances" -- that God's sovereign choice, not lack of power, is in view. Still others note that even in Nazareth, Jesus did heal "a few sick folk," so his power was not entirely absent.

For the healing-in-atonement debate, this passage raises an important question: if unbelief can limit healing even in the presence of the Messiah himself, what does this mean for the claim that healing is a guaranteed provision of the atonement?

### Luke 4:25-27 -- God's Healing Is Selective

> **Luke 4:25-27 (KJV)**
>
> <sup>25</sup> But I tell you of a truth, many widows were in Israel in the days of Elias, when the heaven was shut up three years and six months, when great famine was throughout all the land; <sup>26</sup> But unto none of them was Elias sent, save unto Sarepta, a city of Sidon, unto a woman that was a widow. <sup>27</sup> And many lepers were in Israel in the time of Eliseus the prophet; and none of them was cleansed, saving Naaman the Syrian.

Jesus himself, in his inaugural sermon at Nazareth, makes the point that God's healing and provision are selective. Many widows in Israel went without provision. Many lepers went uncleansed. God chose to act for one Sidonian widow and one Syrian leper. Jesus is not embarrassed by this selectivity -- he states it as a fact about how God operates.

This is significant because it comes from Jesus's own teaching, not from a narrator's observation. Jesus acknowledges that God does not heal everyone, even when he has the power to do so. Divine healing is real but sovereign -- it operates according to God's purposes, not as an automatic right.

## The Relationship Between Healing and Faith

The passages above reveal that the relationship between faith and healing in Jesus's ministry is real but complex. It is not mechanical or formulaic. A brief survey of the evidence shows why:

### Sometimes Jesus healed in response to the sick person's faith

> **Matthew 9:22 (KJV)** -- "Thy faith hath made thee whole." The woman with the issue of blood actively sought healing and demonstrated faith.

### Sometimes Jesus healed in response to others' faith

> **Matthew 8:5-10, 13 (KJV)**
>
> <sup>5</sup> And when Jesus was entered into Capernaum, there came unto him a centurion, beseeching him, <sup>6</sup> And saying, Lord, my servant lieth at home sick of the palsy, grievously tormented. <sup>7</sup> And Jesus saith unto him, I will come and heal him. <sup>8</sup> The centurion answered and said, Lord, I am not worthy that thou shouldest come under my roof: but speak the word only, and my servant shall be healed. <sup>9</sup> For I am a man under authority, having soldiers under me: and I say to this man, Go, and he goeth; and to another, Come, and he cometh; and to my servant, Do this, and he doeth it. <sup>10</sup> When Jesus heard it, he marvelled, and said to them that followed, Verily I say unto you, I have not found so great faith, no, not in Israel. ... <sup>13</sup> And Jesus said unto the centurion, Go thy way; and as thou hast believed, so be it done unto thee. And his servant was healed in the selfsame hour.

The centurion's faith heals his servant. The servant does not express faith at all -- he is absent from the conversation. Faith is present, but it is vicarious.

> **Mark 2:3-5 (KJV)**
>
> <sup>3</sup> And they come unto him, bringing one sick of the palsy, which was borne of four. <sup>4</sup> And when they could not come nigh unto him for the press, they uncovered the roof where he was: and when they had broken it up, they let down the bed wherein the sick of the palsy lay. <sup>5</sup> When Jesus saw their faith, he said unto the sick of the palsy, Son, thy sins be forgiven thee.

"Their faith" -- the faith of the four friends -- is what Jesus responds to. Again, the sick person's own faith is not mentioned.

### Sometimes Jesus healed without any mention of faith

> **John 5:5-9** (quoted above) -- The man at Bethesda did not know who Jesus was (John 5:13). No faith is mentioned, asked for, or demonstrated.

> **Luke 22:50-51 (KJV)**
>
> <sup>50</sup> And one of them smote the servant of the high priest, and cut off his right ear. <sup>51</sup> And Jesus answered and said, Suffer ye thus far. And he touched his ear, and healed him.

Malchus, the high priest's servant, was healed by Jesus during the arrest in Gethsemane. He was an enemy, not a disciple. No faith is recorded.

### During Jesus's earthly ministry, faith for healing was always met with healing

This observation is easy to miss, but it is significant: **the Gospels do not record a single case of an individual coming to Jesus in faith for physical healing and being turned away.** Every person who sought healing from Jesus in faith received it:

- The woman with the issue of blood reached out in faith -- healed (Matt 9:20--22)
- Blind Bartimaeus cried out in faith -- healed (Mark 10:46--52)
- The centurion exercised faith on behalf of his servant -- healed (Matt 8:5--13)
- The four friends brought the paralytic in faith -- healed (Mark 2:3--5)
- The Syrophoenician woman persisted in faith despite an initial test -- healed (Matt 15:22--28)
- The two blind men who followed Jesus crying "Have mercy on us" -- healed (Matt 9:27--30)
- The father who said "Lord, I believe; help thou mine unbelief" -- his son was delivered (Mark 9:24)

The closest exception is the Syrophoenician woman, whom Jesus initially seemed to refuse. But even she was ultimately healed when she persisted -- and Jesus commended her faith: "O woman, great is thy faith: be it unto thee even as thou wilt" (Matt 15:28, KJV). The initial resistance served to test and display her faith, not to deny it.

Nazareth (Mark 6:5--6) is sometimes cited, but the problem there was the absence of faith, not its presence. Jesus "marvelled because of their unbelief." The passage does not describe individuals who had faith and were denied healing; it describes a community whose unbelief limited what Jesus did among them.

Those who see healing in the atonement consider this pattern significant: when someone came to Jesus with faith for physical healing, the answer was always yes. The "no" to healing emerges only in the post-ascension period -- most notably in Paul's experience with his thorn in the flesh (2 Corinthians 12:7--10), which is discussed below.

### Paul's thorn: faith present, healing not given?

> **2 Corinthians 12:7-10 (KJV)**
>
> <sup>7</sup> And lest I should be exalted above measure through the abundance of the revelations, there was given to me a thorn in the flesh, the messenger of Satan to buffet me, lest I should be exalted above measure. <sup>8</sup> For this thing I besought the Lord thrice, that it might depart from me. <sup>9</sup> And he said unto me, My grace is sufficient for thee: for my strength is made perfect in weakness. Most gladly therefore will I rather glory in my infirmities, that the power of Christ may rest upon me. <sup>10</sup> Therefore I take pleasure in infirmities, in reproaches, in necessities, in persecutions, in distresses for Christ's sake: for when I am weak, then am I strong.

Paul prayed three times for his thorn to be removed, and the Lord's answer was not removal but grace. This is the most frequently cited passage against the healing-in-atonement position, and it deserves careful examination.

**The nature of Paul's thorn is genuinely debated.** The text does not identify it. Interpreters have proposed at least four categories:

**1. Physical illness.** This is the most common modern reading. Suggested ailments include eye disease (based on Galatians 4:15 -- "ye would have plucked out your own eyes, and have given them to me" -- and Galatians 6:11 -- "see with how large letters I have written unto you with mine own hand"), chronic malaria, epilepsy, or a speech impediment (2 Corinthians 10:10 -- "his bodily presence is weak, and his speech contemptible"). If the thorn was physical illness, it is a strong case that God sometimes declines to heal even the most faithful believers.

**2. A human adversary or persistent persecution.** Several early church fathers took this view. Chrysostom (c. 347--407 AD) identified the thorn as the collective opposition Paul faced -- "all the adversaries of the word, Alexander the coppersmith, Hymenaeus and Philetus." The language supports this reading: "a messenger [ἄγγελος, *angelos*] of Satan to buffet [κολαφίζω, *kolaphizō*] me." The word *angelos* most commonly means a personal being -- an angel or a messenger -- not an impersonal ailment. And *kolaphizō* means "to strike with the fist" (it is the word used for the soldiers striking Jesus at his trial, Matthew 26:67). The image is of a person or force attacking Paul, not a disease afflicting him.

**3. Spiritual temptation.** Some interpreters, particularly in the patristic period, read the thorn as an inner spiritual struggle -- a persistent temptation that kept Paul humble. This reading emphasizes "lest I should be exalted above measure" as the thorn's purpose.

**4. The cumulative hardships of apostolic ministry.** Paul's own list in verse 10 is suggestive: "I take pleasure in infirmities, in reproaches, in necessities, in persecutions, in distresses for Christ's sake." The word "infirmities" (*astheneiais*) here appears alongside reproaches, persecutions, and distresses -- all of which are external hardships, not diseases. Some interpreters argue that "infirmities" in this context means weaknesses or hardships broadly, not sickness specifically.

**What this means for the debate:** If Paul's thorn was physical illness, it is the clearest case in the New Testament of a faithful believer praying for healing and being told no -- and the implications for the healing-in-atonement position are significant. But if the thorn was persecution, opposition, or some other form of affliction, then the passage does not address healing from sickness at all, and the healing-in-atonement position remains unchallenged on this point. The text does not settle the question. Honesty requires acknowledging the ambiguity.

### What the Pattern Reveals

The faith-healing relationship in the Gospels is real but not formulaic. Jesus sometimes healed in response to the sick person's faith, sometimes in response to the faith of others, and sometimes without any mention of faith at all. During his earthly ministry, faith for healing was always met with healing. In the post-ascension period, the picture becomes more complex -- Paul's thorn being the most significant case, though its nature is debated.

This evidence resists any simple formula: "if you have enough faith, you will always be healed in this life." It equally resists the opposite extreme: "faith has nothing to do with healing." The relationship is better described as *relational* rather than *transactional*. Jesus responds to people in their specific situations, according to the Father's will, with compassion and purpose. Faith creates an environment in which healing often occurs, but the sovereignty of God determines the outcome.

## What the Healing Ministry Reveals: Three Views

The evidence surveyed above -- the scope, the motivations, the sozo connection, the exceptions, and the faith-healing complexity -- has been interpreted through three primary frameworks. Each is presented with its strongest case.

### View 1: The Healings Demonstrate That Physical Healing Is Part of the Messiah's Redemptive Work

**The argument:** Jesus's healing ministry was not incidental to his mission -- it was central to it. He healed comprehensively, compassionately, and as a direct expression of the kingdom's arrival. Matthew 8:17 explicitly connects these healings to the fulfillment of Isaiah 53:4. The sozo vocabulary bridges saving and healing. The sheer volume and consistency of the healing ministry demonstrates that the Messiah's redemptive work addresses the whole person -- body and soul. Physical healing is not a bonus or a side effect; it is part of what the Servant came to accomplish.

**Strongest evidence:**
- The summary passages describe universal, comprehensive healing: "healed them all" (Matt 12:15), "every sickness and every disease" (Matt 9:35)
- Matthew 8:17 uses the fulfillment formula to connect healings to Isaiah 53
- Sozo bridges saving and healing in Jesus's own vocabulary
- Jesus identifies his healings as evidence of his messianic identity (Matt 11:2-6), and that identity is defined by Isaiah's Servant Songs
- The kingdom framework: healing is a sign of the kingdom, and the kingdom has come

**Challenge this view must address:** Why did Jesus not heal everyone at the Pool of Bethesda? Why was healing limited in Nazareth? Why does the New Testament record faithful believers who remained sick (2 Cor 12:7-10; 2 Tim 4:20)?

### View 2: The Healings Authenticate Jesus as Messiah but Do Not Define the Permanent Scope of Atonement Benefits

**The argument:** Jesus's healings were real, powerful, and significant -- but their primary purpose was to identify Jesus as the promised Messiah, not to establish physical healing as a permanent provision of the atonement. The healings are signs, not samples. They point to who Jesus is, not to what every believer should expect. Just as Jesus walked on water without establishing that believers can walk on water, he healed the sick without establishing that all believers can claim healing as a right.

**Strongest evidence:**
- Matthew 11:2-6: Jesus points to healings as evidence of who he is ("Art thou he that should come?"), not as defining atonement provisions
- The healing-as-kingdom-sign framework: signs announce the kingdom but do not represent its complete content
- The selectivity of Jesus's healings (John 5, Luke 4:25-27) argues against a universal healing provision
- The apostolic era itself shows a decline in healing gifts and the presence of sickness among believers, suggesting the sign function was primary
- The explicit atonement language in Jesus's teaching (Matt 20:28, Matt 26:28) centers on sin, not sickness

**Challenge this view must address:** Why does Matthew 8:17 use the fulfillment formula -- the same formula used for other messianic accomplishments -- if healing is merely an authenticating sign? Why does sozo bridge saving and healing if they are fundamentally separate categories?

### View 3: The Healings Are Genuine Foretastes of the Kingdom, Fully Realized at the Resurrection

**The argument:** The already/not yet framework best accounts for the full range of evidence. Jesus's healings are genuine, kingdom-inaugurating acts -- not merely signs but real manifestations of the age to come breaking into the present. They demonstrate what the atonement accomplishes comprehensively: salvation, healing, deliverance, restoration. But the full realization of these benefits belongs to the consummated kingdom. Believers today may experience genuine healings as the Spirit wills, but complete, permanent freedom from sickness is guaranteed only at the resurrection, when "there shall be no more death, neither sorrow, nor crying, neither shall there be any more pain" (Rev 21:4, KJV).

**Strongest evidence:**
- The kingdom-present/kingdom-future tension in Jesus's own teaching (Mark 1:15 -- "the kingdom of God is at hand" vs. Matt 6:10 -- "thy kingdom come")
- The healings fit naturally as inaugural kingdom acts: real but partial, genuine but not yet complete
- This view explains both the extensive healings AND the persistent sickness among believers -- both are real, because the kingdom is both here and not yet fully here
- Romans 8:22-23 teaches that even believers with the "firstfruits of the Spirit" groan and await the "redemption of our body" -- bodily wholeness is future
- The faith-healing complexity is explained: God heals when and as he wills, giving foretastes according to his sovereign purpose, not as automatic dispensations

**Challenge this view must address:** Does the already/not yet framework become a theological escape hatch -- a way to affirm healing without ever having to account for why it does not happen? If healing is "available" but never guaranteed, is the atonement provision meaningful in practice?

## Summary

Jesus's healing ministry is extensive, well-documented, and central to his identity as the Servant of Isaiah 53. He healed comprehensively, compassionately, and with explicit connection to the kingdom of God. His use of the word sozo bridges the concepts of saving and healing. His healings authenticated his messianic identity and demonstrated the kingdom's arrival.

At the same time, Jesus did not heal every sick person he encountered. His healing power was connected to, but not mechanically determined by, faith. He acknowledged that God's healing is selective, not universal. And his closest followers -- the apostles -- experienced both healing power and persistent sickness.

The healing ministry provides strong evidence that physical healing is connected to Jesus's redemptive mission. It does not, by itself, settle whether that connection means healing is a guaranteed atonement provision, an authenticating sign, or an already/not yet kingdom reality. The evidence requires interpretation, and faithful interpreters have reached different conclusions.

The next two sections examine evidence that deepens and complicates the picture: what Jesus taught about suffering, and what he said about the scope of his atoning work.
