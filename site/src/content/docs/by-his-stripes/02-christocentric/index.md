> "The blind receive their sight, and the lame walk, the lepers are cleansed, and the deaf hear, the dead are raised up, and the poor have the gospel preached to them."
> --- Matthew 11:5 (KJV)

## From Text to Person

Chapter 1 examined the words of Scripture -- the Hebrew of Isaiah 53, the Greek of Matthew 8 and 1 Peter 2, the semantic range of choliy, rapha, and sozo. That analysis produced a textual foundation: the words are genuinely capable of carrying both physical and spiritual meaning, and the New Testament authors applied them in both directions. But words do not exist in a vacuum. They were spoken by, written about, and ultimately embodied in a Person.

The SCAR framework places Christocentrism as the second pillar of theological analysis, after Scripture. The reason is straightforward: the entire Bible testifies to Christ (John 5:39 -- "Search the scriptures; for in them ye think ye have eternal life: and they are they which testify of me," KJV), and Isaiah 53 -- the passage at the center of this study -- is supremely about him. Jesus is not merely a subject to be discussed alongside the text. He is the Servant of Isaiah 53. He is the one whose stripes bring healing. He is both the subject of the prophecy and its definitive interpreter.

This means we cannot answer the question "Does Isaiah 53:5 include physical healing?" solely by parsing Hebrew and Greek. We must also ask: **What did the Servant himself reveal -- through his actions, his teaching, and his self-understanding -- about the nature and scope of his redemptive work?**

## The Key Question

Chapter 1 found that the exegetical evidence does not settle the debate. The Hebrew of Isaiah 53:4-5 is genuinely ambiguous. Matthew applies it to physical healing. Peter applies it to spiritual healing. The already/not yet framework claims to hold both in tension. Faithful interpreters reach different conclusions.

Chapter 2 approaches the same question from a different angle. Instead of asking "What do the words mean?" it asks:

**What did Jesus himself demonstrate and teach about the relationship between his redemptive mission and physical healing?**

This question has three dimensions, each explored in its own section:

### Jesus's Healing Ministry

Jesus healed. He healed extensively, publicly, and consistently throughout his earthly ministry. The Gospels record more space devoted to Jesus's healing ministry than to almost any other category of his activity. But what did the healings mean? Were they demonstrations of what the atonement provides? Authentication of his messianic identity? Foretastes of the coming kingdom? The evidence supports multiple readings, and the first section examines it in detail.

[Read the full analysis of Jesus's Healing Ministry &rarr;](/02-christocentric/healing-ministry/)

### Jesus's Teaching on Suffering

Jesus also taught about suffering -- and what he taught complicates any simple equation between faith and physical wholeness. He promised tribulation. He modeled submission to suffering in Gethsemane. He rejected the assumption that sickness always results from sin. He called his followers to take up their cross. Any Christocentric analysis of healing must reckon with this equally Christocentric evidence.

[Read the full analysis of Jesus's Teaching on Suffering &rarr;](/02-christocentric/teaching-on-suffering/)

### The Atonement's Scope Through Christ's Lens

Finally, what did Jesus say the atonement accomplishes? His explicit atonement language centers on sin -- ransom, remission, salvation. But his vocabulary also includes "abundant life" and the comprehensive Greek word sozo, which means both "to save" and "to make whole." The synthesis section examines what Jesus himself revealed about the breadth of his atoning work and whether it includes physical healing as a present provision, a future guarantee, or both.

## Connecting to Chapter 1

Chapter 1's Scripture analysis identified several open questions that the Christocentric lens can help address:

- **The choliy/rapha ambiguity:** The Hebrew words can mean physical sickness/healing or spiritual grief/restoration. Did Jesus's own use of healing language clarify this?
- **The Matthew 8 / 1 Peter 2 tension:** Matthew applies Isaiah 53:4 to physical healing; Peter applies Isaiah 53:5 to spiritual healing. Jesus's ministry and teaching may shed light on how both applications relate to one another.
- **The already/not yet framework:** If healing is genuinely "in the atonement" but not fully realized until the resurrection, what evidence from Jesus's own ministry supports this timing structure?
- **The sozo bridge:** The Greek word sozo appears in Jesus's own speech, used for both spiritual salvation and physical healing. Does his usage reveal a unified redemptive vision or simply a versatile vocabulary?

These questions cannot be answered by lexicons alone. They require attending to the Servant himself -- what he did, what he said, and what he revealed about the purpose of his suffering.

## A Note on Method

This chapter examines Jesus's words and actions as recorded in the four Gospels. It does not treat the Gospels as neutral biographies but as inspired, theological portraits of Christ. Each Gospel writer selected, arranged, and narrated events with theological purpose. Where the Gospel writers' editorial comments are relevant (such as Matthew's fulfillment formula), they are noted. But the primary focus is on what Jesus himself said and did -- his own words, his own actions, his own explanations of his ministry.

As in Chapter 1, every major interpretive position is presented with its strongest arguments. The goal is not to declare a winner but to lay out the Christocentric evidence with honesty and care, so the reader can weigh it alongside the exegetical evidence already examined.
