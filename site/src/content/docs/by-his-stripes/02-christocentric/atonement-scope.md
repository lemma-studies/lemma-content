The previous two sections examined what Jesus did (healing) and what Jesus taught (suffering). This section draws them together by asking the central synthesis question: **What did Jesus himself reveal about the scope of his atoning work, and does that scope include physical healing?**

To answer this, we examine Jesus's own statements about the purpose of his death, the distinction between what was accomplished at the cross and what is applied to believers over time, the already/not yet framework in his own teaching, and the sozo vocabulary that bridges saving and healing. The goal is not to resolve the debate but to present the Christocentric evidence as fully and fairly as possible.

---

## What Jesus Said the Atonement Accomplishes

Jesus spoke about the purpose of his death on multiple occasions. His explicit atonement language provides the foundation for understanding what his redemptive work was designed to achieve.

### Ransom for Many

> **Matthew 20:28 (KJV)**
>
> Even as the Son of man came not to be ministered unto, but to minister, and to give his life a ransom for many.

The word "ransom" (λύτρον, lutron) is a price paid to secure release -- the language of slave markets and prisoner exchanges. Jesus describes his death as a payment that secures the freedom of "many." The context is substitutionary: he gives his life *for* (ἀντί, anti -- "in place of") others. The explicit focus is on liberation from bondage, which in the broader New Testament context is the bondage of sin and death.

### Remission of Sins

> **Matthew 26:28 (KJV)**
>
> For this is my blood of the new testament, which is shed for many for the remission of sins.

Spoken at the Last Supper, this is one of Jesus's most direct statements about what his death accomplishes. "The remission of sins" (ἄφεσιν ἁμαρτιῶν, aphesin hamartiōn) is the explicit purpose of the blood-shedding. The language is covenantal -- "blood of the new testament" (or "new covenant") -- and the benefit named is forgiveness.

### Redemption from the Curse

> **Galatians 3:13--14 (KJV)**
>
> Christ hath redeemed us from the curse of the law, being made a curse for us: for it is written, Cursed is every one that hangeth on a tree: That the blessing of Abraham might come on the Gentiles through Jesus Christ; that we might receive the promise of the Spirit through faith.

Though Paul writes these words, the statement is Christocentric atonement language: Christ himself was "made a curse for us." The verb "redeemed" (ἐξηγόρασεν, *exēgorasen*) means to purchase out of slavery --- closely related to the "ransom" (*lutron*) language of Matthew 20:28. The method of redemption is Christ becoming the curse itself, absorbing the penalty so that the blessing might flow.

The significance for the healing question turns on the scope of "the curse of the law." Paul's immediate context in Galatians 3 concerns justification by faith versus works of the law --- the curse is the penalty for failing to keep the law perfectly (Gal 3:10, quoting Deut 27:26). But the law's curses, as codified in Deuteronomy 28, explicitly include diseases: "The LORD shall make the pestilence cleave unto thee" (Deut 28:21, KJV), "every sickness, and every plague, which is not written in the book of this law" (Deut 28:61, KJV). The Hebrew word for "sickness" in Deuteronomy 28:61 is *choliy* (חֳלִי) --- the same word used in Isaiah 53:4, where the KJV renders it "griefs" and the NASB (2020) renders it "sicknesses."

This lexical connection is one of the strongest arguments in the healing-in-atonement tradition: if Christ redeemed us from the curse, and the curse includes disease, then redemption from the curse includes redemption from disease. The counterargument holds that Paul's use of "the curse of the law" is narrower than the full scope of Deuteronomy 28 --- Paul is arguing about justification, not physical healing. Reading the disease-curses into Galatians 3:13 may expand his argument beyond its intent. Furthermore, Paul himself suffered a "thorn in the flesh" (2 Cor 12:7--10) and did not appeal to Galatians 3:13 as grounds for its removal. A third reading holds that the curse's scope is genuinely comprehensive --- including disease --- but that its full removal unfolds across the already/not yet timeline: secured at the cross, partially experienced now, and fully consummated at the resurrection (Rom 8:23).

The [Curse and Redemption](/by-his-stripes/01-scripture/curse-and-redemption/) section in Chapter 1 treats this argument in full, including the three interpretive options and the Genesis-to-Revelation curse arc.

### Seeking and Saving the Lost

> **Luke 19:10 (KJV)**
>
> For the Son of man is come to seek and to save that which was lost.

The word "save" here is σῶσαι (sōsai), from sozo. "That which was lost" (τὸ ἀπολωλός, to apolōlos) refers to those who are perishing or destroyed -- the image is of sheep who have wandered from the fold (echoing Isaiah 53:6 -- "All we like sheep have gone astray"). The saving is from lostness, estrangement, and spiritual ruin.

### Abundant Life

> **John 10:10 (KJV)**
>
> The thief cometh not, but for to steal, and to kill, and to destroy: I am come that they might have life, and that they might have it more abundantly.

This verse introduces a broader category. "Life" (ζωή, zōē) is one of John's richest theological terms. In John's Gospel, zōē is not merely existence but the divine life that comes from relationship with God (John 17:3 -- "And this is life eternal, that they might know thee the only true God, and Jesus Christ, whom thou hast sent," KJV). "Abundantly" (περισσόν, perisson) means "exceedingly, beyond measure, more than necessary."

The question is what "abundant life" encompasses. Is it spiritual life -- fellowship with God, freedom from sin's guilt and power? Or does it include physical well-being, material provision, and bodily wholeness? The healing-in-atonement view often appeals to John 10:10 as evidence that Jesus's redemptive purpose extends to the whole person, including the body. The spiritual-only view argues that zōē in John consistently refers to spiritual/eternal life, not physical health.

### Drawing All People

> **John 12:32 (KJV)**
>
> And I, if I be lifted up from the earth, will draw all men unto me.

"Lifted up" (ὑψωθῶ, hypsōthō) is a double reference in John: it means both the physical lifting up on the cross and the exaltation/glorification that follows. The purpose of the crucifixion is to "draw all men" -- a universal scope, encompassing people of every nation and condition. The drawing is relational -- toward Christ himself, not merely toward a set of benefits.

### What the Atonement Language Reveals

Surveying Jesus's explicit atonement statements, a pattern emerges:

| Statement | Key Term | Explicit Focus |
|---|---|---|
| Matthew 20:28 | Ransom (lutron) | Liberation from bondage |
| Matthew 26:28 | Remission (aphesis) | Forgiveness of sins |
| Galatians 3:13 | Redeemed (exagorazō) | Redemption from the curse |
| Luke 19:10 | Save (sozo) | Rescue of the lost |
| John 10:10 | Life (zōē) | Abundant, divine life |
| John 12:32 | Draw (helkuō) | Universal attraction to Christ |

The explicit atonement language centers on sin: ransom, remission, saving the lost. This is the primary, stated purpose of Jesus's death. But the language is not exclusively about sin in a narrow sense. Galatians 3:13 introduces curse-bearing --- and if the "curse of the law" encompasses the disease-curses of Deuteronomy 28, the atonement's reach extends to the body. "Abundant life" and "drawing all people" suggest a breadth that exceeds bare forgiveness. And the word sozo, as examined in the previous section, carries both saving and healing meaning.

The question is whether the breadth of these statements -- particularly "abundant life" and sozo -- extends to physical healing as a present provision. Those who affirm healing in the atonement argue that it does: the atonement addresses the whole person, and the "abundant life" Jesus provides includes bodily wholeness. Those who deny healing in the atonement argue that the atonement language is consistently about sin and spiritual rescue, and that "abundant life" refers to the quality of spiritual relationship with God, not to physical health.

The text supports both readings. The explicit words lean toward sin and spiritual rescue. The broader categories -- life, wholeness, sozo -- leave room for a more comprehensive understanding.

## The Distinction Between Accomplished and Applied

One of the most important theological distinctions for the healing question is the difference between what Christ accomplished at the cross and what is applied to believers in their present experience.

### "It Is Finished"

> **John 19:30 (KJV)**
>
> When Jesus therefore had received the vinegar, he said, It is finished: and he bowed his head, and gave up the ghost.

The Greek τετέλεσται (tetelestai) -- "it is finished" or "it has been completed" -- is in the perfect tense, indicating a completed action with ongoing results. Whatever the cross accomplished, it was accomplished fully and finally in that moment. The work of atonement is not partial or ongoing; it is complete.

### The Application Unfolds Over Time

Yet the New Testament teaches that the benefits of the completed atonement are applied to believers progressively:

- **Justification** is applied at the moment of faith -- past tense. "Being justified freely by his grace through the redemption that is in Christ Jesus" (Romans 3:24, KJV).
- **Sanctification** is applied throughout the believer's life -- present tense. "But we all, with open face beholding as in a glass the glory of the Lord, are changed into the same image from glory to glory" (2 Corinthians 3:18, KJV).
- **Glorification** is applied at the resurrection -- future tense. "It is sown in corruption; it is raised in incorruption" (1 Corinthians 15:42, KJV).

This three-stage application is widely accepted across theological traditions. The question is where physical healing fits in this framework:

**Option A: Healing belongs primarily to justification (past).** Healing was accomplished at the cross and is available now as a present right, in the same way that forgiveness is available now. Believers should claim healing by faith, just as they claim forgiveness by faith. This is the classic healing-in-atonement position.

**Option B: Healing belongs primarily to glorification (future).** Healing was accomplished at the cross but will not be fully applied until the resurrection, when "there shall be no more death, neither sorrow, nor crying, neither shall there be any more pain" (Revelation 21:4, KJV). In this age, believers still experience sickness because the body has not yet been redeemed. This is the position of those who see physical healing as an eschatological promise rather than a present guarantee.

**Option C: Healing spans the entire application timeline.** Healing was accomplished at the cross, is genuinely available in the present age (as God wills), and will be fully realized at the resurrection. Believers may experience real healings now -- these are foretastes of the coming glory -- but complete, permanent freedom from sickness awaits the new creation. This is the already/not yet position.

Each option has to account for different biblical evidence:

- **Option A** must explain why Paul, Timothy, Trophimus, and Epaphroditus were sick despite being full of faith (2 Cor 12:7-10; 1 Tim 5:23; 2 Tim 4:20; Phil 2:25-27).
- **Option B** must explain why Jesus and the apostles healed people physically in this age, and why Matthew connects those healings to Isaiah 53 (Matt 8:17).
- **Option C** must explain how "available but not guaranteed" differs in practice from "not available at all" -- and how believers should pray when the outcome is uncertain.

## The Already/Not Yet in Jesus's Own Teaching

The already/not yet framework has appeared repeatedly in this chapter because it emerges directly from Jesus's own teaching about the kingdom. This section examines the specific evidence for this framework in Jesus's words.

### Kingdom Present: Healings, Deliverances, Forgiveness

> **Luke 11:20 (KJV)**
>
> But if I with the finger of God cast out devils, no doubt the kingdom of God is come upon you.

> **Matthew 12:28 (KJV)**
>
> But if I cast out devils by the Spirit of God, then the kingdom of God is come unto you.

When Jesus casts out demons and heals the sick, the kingdom is present. These are not metaphors for the kingdom; they are the kingdom in action. The present tense -- "is come upon you" -- indicates that the kingdom is operative here and now, not merely anticipated.

### Kingdom Future: The Coming of the Son of Man, the Resurrection, the New Creation

> **Matthew 24:30 (KJV)**
>
> And then shall appear the sign of the Son of man in heaven: and then shall all the tribes of the earth mourn, and they shall see the Son of man coming in the clouds of heaven with power and great glory.

> **Matthew 25:31-32 (KJV)**
>
> <sup>31</sup> When the Son of man shall come in his glory, and all the holy angels with him, then shall he sit upon the throne of his glory: <sup>32</sup> And before him shall be gathered all nations: and he shall separate them one from another, as a shepherd divideth his sheep from the goats.

> **John 5:28-29 (KJV)**
>
> <sup>28</sup> Marvel not at this: for the hour is coming, in the which all that are in the graves shall hear his voice, <sup>29</sup> And shall come forth; they that have done good, unto the resurrection of life; and they that have done evil, unto the resurrection of damnation.

These passages describe a future event: the second coming, the final judgment, the resurrection of the dead. The kingdom will reach its consummation at a point still future. What has been inaugurated has not yet been completed.

### Three Possibilities for Physical Healing Within This Framework

**Possibility 1: Healing is fully present now (healing-in-atonement view).** The kingdom's arrival means that all its benefits -- including physical healing -- are available in the present. Just as forgiveness is a present reality, healing is a present reality. Believers who do not experience healing lack faith or face spiritual obstacles, but the provision is there.

**Strongest argument for this reading:** The kingdom "is come upon you" language is present tense. When Jesus heals, he does not say "this is a preview of something you will receive later." He heals now, fully, immediately. The woman with the issue of blood is healed "from that hour" (Matt 9:22). The kingdom's power is present power.

**Weakness:** This reading must account for the many believers in the New Testament who were not healed, and for Jesus's own teaching that tribulation is promised (John 16:33). If all kingdom benefits are fully present, why does Jesus tell his followers to pray "thy kingdom come" (Matt 6:10)?

**Possibility 2: Healing is entirely future (spiritual-only view).** Physical healing belongs exclusively to the "not yet" -- the resurrection and the new creation. The healings in Jesus's ministry were authenticating signs of his messianic identity, not demonstrations of present atonement benefits. In this age, believers receive spiritual benefits (forgiveness, justification, the indwelling Spirit) but not physical benefits (healing, bodily resurrection).

**Strongest argument for this reading:** Jesus's explicit atonement language (Matt 20:28, Matt 26:28) centers on sin. The already/not yet framework clearly places bodily redemption in the future (Rom 8:23). The healings were signs and wonders that belong to the unique apostolic era, not to the normative Christian experience.

**Weakness:** This reading must account for Matthew 8:17, which connects physical healing to Isaiah 53 using the fulfillment formula -- the same formula Matthew uses for other genuine messianic accomplishments. It must also account for James 5:14-16, which instructs the church to pray for the healing of the sick with the expectation that "the Lord shall raise him up."

**Possibility 3: Healing is genuinely available now but fully guaranteed only at the resurrection (already/not yet view).** Physical healing is a real atonement benefit that believers may experience in this age, as God wills. But the full eradication of sickness belongs to the consummated kingdom. In the meantime, God heals some, sustains others through suffering, and brings all believers ultimately to the resurrection, where "there shall be no more death, neither sorrow, nor crying, neither shall there be any more pain" (Rev 21:4, KJV).

**Strongest argument for this reading:** It accounts for both the extensive healing ministry of Jesus and the apostles (the "already") and the persistent sickness among faithful believers (the "not yet"). It takes Matthew 8:17 seriously without requiring a guarantee of healing in every case. It honors the suffering texts without dismissing the healing texts.

**Weakness:** As noted previously, this framework can become unfalsifiable. If healing is "available but not guaranteed," any outcome -- healing or non-healing -- confirms the framework. The practical difference between "available but not guaranteed" and "not available as a provision at all" may be difficult to discern.

## Sozo: The Word That Bridges Saving and Healing

The sozo evidence was examined in detail in the [healing ministry section](/by-his-stripes/02-christocentric/healing-ministry/). Here it is placed in the context of the atonement question specifically.

### Jesus's Uses of Sozo -- A Summary Table

| Passage | Context | Sozo Meaning | Physical or Spiritual? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Matt 9:21-22 | Woman with issue of blood | "Made thee whole" | Physical healing |
| Mark 5:28-34 | Woman with issue of blood (parallel) | "Made thee whole" | Physical healing |
| Luke 8:48 | Woman with issue of blood (parallel) | "Made thee whole" | Physical healing |
| Luke 7:50 | Sinful woman anoints Jesus's feet | "Hath saved thee" | Spiritual (no illness present) |
| Luke 17:19 | Samaritan leper returns | "Made thee whole" | Physical healing + spiritual? |
| Luke 18:42 | Blind Bartimaeus | "Hath saved thee" | Physical healing |
| Luke 19:10 | Zacchaeus encounter | "To seek and to save" | Spiritual salvation |
| John 3:17 | God sent the Son | "That the world might be saved" | Spiritual salvation |
| John 12:47 | Jesus's purpose statement | "To save the world" | Spiritual salvation |

### The Interpretive Question

The table reveals that Jesus uses sozo in clearly physical contexts (healing the sick), clearly spiritual contexts (saving the lost), and at least two contexts that may be both (Luke 7:50, Luke 17:19). The question is whether this dual usage reveals something about the nature of salvation itself.

**Reading 1: Sozo reveals a unified redemptive vision.** When Jesus "saves" (sozo), he saves comprehensively -- body and soul. The fact that the same word covers both physical healing and spiritual salvation is not a linguistic accident but a theological statement. Salvation in the biblical sense is whole-person restoration. The atonement addresses sin (the root) and sickness (the fruit). Sozo holds them together because they belong together.

This reading is supported by the observation that in Luke 17:19, Jesus says sozo to the Samaritan leper who has already been physically cleansed. If sozo meant only physical healing, it would be redundant -- the man is already healed. If sozo meant only spiritual salvation, why does Jesus use it only for the one who returned, not for the nine who were also cleansed? The most natural reading is that the returning leper received something more than physical cleansing -- a wholeness that encompasses body, soul, and relationship with God.

**Reading 2: Sozo is a versatile word, not a theological bridge.** Greek lexicons (including BDAG) list multiple distinct meanings for sozo: to save from danger, to preserve, to rescue, to heal, to deliver. The fact that one word has multiple meanings does not mean those meanings are theologically unified. English "save" can mean "rescue from fire," "deposit in a bank," or "redeem from sin" -- and nobody concludes that banking and redemption are aspects of the same reality.

This reading is supported by the observation that context determines meaning. When Jesus tells the woman with the issue of blood "thy faith hath made thee whole," the context is physical healing. When Jesus says "the Son of man is come to seek and to save that which was lost," the context is spiritual rescue. The word flexes to fit its context, as words naturally do. Reading theological unity into the word's range may overinterpret the data.

**Assessment:** Both readings are linguistically possible. The sozo evidence is genuinely ambiguous -- it can be read as evidence for a unified redemptive vision or as evidence of normal semantic flexibility. Neither reading can be dismissed, and neither can be proven from the word alone. The sozo question must be answered in light of the broader Christocentric evidence -- the healing ministry, the suffering teaching, and the explicit atonement language -- rather than in isolation.

## The Christocentric Verdict -- What Can We Conclude?

### What Is Clear from Jesus's Ministry and Teaching

The following points are established by the Christocentric evidence examined in this chapter and are not seriously disputed across the interpretive spectrum:

**1. Jesus healed extensively, frequently, and compassionately.** The healing ministry is not a marginal feature of the Gospels; it is central to Jesus's public activity. He healed "all manner of sickness and all manner of disease" (Matt 4:23, KJV), and the Gospels record summary statements of mass healings alongside detailed individual narratives.

**2. Jesus connected healing to faith, to the kingdom, and to God's glory.** His healings were not random acts of power. They were motivated by compassion (Matt 14:14), connected to faith (Matt 9:22), identified as kingdom-inaugurating acts (Matt 12:28), and explained as displays of God's glory (John 9:3, John 11:4).

**3. Jesus also promised tribulation and modeled submission to suffering.** He told his followers they would have tribulation (John 16:33), called them to take up their cross (Matt 16:24), and in Gethsemane submitted to suffering he had asked to avoid (Matt 26:39). Suffering is not incompatible with faithfulness; it is, in some cases, the shape faithfulness takes.

**4. Jesus used sozo for both saving and healing.** The same word appears in physical-healing contexts (Matt 9:22, Mark 5:34, Luke 8:48, Luke 18:42) and spiritual-salvation contexts (Luke 19:10, John 3:17, John 12:47). Whether this reveals a unified redemptive vision or simply a versatile vocabulary is debated.

**5. Jesus's explicit atonement language centers on sin, but his ministry addresses the whole person.** His stated purposes for dying -- ransom (Matt 20:28), remission of sins (Matt 26:28), seeking and saving the lost (Luke 19:10) -- focus on sin and its consequences. But his "abundant life" promise (John 10:10) and his comprehensive healing ministry suggest a redemptive vision broader than forgiveness alone.

### What Remains Debated

**1. Whether Jesus's pre-cross healings are evidence of what the atonement provides or evidence of who the Messiah is (or both).** The healing-in-atonement view sees the healings as demonstrations of atonement benefits. The sign/authentication view sees them as messianic credentials. The already/not yet view sees them as inaugural kingdom acts that anticipate the full redemption. All three positions find support in the evidence.

**2. Whether the already/not yet framework adequately explains why some faithful believers are healed and others are not.** The framework is widely accepted in principle, but its practical implications are contested. If healing is "available now" but "guaranteed only at the resurrection," how should believers pray? How should they interpret non-healing? The framework provides theological coherence but may not provide pastoral clarity.

**3. Whether sozo's dual meaning is theologically significant or merely linguistic.** If sozo reveals that salvation and healing are two aspects of one redemptive work, then physical healing is woven into the fabric of the atonement. If sozo is simply a broad word with context-dependent meanings, no such conclusion follows. The lexical evidence alone does not resolve this question.

### The Tension Remains

The Christocentric evidence, like the Scripture evidence examined in Chapter 1, does not produce a simple answer to the question "Is physical healing in the atonement?" It deepens the question.

Jesus healed -- extensively, compassionately, and as a sign of the kingdom. He also suffered -- voluntarily, obediently, and as the model for discipleship. He spoke of the atonement in terms of sin and salvation. He also promised abundant life and used vocabulary that bridges saving and healing.

The evidence resists the extremes. It resists the claim that physical healing has no connection to Jesus's redemptive work -- the healing ministry is too extensive, too central, and too explicitly connected to Isaiah 53 for that. It equally resists the claim that physical healing is a guaranteed right for every believer in this age -- the suffering teaching is too clear, the exceptions too real, and the already/not yet tension too thoroughly embedded in Jesus's own words.

What remains is a question that the Christocentric evidence sharpens but does not resolve: **How should believers live in the tension between the reality of healing and the reality of suffering, both of which are taught and modeled by Christ?**

The answer to that question requires not only Scripture (Chapter 1) and Christocentrism (Chapter 2) but also the witness of the early church (Chapter 3: Antiquity) and the experience of believers throughout history (Chapter 4: Reality). The SCAR framework holds all four lenses together, and only when all four are examined can the reader weigh the evidence fully.
