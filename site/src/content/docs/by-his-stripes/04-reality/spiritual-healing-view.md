> "Who his own self bare our sins in his own body on the tree, that we, being dead to sins, should live unto righteousness: by whose stripes ye were healed."
> --- 1 Peter 2:24 (KJV)

This section presents the position that Isaiah 53:5 -- "with his stripes we are healed" -- refers to spiritual healing: deliverance from sin, reconciliation with God, and the restoration of the soul. Physical healing, while acknowledged as something God can do, is not considered a provision of the atonement. This view is presented as its best advocates would present it: with its strongest arguments, its most compelling evidence, and an honest acknowledgment of its challenges.

---

## Historical Roots

### B.B. Warfield (1851--1921)

Benjamin Breckinridge Warfield was a theologian at Princeton Theological Seminary and one of the most formidable intellects in American church history. His 1918 work *Counterfeit Miracles* remains the most influential systematic argument for cessationism -- the view that the miraculous gifts of the Holy Spirit (including healing) ceased with the apostolic age.

Warfield's argument was built on a careful reading of the New Testament's purpose for miracles. He contended that miraculous signs -- healing, tongues, prophecy -- served a specific, time-bound function: they authenticated the apostles as God's messengers and confirmed the divine origin of the Scriptures they were writing. Once the canon of Scripture was complete and the apostolic office had passed, the purpose of miraculous gifts was fulfilled. The gifts were not withdrawn arbitrarily; their work was done.

Applied to the healing question, Warfield argued that the atonement addresses sin -- the fundamental human problem. Physical healing during the apostolic era was a sign gift that pointed to Christ's authority, not a permanent provision of the cross. The distinction mattered deeply to Warfield: God is able to heal, but healing is an act of sovereign grace, not an entitlement purchased at Calvary.

Warfield was not a cold rationalist dismissing the suffering of the sick. He was a man who cared for his invalid wife, Minnie, for decades. His theology of divine sovereignty in suffering was lived, not merely theorized.

### Cessationism as a Theological Framework

The cessationist position did not originate with Warfield, though he gave it its most rigorous modern expression. The framework holds that:

1. **Miracles had a revelatory purpose.** They authenticated God's messengers and confirmed the word they spoke (Mark 16:20; Heb 2:3--4).
2. **The apostolic office was unique and unrepeatable.** The apostles had a foundational role (Eph 2:20) that was, by nature, temporary.
3. **The closing of the canon marked a transition.** With the completion of Scripture, the church possesses the full revelation of God. The sign gifts that accompanied the giving of that revelation are no longer necessary.
4. **God's sovereignty governs all things, including sickness.** Suffering is not random or meaningless -- it is providentially ordered for the believer's sanctification, God's glory, and purposes that may not be visible this side of eternity.

### Reformed Theology and Suffering

The Reformed tradition, more broadly, has developed a robust theology of suffering that undergirds the spiritual-healing-only position. Drawing on passages like Romans 5:3--5 ("tribulation worketh patience"), Romans 8:28 ("all things work together for good"), and 2 Corinthians 4:16--17 ("our light affliction, which is but for a moment, worketh for us a far more exceeding and eternal weight of glory"), Reformed theology teaches that suffering is not opposed to God's purposes -- it is often the very means by which God accomplishes them.

This is not fatalism. It is the conviction that God is present in suffering, that He uses suffering to shape His people into the image of Christ, and that the ultimate resolution of all suffering is not in this life but in the life to come.

## Modern Representatives

### John MacArthur

John MacArthur is the most prominent modern advocate of the cessationist position. His books *Charismatic Chaos* (1992) and *Strange Fire* (2013) present a comprehensive critique of the charismatic movement and its theology of healing.

MacArthur teaches that:

- The sign gifts (healing, tongues, prophecy) ceased with the apostles
- The healing-in-the-atonement view is not supported by a careful reading of Scripture
- The charismatic movement, at its worst, dishonors God by attributing to the Holy Spirit things that are not from the Holy Spirit
- The proper response to illness is prayer, trust in God's sovereignty, and gratitude for medical care as a gift of common grace

MacArthur's position is unambiguous and forcefully stated. His influence extends through The Master's Seminary, Grace Community Church, and his media ministry (Grace to You). It is worth noting that the Legacy Standard Bible (LSB), produced under the auspices of The Master's Seminary, reflects this theological tradition -- its translation choices in key passages are informed by cessationist convictions. This is why the LSB appears in the cross-version comparisons throughout this study: it represents a serious, self-consistent theological position that deserves a seat at the table.

### The Reformed and Presbyterian Tradition

The broader Reformed and Presbyterian tradition has generally held that while God is certainly able to heal, miraculous healing is not a normative experience for the post-apostolic church. Theologians in this tradition -- including R.C. Sproul, Michael Horton, and many others -- affirm God's sovereignty over sickness and healing while denying that healing is a provision of the atonement in the way that forgiveness of sins is.

This does not mean Reformed churches never pray for the sick. They do. But the prayer is offered in submission to God's will, not as a claim on a promise. The expectation is that God will answer according to His purposes, which may include healing or may include a deeper work through suffering.

### Dispensational and Plymouth Brethren Traditions

The dispensational tradition -- rooted in the work of J.N. Darby (1800--1882) and the Plymouth Brethren movement -- provides one of the most precise formulations of the cessationist argument. Where Warfield argued from the *purpose* of miracles, the dispensational tradition argues from both purpose and *dispensational transition*: the miraculous gifts belong to a specific era in God's redemptive program, and that era has closed.

The central argument rests on two key passages:

> "And they went forth, and preached every where, the Lord working with them, and confirming the word with signs following. Amen."
> --- Mark 16:20 (KJV)

> "How shall we escape, if we neglect so great salvation; which at the first began to be spoken by the Lord, and was confirmed unto us by them that heard him; God also bearing them witness, both with signs and wonders, and with divers miracles, and gifts of the Holy Ghost, according to his own will?"
> --- Hebrews 2:3--4 (KJV)

The argument is straightforward and powerful:

1. **Signs had a specific purpose: confirming the word.** Mark 16:20 is explicit -- the Lord worked with the apostles, "confirming the word with signs following." The signs were not ends in themselves. They served as God's authentication of the message being spoken.

2. **The word has been confirmed.** Hebrews 2:3--4 uses the past tense: the salvation "was confirmed unto us by them that heard him." The verb βεβαιόω (*bebaioō*, "to confirm, establish, make firm") indicates a completed action. God bore witness to the apostolic message through signs, wonders, miracles, and gifts of the Holy Ghost. That confirmation was accomplished; it does not need to be repeated.

3. **The canon is the confirmed word.** Once the apostolic writings were completed and recognized as Scripture, the church possesses the full, confirmed word of God. The signs that accompanied the *giving* of that word fulfilled their purpose. Just as scaffolding is removed when the building is complete, the confirmatory signs were no longer necessary once the word they confirmed was established.

4. **Subsequent generations receive the confirmed word, not the confirming signs.** Hebrews 2:3 describes a chain: the message was spoken by the Lord, confirmed by those who heard Him (the apostles), and received by the letter's audience. The signs belonged to the middle link -- the apostolic generation that was doing the confirming. Later generations receive the benefit of that confirmation (the Scriptures) without needing the confirmation to be repeated.

This is a self-consistent and carefully reasoned position. It does not deny that God performed miracles through the apostles. It does not deny that the miracles were real, powerful, and purposeful. It simply argues that the purpose has been fulfilled. The word has been confirmed. The signs have done their work.

Some dispensational theologians take the further step of arguing that the healing miracles of Jesus and the apostles belong to the transition between the dispensation of the Law and the dispensation of the Church -- that healing was a sign of the coming kingdom offered to Israel, and when Israel rejected the kingdom offer, the sign gifts were withdrawn. This is a more specific dispensational argument, and not all who hold the "confirming the word" position would frame it this way. But both strands share the conviction that the miraculous gifts were time-bound, not permanent provisions for the church age.

**An important note on Mark 16:20:** This verse falls within the longer ending of Mark (16:9--20), whose textual authenticity is debated among scholars. Many critical editions of the Greek New Testament bracket or footnote these verses, noting that some of the earliest manuscripts (Sinaiticus and Vaticanus) do not include them. Dispensational and Brethren scholars who build on Mark 16:20 are aware of this textual question and typically argue either that the longer ending is authentic or that even if its textual status is uncertain, the theology it expresses is confirmed by Hebrews 2:3--4, which is textually undisputed. The Hebrews passage alone carries the full weight of the "confirming the word" argument.

## The Theological Argument at Its Best

### 1 Peter 2:24 -- The Authoritative Interpretation

> "Who his own self bare our sins in his own body on the tree, that we, being dead to sins, should live unto righteousness: by whose stripes ye were healed."
> --- 1 Peter 2:24 (KJV)

Peter quotes Isaiah 53:5 -- "by whose stripes ye were healed" -- and applies it in a context that is entirely about sin and righteousness. The surrounding verses (1 Pet 2:21--25) discuss Christ's sinless suffering, His bearing of sins on the cross, and believers dying to sin and living to righteousness. There is no mention of physical healing in the passage. Peter concludes with a shepherding metaphor: "For ye were as sheep going astray; but are now returned unto the Shepherd and Bishop of your souls" (1 Pet 2:25).

For the spiritual-healing-only advocate, Peter settles the question. He is an apostle. He walked with Jesus. He saw the healings. And when he quotes "by his stripes we are healed," he applies it to the healing of the soul from sin. If Peter -- under the inspiration of the Holy Spirit -- understands Isaiah 53:5 as referring to spiritual restoration, that interpretation carries apostolic authority.

### The Context of Isaiah 53:5

The immediate context of Isaiah 53:5 is saturated with sin-language:

- "He was wounded for our **transgressions**"
- "He was bruised for our **iniquities**"
- "The chastisement of our **peace** was upon him"
- "With his stripes we are **healed**"

The advocate argues that the parallelism is clear: transgressions, iniquities, peace, healing -- all four terms address the same reality, which is the spiritual condition of the people. "Healed" is a metaphor for spiritual restoration, consistent with prophetic usage elsewhere in Isaiah. In Isaiah 6:10, God says of Israel, "lest they see with their eyes, and hear with their ears, and understand with their heart, and convert, and be healed" (KJV). There, "healed" clearly means spiritual restoration, not physical healing. The same prophetic idiom is at work in Isaiah 53:5.

### The Distinction Between Isaiah 53:4 and 53:5

The spiritual-healing-only advocate makes a careful distinction between verse 4 and verse 5 of Isaiah 53. Matthew 8:17 quotes verse 4 -- "Himself took our infirmities, and bare our sicknesses" -- in the context of physical healing. But Matthew does not quote verse 5 -- "with his stripes we are healed." The two verses, while adjacent, may address different dimensions of the Servant's work: verse 4 describes the Servant's identification with human suffering (including physical sickness), while verse 5 describes the Servant's atoning work for sin.

This distinction is not artificial. Matthew had the opportunity to quote verse 5 and chose not to. Peter quoted verse 5 and applied it to sin. The New Testament authors, under inspiration, may be drawing out different dimensions of the same passage -- and the dimension they connect to the atonement is the spiritual one.

### The Septuagint's Witness

The Septuagint (LXX) -- the Greek translation of the Old Testament used extensively in the first century -- renders the Hebrew חֳלִי (*choliy*, "sicknesses/griefs") in Isaiah 53:4 as ἁμαρτίας (*hamartias*, "sins"). This means that pre-Christian Jewish translators understood the passage in spiritual rather than physical terms. The LXX reading does not settle the debate definitively -- it is a translation, not the original -- but it does demonstrate that the spiritual reading of Isaiah 53:4 has deep roots, predating the New Testament by centuries.

### The Narrow Reading of "The Curse of the Law"

Healing-in-the-atonement advocates appeal to Galatians 3:13 --- "Christ hath redeemed us from the curse of the law" --- and argue that since the law's curses include disease (Deuteronomy 28), Christ's redemption includes healing from disease. The spiritual-healing-only advocate responds with a precise reading of Paul's context: in Galatians 3, "the curse of the law" is the curse that falls on those who seek righteousness through law-keeping (Gal 3:10, quoting Deut 27:26). Paul is arguing about justification, not about physical health. Reading the disease-curses of Deuteronomy 28 into Galatians 3:13 expands the argument beyond what Paul himself develops. The atonement addresses the fundamental curse --- separation from God through sin --- and in doing so, it secures reconciliation, not a catalogue of physical provisions. The [Curse and Redemption](/01-scripture/curse-and-redemption/) section in Chapter 1 provides the full exegetical treatment.

### God's Sovereignty and the Gift of Grace

If healing were a provision of the atonement in the same way that forgiveness is, then -- logically -- every believer with sufficient faith should be healed. But this is not what the New Testament describes. Paul was not healed (2 Cor 12:7--10). Trophimus was not healed (2 Tim 4:20). Epaphroditus was healed, but Paul attributes it to God's mercy, not to atonement provision (Phil 2:27).

The spiritual-healing-only view explains these passages straightforwardly: God heals when He chooses to heal, according to His sovereign purposes. Healing is a gift of grace, not a covenant right. This means that godly, faithful believers can be sick -- and remain sick -- without any imputation of spiritual failure. God's "no" to healing is not a failure of the system; it is the exercise of sovereign wisdom.

## Real-World Strengths -- What This View Produces at Its Best

### Freedom from False Guilt

Perhaps the most significant pastoral strength of this view is that it protects sick believers from the crushing burden of false guilt. If healing is not a provision of the atonement, then the believer who is not healed has not failed. Their sickness is not evidence of insufficient faith, unconfessed sin, or spiritual inadequacy. They are free to be sick without the added torment of thinking they are responsible for their condition.

This is not a small thing. The spiritual damage caused by telling sick people that their illness is their fault is well documented. The spiritual-healing-only view eliminates this damage at its root by rejecting the premise that healing is something the believer is expected to claim.

### A Robust Theology of Suffering

> "And not only so, but we glory in tribulations also: knowing that tribulation worketh patience; And patience, experience; and experience, hope."
> --- Romans 5:3--4 (KJV)

The cessationist tradition has produced some of the richest theology of suffering in Christian history. From the Puritans to the Reformed tradition to figures like Joni Eareckson Tada -- who has lived with quadriplegia since a diving accident in 1967 and has become one of the most influential voices on disability and faith -- this tradition teaches that suffering can be purposeful.

Suffering produces endurance (Rom 5:3--4). It conforms believers to the image of Christ (Rom 8:29). It produces an "eternal weight of glory" (2 Cor 4:17). It is a participation in the sufferings of Christ (Phil 3:10). It is not evidence of God's absence but can be evidence of His intimate, transforming presence.

At its best, this theology does not produce resignation. It produces a faith that trusts God in the darkness -- not because the darkness is pleasant, but because God is trustworthy.

### Intellectual Rigor

The spiritual-healing-only view engages seriously with the biblical text and handles difficult passages directly. Paul's thorn, Trophimus's illness, Epaphroditus's near-death experience -- these are not embarrassments that need explaining away. They are straightforward illustrations of the view's central claim: God is sovereign, healing is not guaranteed, and faithful believers can suffer without spiritual failure.

This clarity is a genuine strength. For believers who want to know what the Bible teaches and are prepared to accept hard truths, the spiritual-healing-only view offers a coherent, internally consistent framework.

### God's Sovereignty Is Honored

This view places God firmly in control. He is not waiting for believers to muster enough faith before He can act. He is not constrained by human confession or limited by human doubt. He heals when He chooses to heal, and He withholds healing when His purposes are served by suffering. This is a God who is truly sovereign -- whose will is not dependent on human performance.

For many believers, this is deeply comforting. It means that their sickness is not outside God's plan. It means that God has not forgotten them. And it means that their inability to be healed is not a personal spiritual failure -- it is part of a larger purpose they may not fully understand this side of eternity.

### Medical Care Affirmed

The spiritual-healing-only view affirms medical care without reservation. Seeking a doctor is not "lack of faith." Taking medication is not a compromise of spiritual conviction. Medical science is a gift of God's common grace, and using it is an act of wisdom, not weakness.

This is pastorally important in a world where some (though not all) healing-in-the-atonement advocates have discouraged medical treatment. The cessationist tradition encourages believers to pray for healing *and* to see their doctor -- without any tension between the two.

## Honest Challenges -- Where This View Faces Difficulty

### Matthew 8:17

> "That it might be fulfilled which was spoken by Esaias the prophet, saying, Himself took our infirmities, and bare our sicknesses."
> --- Matthew 8:17 (KJV)

This is the most difficult verse for the spiritual-healing-only position. Matthew, an inspired New Testament author, takes a passage from Isaiah 53 -- the very passage the cessationist claims is about sin -- and applies it to physical healing. The Greek words Matthew uses (ἀσθένεια, *astheneia*, and νόσος, *nosos*) are unmistakably physical in their reference.

Cessationist scholars offer several explanations. D.A. Carson argues that Matthew is using a "pattern of fulfillment" -- Jesus's healings are a typological foreshadowing of the ultimate redemption the Servant will accomplish, not a statement about the atonement's provisions. Others argue that Jesus's pre-cross healings were apostolic sign gifts that authenticated His messianic identity, not examples of atonement provision.

These explanations are reasonable and well-argued. But they require the interpreter to explain why Matthew's plain language -- "Himself took our infirmities, and bare our sicknesses" -- does not mean what it appears to mean. The reader must assess whether the explanations adequately account for the text.

### James 5:14--16

> "Is any sick among you? let him call for the elders of the church; and let them pray over him, anointing him with oil in the name of the Lord: And the prayer of faith shall save the sick, and the Lord shall raise him up; and if he have committed sins, they shall be forgiven him."
> --- James 5:14--15 (KJV)

James instructs the church -- not just the apostles -- to pray for healing, anointing the sick with oil. He connects healing to forgiveness in a single sentence. And he makes no indication that this practice is temporary or limited to the apostolic age.

The cessationist responds that James is describing prayer for healing, which all Christians practice, but that the efficacy of the prayer depends on God's sovereign will, not on a guaranteed atonement provision. This is a reasonable reading, but it must account for the strong, confident language James uses: "the prayer of faith *shall save* the sick, and the Lord *shall raise* him up." The "shall" language is not easily softened.

### Global Christianity

The cessationist position is most strongly represented in Western, Reformed, and historically white evangelical churches. The fastest-growing expressions of Christianity in the world -- Pentecostal and charismatic churches in Africa, Asia, and Latin America -- practice healing prayer as a central element of their faith and report extensive healing testimonies.

This is not a theological argument, strictly speaking, but it is a reality that the cessationist must address. When hundreds of millions of Christians worldwide testify to healings, the cessationist claim that miraculous healing ceased with the apostles requires a comprehensive explanation for what these believers are experiencing. The standard responses -- psychosomatic healing, misdiagnosis, exaggeration, or demonic deception -- may account for some cases but strain to account for all of them.

Craig Keener's *Miracles* (2011) documents healing testimonies from around the world, many with medical corroboration. A serious cessationist position must engage with this evidence, not dismiss it.

### The Pastoral Challenge of Diminished Hope

While the spiritual-healing-only view correctly avoids false guilt, it can create a different pastoral problem: diminished hope for this-life healing. When a sick believer hears that "miraculous healing was for the apostolic age" or that "healing is not in the atonement," the effect can be a sense that God has withdrawn from the present age in some meaningful way.

The best cessationist pastors do not say "God doesn't heal." They say "God can heal, but doesn't always, and we trust His sovereignty." But in practice -- especially in churches where healing is rarely prayed for and never expected -- the functional message the sick believer receives can be: *Don't get your hopes up.*

This is not the intent of the theology, but it is sometimes the pastoral result. And it must be acknowledged honestly.

### The New Testament Evidence for Ongoing Gifts

The New Testament church clearly practiced healing as an ongoing ministry. Paul lists "gifts of healing" among the gifts of the Spirit given to the church (1 Cor 12:9, 28). James instructs the church to pray for healing (James 5:14--16). Luke records healing throughout Acts -- not only by apostles but also by non-apostles like Philip (Acts 8:6--7) and Ananias (Acts 9:17--18).

The cessationist argument that these gifts ceased requires a theological argument based on inference, because nothing in the New Testament explicitly states that the gifts will cease before the return of Christ. First Corinthians 13:8--10 ("when that which is perfect is come") is the most commonly cited text, but its interpretation is debated -- many scholars believe "that which is perfect" refers to the return of Christ, not the completion of the canon.

This is an argument from silence, and the spiritual-healing-only advocate must reckon with the fact that the burden of proof falls on the one claiming cessation, since the New Testament's own testimony is of a church that heals.

## What the Best Advocates Say

### "God Doesn't Heal" Is Not the Position

The best cessationist pastors are careful to distinguish between "God doesn't heal anymore" (which they do not teach) and "healing is not a guaranteed provision of the atonement" (which they do teach). The nuance matters enormously.

God is sovereign. He can heal anyone at any time. And cessationist pastors do pray for healing -- they simply do so with the understanding that God may say no, and that "no" is not a failure of faith but an expression of divine wisdom.

### Counseling the Sick

The best cessationist pastoral care is genuinely compassionate. It looks like this:

- **Pray for healing.** God is able, and prayer is commanded.
- **Trust God's sovereignty.** If healing does not come, God has not failed. His purposes are being worked out, even in suffering.
- **Use medicine gratefully.** Medical care is a gift of God's common grace. There is no spiritual compromise in seeing a doctor.
- **Find meaning in suffering.** Suffering is not meaningless. It can produce endurance, character, and hope (Rom 5:3--5). It can draw the believer closer to Christ. It can produce a witness to the world of a faith that endures in darkness.
- **Rest in the ultimate hope.** Even if healing does not come in this life, it is guaranteed at the resurrection. "And God shall wipe away all tears from their eyes; and there shall be no more death, neither sorrow, nor crying, neither shall there be any more pain: for the former things are passed away" (Rev 21:4, KJV).

This pastoral approach is not cold or dismissive. At its best, it offers the sick believer deep comfort: *You are not failing. God has not abandoned you. Your suffering has purpose. And the day is coming when all will be made right.*

### The Courage to Say "I Don't Know"

The best cessationist pastors are honest about mystery. They do not pretend to know why God heals some and not others. They do not reduce God's ways to a formula. They hold their theology firmly while holding the mystery humbly -- and they are willing to say, "I don't know why God has allowed this, but I know He is good, and I will sit with you in this."

That honesty -- the willingness to be present in suffering without offering glib answers -- is one of the greatest pastoral gifts the cessationist tradition has to offer.
