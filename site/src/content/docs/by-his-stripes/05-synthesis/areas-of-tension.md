The common ground is real, but so are the disagreements. The tensions mapped in this section are not the result of careless reading or bad faith. They arise because the biblical evidence genuinely points in more than one direction, because the early church did not ask our question, and because the lived experience of believers includes both remarkable healings and persistent, unexplained suffering.

Each tension below is presented with the strongest argument from each major position. No verdict is offered. The reader must weigh the evidence.

---

## 1. The Meaning of Rapha in Isaiah 53:5

> "And with his stripes we are healed."
> --- Isaiah 53:5 (KJV)

The Hebrew word behind "healed" is *rapha* (H7495). As Chapter 1's word study documented, this word is used extensively for physical healing throughout the Old Testament --- God heals Abimelech's household (Gen 20:17), heals Hezekiah's terminal illness (2 Kgs 20:5), heals Naaman's leprosy (2 Kgs 5:3, 6, 7, 10, 11). But it is also used for spiritual or metaphorical restoration --- "heal your backslidings" (Jer 3:22), "heal my soul; for I have sinned" (Ps 41:4), "lest they... convert, and be healed" (Isa 6:10).

**The healing-in-the-atonement view:** The primary and most frequent meaning of *rapha* is physical healing. Its use alongside *choliy* ("sickness") in verse 4 and *chaburah* ("stripes" --- physical wound marks) in verse 5 points toward a physical referent. The word should be read in its natural, primary sense unless the context compels otherwise.

**The spiritual-healing-only view:** The immediate context of verse 5 is dominated by sin-language: "transgressions," "iniquities," "chastisement." The four parallel lines of the verse move from sin to its remedy. "Healed" here functions like *rapha* in Isaiah 6:10 and Jeremiah 3:22 --- a prophetic metaphor for spiritual restoration. Peter confirms this reading in 1 Peter 2:24, where he quotes the verse in a context entirely about sin and righteousness.

**The already/not yet view:** *Rapha* is genuinely comprehensive. It does not require a choice between physical and spiritual. The Servant's suffering produces *shalom* --- total well-being --- and the healing encompasses the whole person. The spiritual dimension is primary in the immediate context, but the physical dimension is present in the broader passage (verse 4) and in the word's natural range.

The tension is irreducible. The Hebrew permits all three readings. The question is whether the context narrows the meaning or leaves it open.

## 2. Matthew 8:17 vs. 1 Peter 2:24 --- Does the New Testament Give Two Different Applications?

This is one of the most important tensions in the entire study. Two inspired authors quote Isaiah 53 --- and they apply it in different directions.

**Matthew 8:17:** Matthew quotes Isaiah 53:4 in the context of physical healing: "Himself took our infirmities, and bare our sicknesses" (KJV). His Greek words --- *astheneia* ("infirmity") and *nosos* ("disease") --- are unambiguously physical. He deliberately departed from the LXX's rendering of *choliy* as *hamartias* ("sins") and chose physical-sickness language instead.

**1 Peter 2:24:** Peter quotes Isaiah 53:5 in the context of sin and righteousness: "Who his own self bare our sins in his own body on the tree, that we, being dead to sins, should live unto righteousness: by whose stripes ye were healed" (KJV). His context is entirely spiritual --- sin, righteousness, sheep going astray and returning to the Shepherd.

**The healing-in-the-atonement view:** Both applications are valid because Isaiah 53 encompasses both dimensions. Matthew draws out the physical dimension (verse 4); Peter draws out the spiritual dimension (verse 5). Together they reveal the full scope of the Servant's work. Peter's spiritual application does not cancel Matthew's physical application, any more than Matthew's physical application cancels Peter's spiritual one.

**The spiritual-healing-only view:** Peter's application carries more weight for the atonement question because Peter is quoting verse 5 --- the very verse that says "by his stripes we are healed" --- and applying it to spiritual healing. Matthew quotes verse 4, not verse 5, and applies it to the pre-cross healing ministry, not to the atonement specifically. The distinction between verse 4 (the Servant's identification with suffering) and verse 5 (the Servant's atoning work) may be intentional.

**The already/not yet view:** Matthew and Peter are drawing on different dimensions of a single, multifaceted passage. The passage's meaning is not exhausted by either apostle's application. Isaiah 53:4--5 is a unity in the original Hebrew, and both physical and spiritual dimensions are woven into its fabric. The New Testament authors are not contradicting each other; they are illuminating different facets of the same diamond.

The tension stands. Both applications are inspired. Both are in the canon. The reader must decide how they relate.

## 3. "Fulfilled" --- Exhaustive Completion or Typological Application?

Matthew 8:17 uses the word *pleroo* ("to fulfill") to connect Jesus's healings to Isaiah 53:4. But what does "fulfilled" mean? Chapter 1's analysis of Matthew's use of *pleroo* across his Gospel showed that Matthew employs the word in at least four different senses.

**The healing-in-the-atonement view:** Matthew uses his standard fulfillment formula --- the same construction used for unambiguous messianic fulfillments like the virgin birth (Matt 1:22), the flight to Egypt (Matt 2:15), and the triumphal entry (Matt 21:4). If *pleroo* establishes genuine messianic fulfillment in those cases, it does the same in Matthew 8:17. The healings are not merely illustrative; they are genuine fulfillments of what Isaiah 53 prophesied the Servant would accomplish.

**The spiritual-healing-only view:** Matthew's use of *pleroo* is more nuanced than simple prediction-and-completion. In Matthew 2:17--18, he says Jeremiah 31:15 is "fulfilled" in Herod's slaughter of the innocents --- but Jeremiah was describing the Babylonian exile, not predicting Herod. Matthew sees patterns in Israel's history that are "filled up" in Jesus. The fulfillment in 8:17 may be typological --- Jesus's healings fill up the Servant pattern without establishing healing as a permanent atonement provision.

**The already/not yet view:** The fulfillment is genuine but inaugural. Jesus's pre-cross healings are real manifestations of the Servant's mission, but they point forward to the full redemption that the cross secures and the resurrection consummates. The healings are first fruits, not the full harvest.

This tension is sharpened by the timing question that Chapter 1 identified: Jesus's healings in Matthew 8 occur *before* the crucifixion. If the healings are atonement provisions, how can they be "fulfilled" before the atonement happens? If they are merely signs, why does Matthew use his strongest fulfillment language?

However, as Chapter 1's expanded analysis showed, **the timing objection applies equally to forgiveness** --- and no one finds it troubling there. David was forgiven before the cross (2 Samuel 12:13). Abraham was justified by faith before the cross (Genesis 15:6). Paul explains that God "passed over" former sins because the atonement, though future in time, was certain in God's purposes (Romans 3:25). Jesus himself connected an Old Testament physical healing --- the bronze serpent (Numbers 21:8--9) --- directly to the cross: "As Moses lifted up the serpent in the wilderness, even so must the Son of man be lifted up" (John 3:14, KJV). The prophets Elijah and Elisha also healed before the cross, by God's power, in anticipation of redemption yet to come.

If forgiveness before the cross was grounded in the future atonement, the healing-in-atonement advocate asks, why can't healing before the cross be grounded the same way? The spiritual-only advocate responds that Old Testament forgiveness had a mechanism --- blood sacrifice --- pointing forward to the cross, while Old Testament healings had no comparable sacrificial mechanism. This is a fair distinction, though the bronze serpent (a type Jesus himself connected to the cross) complicates it.

The timing question remains a genuine tension, but the forgiveness parallel significantly weakens its force as a one-sided objection against healing in the atonement.

## 4. The Scope of Atonement Application: What Is "Now" vs. "Not Yet"?

All three views acknowledge that the atonement's benefits unfold over time. Justification is past. Sanctification is present. Glorification is future. The question is where physical healing fits.

**The healing-in-the-atonement view:** Healing belongs with justification --- it is a present provision, available now by faith. Just as the believer receives forgiveness at the moment of faith, the believer can receive healing at the moment of faith. The provision was made at the cross; it waits only to be claimed. Psalm 103:3 places them in parallel: "Who forgiveth all thine iniquities; who healeth all thy diseases" (KJV).

**The spiritual-healing-only view:** Healing belongs with glorification --- it is a future provision, guaranteed at the resurrection. Romans 8:23 is explicit: believers who have the "firstfruits of the Spirit" are still "waiting for... the redemption of our body." If the body's redemption were a present provision, Paul would not describe it as something believers groan and wait for.

**The already/not yet view:** Healing spans the timeline. It has been accomplished (Isa 53:4; Matt 8:17), is genuinely available now as the Spirit wills (1 Cor 12:9; James 5:14--16), and will be fully realized at the resurrection (1 Cor 15:42--44; Rev 21:4). This is the same pattern as salvation itself: purchased at the cross, applied progressively, consummated at the return.

The pastoral stakes of this tension are high. If healing is a present provision, the unhealed believer faces the question: why have I not received what is available? If healing is entirely future, the believer who prays for healing may be told to lower their expectations. If healing spans the timeline, the practical guidance can feel unclear. Each position must face the consequences of its answer.

## 5. The Cessation Question: Did Confirmatory Signs Cease?

Chapter 4's presentation of the spiritual-healing-only view included the cessationist argument built on two passages:

> "And they went forth, and preached every where, the Lord working with them, and confirming the word with signs following."
> --- Mark 16:20 (KJV)

> "How shall we escape, if we neglect so great salvation; which at the first began to be spoken by the Lord, and was confirmed unto us by them that heard him; God also bearing them witness, both with signs and wonders, and with divers miracles, and gifts of the Holy Ghost, according to his own will?"
> --- Hebrews 2:3--4 (KJV)

**The cessationist argument:** Signs had a specific purpose --- confirming the word during the apostolic era. The past tense of Hebrews 2:3--4 ("was confirmed") indicates this confirmation has been accomplished. The canon is the confirmed word. The signs that accompanied its giving are no longer necessary.

**The continuationist argument:** Nothing in the New Testament explicitly states that gifts of healing will cease before Christ's return. Paul lists "gifts of healing" among the Spirit's gifts to the church (1 Cor 12:9, 28) without any temporal limitation. James instructs the church --- not just the apostles --- to pray for the sick (James 5:14--16). First Corinthians 13:8--10 ("when that which is perfect is come") likely refers to Christ's return, not the completion of the canon. And the global church's testimony of ongoing healings is difficult to dismiss entirely.

**The evidence from antiquity:** Chapter 3 documented that the early church practiced healing well beyond the apostolic generation. Justin Martyr (c. 165 AD), Origen (c. 250 AD), and the *Apostolic Tradition* (c. 215 AD) all attest to healing in their communities. If the signs ceased with the apostles, the early church did not seem to know it.

This tension cannot be resolved by proof texts alone. It requires a judgment about the nature of the apostolic era, the purpose of miracles, and the weight to be given to the global church's ongoing testimony.

## 6. Why the Early Church Practiced Healing but Did Not Ground It in Isaiah 53

This is one of the most striking findings of Chapter 3, and it creates tension for every position.

The evidence is consistent: from Clement of Rome (c. 96 AD) to Cyprian of Carthage (c. 252 AD), the early church's writers read Isaiah 53 as a prophecy of Christ's substitutionary suffering for sin. Not one surviving pre-Nicene document connects "by his stripes we are healed" to physical healing. And yet the same church clearly practiced healing prayer, recognized gifts of healing, and testified to divine cures.

**The healing-in-the-atonement advocate must explain** why the earliest post-apostolic witnesses show no awareness of the connection between Isaiah 53 and physical healing. The typical explanation --- they were asking different questions, facing persecution, writing in different genres --- is reasonable, but the silence is broad and consistent.

**The spiritual-healing-only advocate must explain** why the early church practiced healing vigorously without grounding it in the atonement. If healing is not an atonement provision, and if the early church knew this, why was healing still a central practice? The answer --- that healing flows from God's sovereign grace and the Spirit's gifts, not from the atonement specifically --- is coherent, but it raises the question of what precisely drives healing in the church if not the cross.

**The already/not yet advocate** may find the early evidence most congenial: the church experienced genuine healing (the "already") while its theological reflection focused on sin-bearing (the primary meaning of Isaiah 53 in its context). The practice and the theology were not in contradiction; they were operating at different levels.

## 7. The Rabbinic Interpretive Shift --- What Does the Trajectory Tell Us?

Chapter 3 traced a clear arc: the messianic reading of Isaiah 53 was well-attested in pre-Christian and first-century Judaism (Targum Jonathan's identification "my servant the Messiah," Sanhedrin 98b's "leper scholar"). After Christianity adopted the passage as its central proof text, Jewish interpretation shifted toward the corporate-Israel reading, reaching its decisive expression in Rashi (c. 1040--1105 AD).

**The argument for significance:** The trajectory suggests that the messianic reading was the earlier and more natural one. The shift away from it correlates with Christianity's use of the passage. This supports the Christian claim that Isaiah 53 describes a suffering Messiah --- and strengthens the case that "by his stripes we are healed" refers to what the Messiah's suffering accomplishes.

**The argument for caution:** The trajectory tells us about the *identity* of the Servant, not about the *nature* of the healing. A reader could fully accept the messianic reading and still conclude that "healed" means spiritually healed. The Talmudic "leper scholar" passage (Sanhedrin 98b) uses the physical-sickness language of Isaiah 53:4 but presents the Messiah as identified *with* the sick, not as healing them *through* his suffering.

**What the trajectory does not resolve:** Whether the healing of Isaiah 53:5 is physical, spiritual, or both. The rabbinic evidence illuminates the Servant's identity but leaves the nature of the healing open.

## 8. Faith and Healing: Is Faith a Condition? What About Paul's Thorn?

The relationship between faith and healing is one of the most pastorally charged tensions in the entire debate.

Chapter 2 documented a complex pattern in Jesus's ministry. Sometimes he healed in response to the sick person's faith: "Thy faith hath made thee whole" (Matt 9:22, KJV). Sometimes he healed in response to others' faith --- the centurion's servant (Matt 8:5--13), the paralytic's friends (Mark 2:3--5). Sometimes he healed with no mention of faith at all --- the man at Bethesda (John 5:5--9), Malchus's ear (Luke 22:50--51).

But Chapter 2's analysis also uncovered a striking pattern that is easy to miss: **during Jesus's earthly ministry, the Gospels do not record a single case of an individual coming to him in faith for physical healing and being turned away.** Every person who sought healing from Jesus in faith received it --- the woman with the issue of blood, blind Bartimaeus, the centurion's servant, the Syrophoenician woman (who was initially tested but ultimately healed and commended for her faith). The Nazareth episode (Mark 6:5--6) is about the *absence* of faith, not its presence. During the earthly ministry, faith for healing was always met with healing.

The "no" to healing emerges only in the post-ascension period --- most notably in Paul's thorn in the flesh (2 Corinthians 12:7--10). But as Chapter 1 and Chapter 2 both documented, **the nature of Paul's thorn is genuinely debated**. The text does not identify it. Paul calls it "a messenger [*angelos*] of Satan to buffet [*kolaphizō*] me" --- language that points more naturally toward a personal adversary or persistent persecution than toward a disease. Early church fathers like Chrysostom identified the thorn as Paul's human opponents. If the thorn was not physical illness, this passage has no direct bearing on the healing question, and the Gospel pattern of faith always being met with healing stands unchallenged. If it was physical illness, it is the clearest New Testament case of a faithful believer praying for healing and being told no. The weight of this passage in the debate depends on an identification the text does not provide.

**The healing-in-the-atonement view:** Faith is the normal means by which believers receive what the atonement provides, just as faith is the means of receiving forgiveness. The Gospel pattern confirms this: when faith for healing was present during Jesus's ministry, healing always followed. Paul's thorn was likely persecution, not sickness (based on the *angelos* and *kolaphizō* language), and even if it was sickness, it was a unique case with a stated purpose (preventing pride), not the normative pattern. The normative pattern is: "the prayer of faith shall save the sick" (James 5:15, KJV).

**The spiritual-healing-only view:** The Gospel healings reflect the unique dynamics of the incarnation --- Jesus was physically present, performing messianic signs to authenticate his identity. The post-ascension period is the relevant comparison for the church's experience, and there the evidence includes Paul's thorn (most naturally read as a physical ailment, given the parallel with Galatians 4:13--15 and the "flesh" language), Trophimus left sick (2 Tim 4:20), and Epaphroditus near death (Phil 2:25--27). God said to Paul: "My grace is sufficient for thee: for my strength is made perfect in weakness" (2 Cor 12:9, KJV). If the greatest apostle was told that God's purposes would be accomplished through his weakness rather than through his healing, this is normative, not exceptional.

**The already/not yet view:** The relationship between faith and healing is real but not mechanical. The Gospel pattern --- where faith was always met with healing --- reflects the full presence of the kingdom in Jesus's person. In the present age, between the ascension and the return, the kingdom is genuinely present but not yet consummated. Faith creates an environment where healing often occurs, but the sovereignty of God determines the outcome. Paul's thorn, whatever its nature, illustrates that God's purposes sometimes include suffering --- and that grace in suffering is not a lesser gift than healing.

The pastoral weight of this tension cannot be overstated. How a pastor understands the faith-healing relationship will shape what they say to the believer who has prayed and not been healed. Every position carries pastoral risk: the healing view risks blaming the sick; the spiritual-only view risks extinguishing hope; the already/not yet view risks vagueness in the moment of crisis.

## 9. Pastoral Damage: Does Each View Risk Real Harm?

Chapter 4's pastoral considerations section was unflinching in its assessment. Every theological position on healing in the atonement carries the potential for pastoral harm when applied without wisdom and love.

**The healing-in-the-atonement view, at its worst,** tells the sick believer that their illness is evidence of insufficient faith. The spiritual damage --- guilt compounded on suffering, isolation from community, crisis of faith, distortion of God's character --- is well-documented and devastating. Chapter 4 stated this plainly: any theology that leaves the sick feeling condemned has failed the most basic test of pastoral care.

**The spiritual-healing-only view, at its worst,** tells the sick believer to lower their expectations. When healing is relegated to the past or to the future, the functional message can become: God has withdrawn from the present in some meaningful way. The result --- diminished hope, perfunctory prayer, a sense that God cares about the soul but not the body --- is a different kind of wound, but it is real.

**The already/not yet view, at its worst,** produces theological ambiguity that sounds sophisticated but offers no clear guidance at the bedside. When both healing and non-healing are explained by the same framework, the sick believer may hear: "I don't really know what to expect, and neither does my theology."

The honest conclusion from Chapter 4 was this: every view, when applied with love, wisdom, and humility, produces good fruit. Every view, when applied with arrogance, rigidity, or insensitivity, produces harm. The fruit test does not resolve the theological question. But it does expose how each position functions when it meets real human suffering.

The reader must weigh not only the exegetical arguments but also the pastoral consequences of the position they adopt --- and hold that position with the kind of love that refuses to add suffering to suffering.

## 10. The Scope of the Curse and the Scope of Redemption

The [Curse and Redemption](/01-scripture/curse-and-redemption/) section in Chapter 1 traced a biblical arc that runs from Genesis 3 to Revelation 22:3: the curse introduced by the fall, the curse codified in the law (Deuteronomy 28), the curse borne by Christ (Galatians 3:13), and the curse removed in the new creation ("there shall be no more curse," Rev 22:3, KJV). The central question is whether this arc includes physical healing as a present provision or a future hope.

**The healing-in-the-atonement view:** The law's curses explicitly include disease. Deuteronomy 28:21--22 names pestilence, consumption, fever, and inflammation. Deuteronomy 28:61 adds "every sickness, and every plague" (*choliy* --- the same Hebrew word used in Isaiah 53:4). Galatians 3:13 declares that Christ redeemed us from these curses. If the curses include disease and Christ redeemed us from the curses, then healing from disease is a present provision of the cross. Derek Prince and T.L. Osborn developed this argument with particular force.

**The spiritual-healing-only view:** Paul's use of "the curse of the law" in Galatians 3:10--13 is specifically about the curse that falls on those who seek justification through law-keeping ("Cursed is every one that continueth not in all things which are written in the book of the law to do them," Gal 3:10, KJV). Paul is arguing about justification, not about the full scope of Deuteronomy 28's penalties. Reading the disease-curses into Galatians 3:13 expands his argument beyond its context. Furthermore, Paul himself suffered physically (2 Cor 12:7--10; Gal 4:13--14) and did not appeal to the curse-redemption argument for relief.

**The already/not yet view:** The curse-redemption arc is real and comprehensive. Christ has redeemed believers from the curse (already). But the full removal of the curse --- including all disease and death --- awaits the new creation (not yet). The disease-curses of Deuteronomy 28 have been addressed at the cross; their complete eradication belongs to the resurrection. Present healings are genuine anticipations of the curse's final removal, but the removal itself is not complete in this age.

**The lexical connection that sharpens the tension:** The Hebrew word *choliy* in Deuteronomy 28:61 is the same word used in Isaiah 53:4. If Isaiah 53:4 is about physical sickness (as Matthew 8:17 applies it), and if Deuteronomy 28:61 uses the same word for the law's disease-curses, then the thread connecting the curse, the Servant's bearing, and healing through the atonement is lexically grounded, not merely theologically constructed. The reader must decide whether this lexical connection establishes the link or whether context overrides etymology.

## Summary

The ten tensions mapped above are genuine. They arise from the complexity of the biblical evidence, the limitations of the historical record, and the irreducible mystery of a God who both heals and allows suffering. None of them are resolved by a single verse, a single tradition, or a single experience.

But these tensions are not cause for despair. They are the mark of a question that touches the deepest realities of human life --- suffering, faith, hope, and the nature of God's redemptive work. The question is worth wrestling with, and the wrestling itself can deepen faith.

The final section of this chapter offers the reader a set of questions --- not to resolve the tensions but to help the reader think through them with honesty, care, and dependence on the Holy Spirit.
