One of the most significant biblical threads in the healing-in-the-atonement discussion is the arc from curse to redemption. The Bible opens with a curse (Genesis 3) and closes with its removal (Revelation 22:3). Between those bookends, the Law codifies disease as part of the curse (Deuteronomy 28), the prophets declare that the Servant bears our sicknesses (Isaiah 53:4), and Paul announces that Christ has redeemed us from the curse of the law (Galatians 3:13). Whether this arc supports a present provision of physical healing --- or points instead to an eschatological hope --- is one of the sharpest questions in the debate.

This chapter traces the curse-redemption thread through its major biblical waypoints and presents what each major view does with the evidence.

---

## The Curse Introduced: Genesis 3:14--19

> **Genesis 3:16--19 (KJV)**
>
> Unto the woman he said, I will greatly multiply thy sorrow and thy conception; in sorrow thou shalt bring forth children; and thy desire shall be to thy husband, and he shall rule over thee. And unto Adam he said, Because thou hast hearkened unto the voice of thy wife, and hast eaten of the tree, of which I commanded thee, saying, Thou shalt not eat of it: cursed is the ground for thy sake; in sorrow shalt thou eat of it all the days of thy life; Thorns also and thistles shall it bring forth to thee; and thou shalt eat the herb of the field; In the sweat of thy face shalt thou eat bread, till thou return unto the ground; for out of it wast thou taken: for dust thou art, and unto dust shalt thou return.

The consequences of the fall are comprehensive. They include:

- **Death:** "Dust thou art, and unto dust shalt thou return" (v. 19). Physical death enters human experience. Paul confirms the connection directly: "By one man sin entered into the world, and death by sin; and so death passed upon all men" (Romans 5:12, KJV).
- **Pain:** The Hebrew word *'itstsabown* (H6093) --- "sorrow" in the KJV --- means pain, labor, toil. It is used for the woman's pain in childbearing (v. 16) and for the man's painful toil in working the ground (v. 17).
- **Thorns and thistles:** The ground itself is cursed, producing resistance to human labor (v. 18). Later, Jesus would wear a crown of thorns (Matthew 27:29) --- an image some interpreters read as the Servant bearing the curse.
- **Bodily decay:** The trajectory from "dust thou art" to "unto dust shalt thou return" describes the deterioration of the human body over time --- the process that includes aging, weakness, sickness, and death.

### What the Text Does and Does Not Say

The word "sickness" does not appear in Genesis 3. The curse produces death, pain, toil, and a cursed ground --- but it does not specifically mention disease. All three views agree, however, that the fall introduced the conditions under which sickness occurs. The question is whether the atonement reverses those conditions now or at the resurrection.

**All views agree:** The fall introduced death and physical suffering into the human experience. The conditions that produce sickness are a consequence of the fall. Christ's redemption addresses the fall's consequences.

**Where the views diverge:** Does Christ's reversal of the fall include present physical healing, or is bodily restoration reserved for the resurrection?

## The Curse Codified: Deuteronomy 28

When God established the Mosaic covenant with Israel, the blessings for obedience (Deuteronomy 28:1--14) and the curses for disobedience (Deuteronomy 28:15--68) were spelled out in extraordinary detail. Among the curses, disease features prominently:

> **Deuteronomy 28:21--22 (KJV)**
>
> The LORD shall make the pestilence cleave unto thee, until he have consumed thee from off the land, whither thou goest to possess it. The LORD shall smite thee with a consumption, and with a fever, and with an inflammation, and with an extreme burning, and with the sword, and with blasting, and with mildew; and they shall pursue thee until thou perish.

> **Deuteronomy 28:27--28 (KJV)**
>
> The LORD will smite thee with the botch of Egypt, and with the emerods, and with the scab, and with the itch, whereof thou canst not be healed. The LORD shall smite thee with madness, and blindness, and astonishment of heart.

> **Deuteronomy 28:35 (KJV)**
>
> The LORD shall smite thee in the knees, and in the legs, with a sore botch that cannot be healed, from the sole of thy foot unto the top of thy head.

And then the passage reaches its climax:

> **Deuteronomy 28:59--61 (KJV)**
>
> Then the LORD will make thy plagues wonderful, and the plagues of thy seed, even great plagues, and of long continuance, and sore sicknesses, and of long continuance. Moreover he will bring upon thee all the diseases of Egypt, which thou wast afraid of; and they shall cleave unto thee. Also every sickness, and every plague, which is not written in the book of this law, them will the LORD bring upon thee, until thou be destroyed.

### The *Choliy* Connection

The Hebrew word translated "sickness" in Deuteronomy 28:61 is *choliy* (H2483). This is the same word that appears in Isaiah 53:3--4:

> "He is despised and rejected of men; a man of sorrows, and acquainted with grief [*choliy*]... Surely he hath borne our griefs [*choliy*], and carried our sorrows."
> --- Isaiah 53:3--4 (KJV)

The KJV translates *choliy* as "griefs" in Isaiah 53 but as "sickness" in Deuteronomy 28. The NASB (2020) changed its rendering of Isaiah 53:4 from "griefs" to "sicknesses," recognizing the physical meaning of the word. In Deuteronomy 28:61, *choliy* clearly means physical disease --- "every sickness" is the plain meaning. The question is whether *choliy* carries the same physical sense in Isaiah 53:4 or has been elevated to a broader metaphorical meaning by the prophetic context.

This lexical connection is the exegetical backbone of the curse-redemption argument for healing in the atonement. The argument runs: the curse of the law includes *choliy* (disease); the Servant bears our *choliy*; therefore the Servant's suffering addresses the very diseases the law pronounced as curses.

### Three Views on the Deuteronomy 28 Connection

**The healing-in-the-atonement view:** If the curse of the law includes disease (as Deuteronomy 28 plainly states), and if Christ redeemed us from the curse of the law (Galatians 3:13), then redemption from disease is included. The *choliy* connection between Deuteronomy 28:61 and Isaiah 53:4 is not a coincidence --- it is the same vocabulary describing the same category of suffering. Derek Prince, T.L. Osborn, and Kenneth Hagin all build their healing theology partly on this link.

**The spiritual-healing-only view:** Deuteronomy 28 is a covenant document addressed to national Israel under the Mosaic law. Its curses are covenant sanctions for covenant disobedience, not a universal catalog of the consequences of sin. Gentile believers were never under the Deuteronomy 28 curses to begin with. And when Paul speaks of "the curse of the law" in Galatians 3:13, he defines it in the preceding verse: "Cursed is every one that continueth not in all things which are written in the book of the law to do them" (Galatians 3:10, quoting Deuteronomy 27:26). The curse is condemnation for failing to keep the law --- a legal verdict, not a medical diagnosis.

**The already/not yet view:** The Deuteronomy 28 curses represent the full consequences of the fall as expressed in the covenant. Christ's redemption addresses the whole curse --- sin, sickness, and death --- but the full outworking follows the same pattern as every other dimension of redemption: inaugurated now, consummated later. Disease-freedom belongs to the "not yet" of full bodily redemption (Romans 8:23), while present healings are foretastes of the coming fullness.

## The Curse Borne: Galatians 3:13--14

> **Galatians 3:13--14 (KJV)**
>
> Christ hath redeemed us from the curse of the law, being made a curse for us: for it is written, Cursed is every one that hangeth on a tree: That the blessing of Abraham might come on the Gentiles through Jesus Christ; that we might receive the promise of the Spirit through faith.

This is the crux of the curse-redemption argument. Paul declares that Christ has redeemed believers from "the curse of the law" by becoming a curse himself on the cross (quoting Deuteronomy 21:23 --- "he that is hanged is accursed of God"). The word "redeemed" is *exagorazō* (G1805) --- to buy out of the marketplace, to ransom, to purchase from bondage. The language implies complete deliverance from a previous state of enslavement.

### Paul's Argument in Context

Paul's argument in Galatians 3:1--14 concerns justification and the inclusion of the Gentiles:

- Galatians 3:6--9: Abraham was justified by faith, and those who believe are blessed with Abraham.
- Galatians 3:10: Those who rely on works of the law are under a curse (quoting Deuteronomy 27:26).
- Galatians 3:11--12: No one is justified by the law; the righteous live by faith.
- Galatians 3:13: Christ redeemed us from the curse of the law.
- Galatians 3:14: The result --- "the blessing of Abraham" comes to the Gentiles, and "we receive the promise of the Spirit through faith."

Paul's stated outcome of the redemption is "the promise of the Spirit through faith" --- an explicitly spiritual blessing. His entire argument addresses how Gentiles are included in God's people without submitting to the Mosaic law.

### The Interpretive Crux: What Is "the Curse of the Law"?

**The narrow reading:** "The curse of the law" is the condemnation for failing to keep the law perfectly (as defined in Galatians 3:10). Christ bore that condemnation on the cross. The result is justification, not healing. Paul is addressing a legal problem (guilt before God) with a legal solution (substitutionary curse-bearing). The diseases listed in Deuteronomy 28 are not in view.

**The broad reading:** "The curse of the law" encompasses everything the law pronounced as a curse --- including the diseases of Deuteronomy 28:21--28, 35, 59--61. Paul references the law's curse; the law's curse includes disease. Christ bore the curse; therefore Christ bore the disease-curse as well. The *choliy* connection from Deuteronomy 28:61 to Isaiah 53:4 confirms that the Servant's work addresses the same diseases the law cursed.

**The already/not yet reading:** The broad reading is correct --- the curse of the law does encompass disease --- but the full removal of the curse unfolds across the redemptive timeline. Christ has redeemed believers from the curse (already), securing complete freedom from every dimension of it. But the disease-curses, like death itself, are not fully eradicated until the resurrection (not yet). Present healings are genuine anticipations of the curse's final removal, not evidence that the curse has been entirely lifted in this age.

All three readings are exegetically defensible. The narrow reading fits Paul's immediate argument in Galatians 3 (which is about justification and the inclusion of Gentiles). The broad reading fits the comprehensive scope of the curse in Deuteronomy 28 and the comprehensive scope of Christ's redemptive work elsewhere in Paul (Romans 5:12--21; 8:19--23; 1 Corinthians 15:21--22). The already/not yet reading accepts the broad scope while accounting for the temporal pattern visible in Romans 8:23.

### Justin Martyr's Early Witness

It is worth noting that the connection between Christ and the curse was recognized very early. Justin Martyr, writing around 165 AD, argues in his *Dialogue with Trypho* (chapter 95):

> "For the whole human race will be found to be under a curse. For it is written in the law of Moses, 'Cursed is every one that continueth not in all things that are written in the book of the law to do them.' And no one has accurately done all... but if the Father of all wished His Christ to take upon Himself the curses of all, knowing that after He had been crucified and was dead He would raise Him up, why do you argue about Him who was willing to suffer these things according to the Father's will, as if He were accursed?"

Justin describes Christ as taking upon himself "the curses of all" --- language that is comprehensive, though Justin does not develop it into a healing-in-the-atonement theology. He is making a christological argument (the Messiah's death was not shameful but purposeful), not a healing argument. But the comprehensive language is present in the earliest theological reflection on this passage.

## The Adam-Christ Parallel: Romans 5 and 1 Corinthians 15

Paul develops the curse-redemption arc in two major passages that place Adam and Christ in direct contrast.

### Romans 5:12, 17--19

> **Romans 5:12 (KJV)**
>
> Wherefore, as by one man sin entered into the world, and death by sin; and so death passed upon all men, for that all have sinned.

> **Romans 5:17--19 (KJV)**
>
> For if by one man's offence death reigned by one; much more they which receive abundance of grace and of the gift of righteousness shall reign in life by one, Jesus Christ.) Therefore as by the offence of one judgment came upon all men to condemnation; even so by the righteousness of one the free gift came upon all men unto justification of life. For as by one man's disobedience many were made sinners, so by the obedience of one shall many be made righteous.

The parallel is sustained and deliberate. By one man (Adam) came sin, death, condemnation. By one Man (Christ) came grace, life, justification. The structure invites a comprehensive reading: everything Adam introduced, Christ reverses.

### 1 Corinthians 15:21--22, 45--49

> **1 Corinthians 15:21--22 (KJV)**
>
> For since by man came death, by man came also the resurrection of the dead. For as in Adam all die, even so in Christ shall all be made alive.

> **1 Corinthians 15:45, 47, 49 (KJV)**
>
> And so it is written, The first man Adam was made a living soul; the last Adam was made a quickening spirit... The first man is of the earth, earthy: the second man is the Lord from heaven... And as we have borne the image of the earthy, we shall also bear the image of the heavenly.

Paul calls Christ "the last Adam" --- a designation unique to this passage. The implication is that Christ is the definitive, final representative human who undoes what the first Adam did. But Paul's context in 1 Corinthians 15 is the **resurrection of the dead**, not present healing. "In Christ shall all be made alive" is future tense. "We shall also bear the image of the heavenly" is future tense. The chapter's argument is about what happens to the body at the resurrection, not what happens to the body during this life.

### The Debate Over Scope and Timing

**The healing-in-the-atonement view:** The Adam-Christ parallel is comprehensive. If Adam introduced sin, sickness, and death, then Christ reverses all three. Death is the ultimate curse; sickness is the process of dying. If Christ defeated death (which He did), He defeated sickness too. Present healing is part of the "already" aspect of Christ's victory over the curse. Just as believers receive spiritual life now (not only at the resurrection), they can receive physical healing now.

**The spiritual-healing-only view:** The parallel is theological, not chronological. Adam's sin had immediate spiritual consequences (alienation from God) and eventual physical consequences (death came later, not instantly). Similarly, Christ's work provides immediate spiritual benefits (justification) and eventual physical benefits (resurrection). Paul says "shall all be made alive" --- future tense. "We shall bear the image of the heavenly" --- future tense. The chapter is about resurrection, not healing. No believer experiences full physical redemption now --- all still die.

**The already/not yet view:** The parallel is comprehensive but temporally unfolding. Christ is the "firstfruits" of resurrection (1 Corinthians 15:20) --- the beginning, with the rest to follow. The "already" includes spiritual life, justification, and occasional physical healings as foretastes. The "not yet" includes the resurrection body, the full removal of the curse, and permanent freedom from sickness and death. Romans 8:23 clarifies the timing: "We ourselves, which have the firstfruits of the Spirit, even we ourselves groan within ourselves, waiting for the adoption, to wit, the redemption of our body."

## The Curse Removed: Revelation 22:3 and Romans 8:19--23

The Bible's final word on the curse is unambiguous:

> **Revelation 22:3 (KJV)**
>
> And there shall be no more curse: but the throne of God and of the Lamb shall be in it; and his servants shall serve him.

Genesis 3 introduces the curse. Revelation 22:3 removes it. The entire biblical narrative runs from curse to no-more-curse, and the cross is the pivot point. What is debated is not whether the curse is removed but when.

Paul addresses the timing in Romans 8:

> **Romans 8:19--21 (KJV)**
>
> For the earnest expectation of the creature waiteth for the manifestation of the sons of God. For the creature was made subject to vanity, not willingly, but by reason of him who hath subjected the same in hope, Because the creature itself also shall be delivered from the bondage of corruption into the glorious liberty of the children of God.

Creation itself is under the curse --- "subjected to vanity," in "bondage of corruption." These phrases echo the language of Genesis 3. But creation's deliverance is future: "shall be delivered" (future tense), "waiting for" (present groaning). And Paul includes believers in this groaning:

> **Romans 8:23 (KJV)**
>
> And not only they, but ourselves also, which have the firstfruits of the Spirit, even we ourselves groan within ourselves, waiting for the adoption, to wit, the redemption of our body.

The "redemption of our body" (*apolytrosis tou somatos*) connects the body's future transformation to Christ's redemptive work. The word "redemption" (*apolytrosis*) is the same word family used for Christ's ransom. The body *will* be redeemed. But Paul says we are "waiting for" it --- it has not yet occurred. The "firstfruits of the Spirit" is a down payment, a foretaste, a guarantee of the full inheritance to come.

### Three Views on the Timing of Curse Removal

**The healing-in-the-atonement view:** Revelation 22:3 describes the consummation, not the current state. Just as Jesus is already "King of Kings" but will fully reign in the future, curse removal was accomplished at the cross and will be universally manifested at the return. Between the cross and the consummation, the curse is defeated in principle and its effects can be overcome by faith. "Redemption of our body" in Romans 8:23 refers to the resurrection body, not to present healing --- but present healing is available as an atonement provision, distinct from the final glorification.

**The spiritual-healing-only view:** If the curse were already removed, Revelation 22:3 would be unnecessary. Paul says we "groan inwardly" and "wait for the redemption of our body" --- present reality for believers. Physical death remains universal for all believers, proving that the physical dimension of the curse is not yet reversed. Spiritual curse-effects (condemnation, alienation from God) are removed now. Physical curse-effects (sickness, aging, death) are removed at the resurrection.

**The already/not yet view:** The cross secured the victory; the return consummates it. Oscar Cullmann's illustration applies: D-Day (Christ's death and resurrection) decided the war; V-Day (His return) ends it. Between D-Day and V-Day, the enemy is defeated but still active. Present healings are genuine incursions of the coming kingdom into this present age. But the groaning is real, the waiting is real, and the full removal of the curse is eschatological.

## The Interpretive Question: How Big Is the Redemption?

The curse-redemption arc is a genuine biblical thread, not an invention. The *choliy* connection between Deuteronomy 28:61 and Isaiah 53:4 is a real linguistic link. The Adam-Christ parallel in Romans 5 and 1 Corinthians 15 is genuinely comprehensive. Galatians 3:13 uses "the curse of the law" in a passage that includes Deuteronomy. And Revelation 22:3 closes the arc that Genesis 3 opens.

The question this thread raises is one of the deepest in the entire study: **How big is the redemption?** Does "Christ hath redeemed us from the curse of the law" include the disease-curses of Deuteronomy 28, or is it limited to the condemnation for failing to keep the law? Does the Adam-Christ parallel demand that everything Adam broke, Christ fixes now --- or does the fixing unfold across the already and the not yet?

Both readings are exegetically defensible. The narrow reading fits Paul's immediate argument in Galatians 3. The broad reading fits the comprehensive scope of the curse in Deuteronomy 28, the comprehensive parallel in Romans 5, and the sweeping language of Revelation 22:3. The reader must weigh the evidence.

One final observation: The fact that the cross is the pivot between curse and no-more-curse is not debated by any view. What is debated is how much of the curse's reversal is available between the cross and the consummation. On that question, faithful interpreters stand on different ground --- and this study trusts the reader to decide where they stand.
