Matthew 8:17 is the only place in the New Testament where Isaiah 53:4 is explicitly quoted -- and Matthew applies it directly to physical healing. This makes it one of the most important passages in the entire healing-in-the-atonement discussion. Whatever one concludes about Isaiah 53:5, one must reckon with what Matthew does with Isaiah 53:4.

## The Text in Context (KJV)

Matthew places his quotation of Isaiah within a sequence of healing narratives. The immediate context is the healing of Peter's mother-in-law, followed by an evening of mass healings:

> **Matthew 8:14-17 (KJV)**
>
> <sup>14</sup> And when Jesus was come into Peter's house, he saw his wife's mother laid, and sick of a fever. <sup>15</sup> And he touched her hand, and the fever left her: and she arose, and ministered unto them. <sup>16</sup> When the even was come, they brought unto him many that were possessed with devils: and he cast out the spirits with his word, and healed all that were sick: <sup>17</sup> That it might be fulfilled which was spoken by Esaias the prophet, saying, Himself took our infirmities, and bare our sicknesses.

Several things are immediately striking. First, the context is entirely physical. Fever, demon possession, sickness -- these are bodily afflictions, and Jesus addresses them with physical acts of healing. Second, Matthew introduces his quotation with his characteristic fulfillment formula: "that it might be fulfilled which was spoken by Esaias the prophet." Third, the words Matthew chooses -- "infirmities" and "sicknesses" -- are unmistakably physical in their reference.

### Comparison Translations

> **Matthew 8:16-17 (ESV):** That evening they brought to him many who were oppressed by demons, and he cast out the spirits with a word and healed all who were sick. This was to fulfill what was spoken by the prophet Isaiah: "He took our illnesses and bore our diseases."

> **Matthew 8:16-17 (NASB):** When evening came, they brought to Him many who were demon-possessed; and He cast out the spirits with a word, and healed all who were ill. This was to fulfill what was spoken through Isaiah the prophet: "He Himself took our infirmities and carried away our diseases."

> **Matthew 8:16-17 (LSB):** And when evening came, they brought to Him many who were demon-possessed; and He cast out the spirits with a word, and healed all who were sick, so that what was spoken through Isaiah the prophet would be fulfilled: "He Himself took our infirmities and carried away our diseases."

Every major English translation renders the quotation in terms of physical illness, not sin. This is consistent across versions, and it reflects the underlying Greek.

## The Greek Text and Its Significance

### Matthew's Greek

The Greek text of Matthew 8:17b reads:

> Αὐτὸς τὰς ἀσθενείας ἡμῶν ἔλαβεν καὶ τὰς νόσους ἐβάστασεν.
>
> *Autos tas astheneias hēmōn elaben kai tas nosous ebastasen.*
>
> "He himself took our weaknesses and bore our diseases."

### Key Greek Terms

**ἀσθένεια (astheneia)** -- "weakness, sickness, infirmity." This word is used throughout the New Testament for physical ailments (Luke 5:15, 8:2; John 5:5, 11:4; Acts 28:9). While it can refer to weakness more broadly, Matthew's context here -- a series of physical healings -- anchors the meaning firmly in bodily infirmity.

**νόσος (nosos)** -- "disease, sickness." This word is exclusively used for physical disease in the New Testament (Matt 4:23, 4:24, 9:35, 10:1; Mark 1:34; Luke 4:40, 6:17, 7:21, 9:1; Acts 19:12). There is no instance in the NT where *nosos* refers to spiritual or moral conditions. Matthew's choice of this word is significant: he is describing physical sickness, not sin.

**λαμβάνω (lambanō)** -- "to take, to take up, to receive." Used here in the aorist (ἔλαβεν, *elaben*), indicating a completed action. Jesus "took" these infirmities.

**βαστάζω (bastazō)** -- "to bear, to carry, to remove." Used here in the aorist (ἐβάστασεν, *ebastasen*). This word carries the sense of lifting a burden. In the context of disease, it can mean either "to carry/endure" or "to carry away/remove." Notably, when *bastazō* appears alongside words for disease in broader Greek usage, it frequently carries the sense of removal rather than sympathetic bearing.

### How Matthew Differs from the LXX

This is one of the most significant textual observations in the entire discussion. When Matthew quotes Isaiah 53:4, he does **not** follow the Septuagint (LXX) translation. The LXX renders Isaiah 53:4 as:

> οὗτος τὰς ἁμαρτίας ἡμῶν φέρει καὶ περὶ ἡμῶν ὀδυνᾶται
>
> *Houtos tas hamartias hēmōn pherei kai peri hēmōn odynatai*
>
> "This one bears our **sins** and suffers pain for us."

The LXX translates the Hebrew *choli* ("sickness") as *hamartias* ("sins") -- a striking interpretive move that shifts the meaning from physical sickness to moral transgression. The LXX translators apparently understood Isaiah 53:4 as referring to sin-bearing rather than sickness-bearing.

Matthew, however, **rejects the LXX rendering** and produces his own translation that stays much closer to the Hebrew Masoretic Text:

| Element | Hebrew (MT) | LXX | Matthew |
|---|---|---|---|
| "Our sicknesses" | חֳלָיֵנוּ (*cholayenu*) -- sicknesses | ἁμαρτίας ἡμῶν -- our **sins** | ἀσθενείας ἡμῶν -- our **weaknesses/infirmities** |
| "He bore" | נָשָׂא (*nasa*) -- he bore/carried | φέρει -- he bears | ἔλαβεν -- he took |
| "Our pains" | מַכְאֹבֵינוּ (*makhovenu*) -- our pains | περὶ ἡμῶν ὀδυνᾶται -- he suffers for us | νόσους -- **diseases** |
| "Carried them" | סְבָלָם (*sevalam*) -- carried them | (included in ὀδυνᾶται) | ἐβάστασεν -- he bore/carried |

Matthew's translation preserves the physical-sickness language of the Hebrew original. Where the LXX spiritualized the passage (changing "sicknesses" to "sins"), Matthew keeps it firmly in the realm of bodily ailment. This appears to be a deliberate choice: Matthew had the LXX available but chose to translate directly from the Hebrew in a way that fit his narrative context -- Jesus physically healing the sick.

This observation is significant regardless of one's theological conclusions. It tells us that Matthew understood Isaiah 53:4 as speaking about physical sickness, and he applied it to Jesus's healing ministry as a direct fulfillment.

## The Timing Question

A detail easily overlooked but critical to the debate: Matthew 8 takes place **before the crucifixion**. Jesus has not yet gone to the cross. He is healing during His earthly ministry, not through His atoning death. This raises an important question: If Matthew connects Isaiah 53:4 to pre-cross healings, what does this tell us about the relationship between healing and the atonement?

Those who see healing as a provision of the atonement must explain how Jesus could "fulfill" Isaiah 53:4 before the atonement occurred. Those who deny healing in the atonement must explain why Matthew uses his fulfillment formula -- the same formula he uses for messianic prophecy -- to connect Isaiah 53 to physical healing.

### The Forgiveness Parallel

However, those who hold healing in the atonement raise a pointed counter-question: **the same timing problem applies to forgiveness, and no one finds it troubling there.**

Consider: David committed adultery and murder, and the prophet Nathan told him, "The LORD also hath put away thy sin; thou shalt not die" (2 Samuel 12:13, KJV). David was forgiven --- before the cross. Abraham "believed in the LORD; and he counted it to him for righteousness" (Genesis 15:6, KJV) --- before the cross. Every Old Testament saint who received forgiveness through the sacrificial system was forgiven before the atonement occurred.

How? Paul explains it directly:

> "Whom God hath set forth to be a propitiation through faith in his blood, to declare his righteousness for the remission of sins that are past, through the forbearance of God."
> --- Romans 3:25 (KJV)

God "passed over" former sins because the atonement, though future in time, was certain in God's purposes. The cross reaches backward as well as forward. The writer of Hebrews says Christ died "for the redemption of the transgressions that were under the first testament" (Hebrews 9:15, KJV). Revelation calls him "the Lamb slain from the foundation of the world" (Revelation 13:8, KJV). The atonement is not confined to its moment in history; its power operates across the whole biblical story.

If forgiveness before the cross was grounded in the future atonement, the healing-in-atonement advocate asks, why can't healing before the cross be grounded in the same way? If the timing objection invalidates pre-cross healing as evidence for the atonement's scope, it should equally invalidate pre-cross forgiveness --- and no one is willing to follow that logic.

### Old Testament Healings Through God's Power

The parallel extends further. The prophets performed physical healings long before the cross:

- Elijah raised the widow's son from death (1 Kings 17:17--24)
- Elisha healed Naaman's leprosy (2 Kings 5:1--14)
- Elisha raised the Shunammite's son (2 Kings 4:32--37)
- Moses lifted up the bronze serpent in the wilderness, and those who looked upon it were healed of their snake bites (Numbers 21:8--9)

The bronze serpent is especially significant because Jesus himself connects it to the cross: "And as Moses lifted up the serpent in the wilderness, even so must the Son of man be lifted up" (John 3:14, KJV). Jesus draws a direct line from an Old Testament physical healing to his crucifixion. If the bronze serpent --- a type of the cross --- healed physical illness, the suggestion that physical healing is connected to the atonement has a typological precedent from Jesus's own teaching.

All of these healings were accomplished by God's power, through the Spirit, before the atonement occurred. If they were possible by God's sovereign grace in anticipation of the cross, then the pre-cross healings in Matthew 8 can be understood the same way --- as the Servant doing what the atonement will fully accomplish, applied proleptically by the same God who forgave David and justified Abraham before Calvary.

### The Counter-Argument

Those who hold the spiritual-only or typological views respond that the parallel is not exact. Forgiveness in the Old Testament was always grounded in sacrifice --- the Levitical system pointed forward to the cross. There was a mechanism (blood sacrifice) that connected pre-cross forgiveness to the future atonement. No comparable mechanism connects pre-cross healing to the atonement. The healings were acts of God's sovereign power, but they were not presented as flowing from a sacrificial provision.

This is a fair point. But the healing-in-atonement advocate replies that the bronze serpent *was* a mechanism --- a type that Jesus himself connected to the cross --- and that the healings performed "in the name of Jesus" (Acts 3:6, 16) in the post-cross era suggest a similar grounding in Christ's redemptive work.

The timing question does not settle the debate, but the forgiveness parallel significantly weakens its force as an objection against healing in the atonement.

## The Interpretive Debate: What Does "Fulfilled" Mean?

The hinge of the entire passage is the word **πληρόω** (*plēroō*), "to fulfill." Matthew uses this word fifteen times in his Gospel (1:22; 2:15, 2:17, 2:23; 3:15; 4:14; 5:17; 8:17; 12:17; 13:35; 21:4; 23:32; 26:54, 56), and his use of it is more complex than simple prediction-and-fulfillment. Understanding how Matthew uses *plēroō* elsewhere is essential to understanding what he means in 8:17.

Four major interpretive positions have emerged. Each has serious scholarly support and deserves careful consideration.

### View 1: Full Atonement Provision

**The argument:** Matthew is declaring that physical healing is part of what Isaiah prophesied the Servant would accomplish. Jesus's healing ministry flows from the same redemptive work that culminates at the cross. The healings in Matthew 8 are not merely signs of who Jesus is -- they are down payments on what the atonement secures. Just as forgiveness of sins is a provision of the cross, so is physical healing. Matthew's use of the fulfillment formula places healing on the same plane as other messianic accomplishments.

**Strongest evidence for this view:**
- Matthew uses his standard fulfillment formula (*hina/hopōs plērōthē*), the same construction he uses for unambiguous messianic fulfillments (Matt 1:22, 2:15, 4:14, 12:17, 21:4). If healing were not a genuine dimension of the Servant's work, why would Matthew invoke this formula?
- Matthew deliberately chose physical-sickness language over the LXX's sin language. He wanted his readers to understand that Isaiah 53:4 speaks about bodily ailment, not (only) spiritual condition.
- Isaiah 53:4-5 forms a unified passage. If verse 5 ("with his stripes we are healed") is about the atonement, then verse 4 ("he hath borne our griefs and carried our sorrows") is about the same redemptive work. Matthew's application of verse 4 to physical healing supports reading verse 5's "healed" as including the physical.

**Advocates of this view include:** Andrew Murray (*Divine Healing*, 1884), A.B. Simpson (*The Gospel of Healing*, 1915), and much of the Pentecostal and charismatic tradition.

### View 2: Pattern of Fulfillment (Typological)

**The argument:** Matthew uses "fulfilled" in a richer, more complex sense than simple prediction-and-completion. Throughout his Gospel, Matthew sees patterns in Israel's history that are "filled up" in Jesus. For example:
- In Matthew 2:15, he quotes Hosea 11:1 ("Out of Egypt have I called my son") -- but Hosea was describing Israel's exodus, not predicting the Messiah. Matthew sees Jesus as the true Israel, replaying and completing Israel's story.
- In Matthew 2:17-18, he quotes Jeremiah 31:15 (Rachel weeping for her children) -- Jeremiah was describing the Babylonian exile, not predicting Herod's slaughter. Matthew sees a pattern being "filled" again.

In the same way, Matthew sees Jesus's healings as "filling up" the pattern described in Isaiah 53:4. The Servant Song describes one who enters into human suffering and bears it -- and Jesus does precisely this in His healing ministry. But this does not necessarily mean that physical healing is a permanent provision of the atonement in the same way that forgiveness is. It means Jesus's healings are one dimension of His identity as the Servant of Isaiah 53.

**Strongest evidence for this view:**
- Matthew's other uses of *plēroō* demonstrate that "fulfillment" is not always direct prediction. If Matthew can say Hosea 11:1 is "fulfilled" in Jesus without meaning Hosea predicted the flight to Egypt, then he can say Isaiah 53:4 is "fulfilled" in Jesus's healings without meaning healing is a permanent atonement provision.
- The timing problem (pre-cross fulfillment) is more naturally explained by this view. Jesus is fulfilling the Servant pattern throughout His ministry, not just at the cross.
- This view takes Matthew's literary and theological method seriously without denying that the healings were real or significant.

**Advocates of this view include:** D.A. Carson (*Matthew*, Expositor's Bible Commentary), R.T. France (*The Gospel of Matthew*, NICNT), and many evangelical commentators.

### View 3: Prophetic Demonstration (Authenticating Sign)

**The argument:** Matthew is showing that Jesus's healings authenticate Him as the Servant described in Isaiah 53. The healing ministry demonstrates *who Jesus is* -- the one about whom Isaiah prophesied -- rather than defining *what the atonement provides*. The miracles are messianic credentials. They prove that Jesus is indeed the Suffering Servant, and they point forward to the cross, but they do not establish healing as a covenant right for believers in the same way that forgiveness is.

**Strongest evidence for this view:**
- The broader structure of Matthew 8-9 presents a series of miracles that demonstrate Jesus's authority over disease (8:1-17), nature (8:23-27), demons (8:28-34), sin (9:1-8), and death (9:18-26). The fulfillment citation in 8:17 functions as a summary statement identifying the healer as Isaiah's Servant.
- John the Baptist's question from prison ("Art thou he that should come?" Matt 11:2-6) is answered by pointing to healing miracles. The healings identify Jesus, not necessarily the permanent scope of atonement benefits.
- Acts 2:22 describes Jesus as "a man approved of God among you by miracles and wonders and signs" -- the miracles authenticate the person.

**Advocates of this view include:** B.B. Warfield (*Counterfeit Miracles*, 1918), many in the cessationist Reformed tradition, and some dispensational scholars.

### View 4: Already-Inaugurated Fulfillment

**The argument:** Jesus's healing ministry in Matthew 8 is a genuine foretaste of the full redemption that the cross will accomplish -- including eventual bodily resurrection and restoration. Healing is "in the atonement" in the same way that the resurrection of the body is in the atonement: it is secured by Christ's work, but its full realization awaits the consummation. The healings in Matthew 8 are inaugural signs of the kingdom -- real, physical, and grounded in the Servant's redemptive mission -- but not yet the final eradication of sickness that will come at the resurrection (Rom 8:22-23; 1 Cor 15:42-44; Rev 21:4).

**Strongest evidence for this view:**
- It takes Matthew's fulfillment language seriously (the healings are genuine fulfillment, not merely illustrative) while also accounting for the New Testament's clear teaching that believers still get sick and die (2 Cor 12:7-10; 2 Tim 4:20; Phil 2:25-27).
- It fits the broader "already/not yet" framework of New Testament eschatology. The kingdom is present (already) but not consummated (not yet). Salvation is received now but glorification is future. In the same way, healing is available now (partially, by God's sovereign will) but guaranteed fully only at the resurrection.
- It explains the pre-cross timing naturally: the healings are inaugural acts of the same redemptive mission that will reach its climax at the cross and its completion at the second coming.

**Advocates of this view include:** Wayne Grudem (*Systematic Theology*), Gordon Fee (*The Disease of the Health and Wealth Gospels*), and many evangelical scholars who affirm both divine healing and the reality of ongoing suffering.

## What Is Not in Dispute

Across all four views, several points are widely agreed upon:

1. **Matthew understood Isaiah 53:4 as referring to physical sickness.** His deliberate departure from the LXX's "sins" language and his choice of *astheneia* and *nosos* make this clear.
2. **Jesus did physically heal people.** The healings in Matthew 8 were real, bodily, and complete. This is not disputed by any serious interpreter.
3. **Matthew saw a genuine connection between Jesus's healings and Isaiah 53.** He invoked his fulfillment formula -- he did not treat the connection as incidental or illustrative.
4. **The scope and permanence of that connection is the question.** Does Matthew's application mean healing is a permanent atonement provision? A typological pattern? An authenticating sign? An inaugural foretaste? This is where faithful interpreters disagree.
