If Matthew 8:17 gives us the clearest New Testament application of Isaiah 53 to *physical* healing, then 1 Peter 2:24 gives us the clearest application to *spiritual* healing. Peter quotes directly from Isaiah 53:5 -- the very verse at the center of this study -- and places it in a context that is unmistakably about moral and spiritual transformation. But does Peter's application *settle* the meaning of Isaiah 53:5, or does it represent one dimension of a passage with broader scope?

This is one of the most consequential interpretive questions in the entire discussion.

## The Text in Context (KJV)

The surrounding passage is essential. Peter is writing to persecuted believers about following Christ's example in suffering, and he weaves multiple phrases from Isaiah 53 into a sustained meditation on the cross:

> **1 Peter 2:21-25 (KJV)**
>
> <sup>21</sup> For even hereunto were ye called: because Christ also suffered for us, leaving us an example, that ye should follow his steps: <sup>22</sup> Who did no sin, neither was guile found in his mouth: <sup>23</sup> Who, when he was reviled, reviled not again; when he suffered, he threatened not; but committed himself to him that judgeth righteously: <sup>24</sup> Who his own self bare our sins in his own body on the tree, that we, being dead to sins, should live unto righteousness: by whose stripes ye were healed. <sup>25</sup> For ye were as sheep going astray; but are now returned unto the Shepherd and Bishop of your souls.

Peter's passage is saturated with Isaiah 53. Notice the echoes:

| 1 Peter 2 | Isaiah 53 (KJV) |
|---|---|
| "Who did no sin" (v. 22) | "he had done no violence" (Isa 53:9) |
| "neither was guile found in his mouth" (v. 22) | "neither was any deceit in his mouth" (Isa 53:9) |
| "bare our sins in his own body" (v. 24) | "he bare the sin of many" (Isa 53:12) |
| "by whose stripes ye were healed" (v. 24) | "with his stripes we are healed" (Isa 53:5) |
| "ye were as sheep going astray" (v. 25) | "All we like sheep have gone astray" (Isa 53:6) |

Peter is not casually alluding to Isaiah 53. He is immersed in it. He draws from verses 5, 6, 9, and 12 -- spanning the entire Servant Song. His application of "by his stripes we are healed" comes within this broader meditation on Christ's substitutionary suffering for sin.

### Comparison Translations

> **1 Peter 2:24 (ESV):** He himself bore our sins in his body on the tree, that we might die to sin and live to righteousness. By his wounds you have been healed.

> **1 Peter 2:24 (NASB):** and He Himself bore our sins in His body on the cross, so that we might die to sin and live to righteousness; for by His wounds you were healed.

> **1 Peter 2:24 (LSB):** and He Himself bore our sins in His body on the tree, so that we might die to sin and live to righteousness, for by His wounds you were healed.

> **1 Peter 2:24 (NIV):** "He himself bore our sins" in his body on the cross, so that we might die to sins and live for righteousness; "by his wounds you have been healed."

Note that the NIV places the Isaiah quotations in quotation marks, making the allusion explicit. All translations agree on the core rendering: the healing is described in the past tense -- "ye *were* healed" -- and the surrounding context addresses sin and righteousness.

## The Greek Text

### Peter's Greek

The key phrase in 1 Peter 2:24 reads:

> οὗ τῷ μώλωπι ἰάθητε
>
> *hou tō mōlōpi iathēte*
>
> "by whose stripe/wound ye were healed"

### Key Greek Terms

**μώλωψ (mōlōps)** -- "stripe, wound, bruise, weal." This word appears only here in the entire New Testament. It refers to a wound that leaves a mark -- a bruise or welt from a blow. The LXX uses the same word in Isaiah 53:5 to translate the Hebrew חַבּוּרָה (*chaburah*), meaning a wound or bruise caused by a beating. Peter's use of the singular *mōlōpi* (dative singular) -- "by his *stripe*" -- may emphasize the unity of Christ's suffering as a single redemptive act, or it may simply follow the LXX rendering. The KJV translates this as "stripes" (plural) in keeping with the underlying Hebrew and the broader image of a beating, while the Greek here is technically singular.

**ἰάομαι (iaomai)** -- "to heal, to cure, to make whole." Peter uses the aorist passive indicative: ἰάθητε (*iathēte*), "ye were healed." This verb is significant for the discussion because *iaomai* is used predominantly for physical healing in the New Testament (Matt 8:8, 8:13, 15:28; Mark 5:29; Luke 5:17, 6:19, 8:47, 9:2, 9:42, 14:4, 17:15, 22:51; John 4:47, 5:13; Acts 9:34, 10:38, 28:8). However, it is also used for spiritual healing or restoration in some contexts (Matt 13:15, quoting Isaiah 6:10; Heb 12:13; and arguably here in 1 Peter 2:24).

The tense is important: the aorist ("ye *were* healed") points to a completed past action. Peter looks back to the cross as the decisive moment when this healing was accomplished. Whatever the healing is -- spiritual, physical, or both -- Peter treats it as already secured by Christ's suffering.

**ἁμαρτία (hamartia)** -- "sin." Peter writes that Christ "bare our *sins* in his own body on the tree." The word for what Christ bore is unambiguously *sins* (ἁμαρτίας, *hamartias*), not sicknesses. This is the language of substitutionary atonement for moral guilt, not physical illness.

### Peter's Context vs. Matthew's Context

The contrast with Matthew 8:17 is striking and instructive:

| | Matthew 8:17 | 1 Peter 2:24 |
|---|---|---|
| **Quotes** | Isaiah 53:4 | Isaiah 53:5 |
| **What Christ bears** | ἀσθενείας / νόσους (infirmities / diseases) | ἁμαρτίας (sins) |
| **Context** | Physical healings | Suffering for righteousness |
| **Application** | Bodily healing | Spiritual transformation |
| **Tense** | Past/completed (during ministry) | Past/completed (at the cross) |

Both authors are inspired. Both are quoting Isaiah 53. Both use fulfillment language. Yet they emphasize very different dimensions of the Servant's work. This dual application is itself a significant piece of evidence in the discussion.

## Peter's Immediate Context: Spiritual Transformation

The immediate context of 1 Peter 2:24 is unambiguously about spiritual and moral change. Peter's argument in verses 21-25 follows this structure:

1. **Christ suffered for us** (v. 21) -- establishing the pattern
2. **Christ was sinless** (v. 22) -- establishing His qualifications
3. **Christ endured without retaliation** (v. 23) -- establishing the example
4. **Christ bore our sins** (v. 24a) -- establishing the substitution
5. **Purpose: that we die to sins and live to righteousness** (v. 24b) -- establishing the result
6. **By His stripes ye were healed** (v. 24c) -- describing the transformation
7. **Ye were as sheep going astray but have returned** (v. 25) -- describing the restoration

The "healing" in verse 24 is bracketed on both sides by sin language. Before it: "bare our sins...dead to sins...live unto righteousness." After it: "ye were as sheep going astray...returned unto the Shepherd and Bishop of your souls." Peter's concern in this passage is the believer's deliverance from sin and restoration to God -- not physical health.

This much is agreed upon by virtually all interpreters. The question is what follows from it.

## The Interpretive Debate

### View 1: Peter Settles the Question

**The argument:** Peter, writing under the inspiration of the Holy Spirit, applies "by his stripes we are healed" to spiritual healing -- deliverance from sin and restoration to righteousness. This is the authoritative, inspired interpretation of Isaiah 53:5. Since Peter tells us what "healed" means in Isaiah 53:5, we should not go beyond what the Spirit-guided apostle has said. Physical healing may be a gift God gives, but it is not what Isaiah 53:5 promises. The verse is about the healing of the soul, not the body.

**Strongest evidence for this view:**
- Peter's context is entirely about sin and righteousness. There is no mention of physical illness in 1 Peter 2:21-25. The "healing" is the healing of those who "were as sheep going astray" -- a spiritual metaphor drawn from Isaiah 53:6.
- Peter explicitly says Christ bore "our *sins*" (not our sicknesses) on the tree. The object of the atonement in Peter's application is sin, not disease.
- The New Testament pattern of apostolic interpretation should guide our reading of the Old Testament. When an inspired author tells us what a passage means, that settles the matter (compare how Hebrews 10:1-18 authoritatively interprets Old Testament sacrifice).
- If physical healing were in view, Peter's flow of thought -- from suffering → sinlessness → substitution → spiritual transformation → healing → restoration from wandering -- would have an unexplained intrusion of a physical-health promise in the middle of an entirely spiritual argument.

**Advocates of this view include:** B.B. Warfield, many Reformed scholars, and those in traditions that distinguish between the atonement's provisions (forgiveness) and God's general gifts (healing).

### View 2: Peter Applies but Does Not Limit

**The argument:** Peter applies Isaiah 53:5 to spiritual healing in this specific context, but that does not exhaust the meaning of Isaiah's words. New Testament authors regularly apply Old Testament texts to specific situations without claiming to have uncovered the only valid meaning. Peter's use of Isaiah 53:5 for spiritual healing no more limits the passage to *only* spiritual healing than Paul's use of Deuteronomy 25:4 ("Thou shalt not muzzle the ox that treadeth out the corn") for ministerial support limits that passage to *only* ministerial support (1 Cor 9:9-10). An application is not the same as an exhaustive definition.

**Strongest evidence for this view:**
- New Testament authors frequently apply Old Testament passages in ways that do not exhaust their original meaning. Matthew applies Isaiah 53:4 to physical healing; Peter applies Isaiah 53:5 to spiritual healing. Both are inspired, and both are drawing out different dimensions of the same passage. If Matthew's application does not limit Isaiah 53:4 to *only* physical healing, then Peter's application does not limit Isaiah 53:5 to *only* spiritual healing.
- Isaiah 53:4-5 is a single unit in the original Hebrew. Verse 4 speaks of bearing sickness and pain; verse 5 speaks of being wounded for transgressions and healed by stripes. The passage itself encompasses both physical and spiritual dimensions. Peter draws on the spiritual dimension; Matthew draws on the physical dimension. Together, they reveal the full scope of the Servant's work.
- Peter's audience was experiencing physical persecution and suffering (1 Pet 2:18-20, 3:14, 4:12-19). It would not be foreign to his context for "by whose stripes ye were healed" to carry overtones of the whole person's restoration, even if his primary emphasis is spiritual.
- The verb ἰάομαι (*iaomai*) is predominantly a physical-healing word in the New Testament. Peter's use of it may carry physical connotations even within a spiritual argument, suggesting that the healing of the whole person -- body and soul -- is ultimately in view.

**Advocates of this view include:** Andrew Murray, A.B. Simpson, Gordon Fee (in a qualified sense), and many who hold that healing is in the atonement in some form.

### View 3: Spiritual and Physical Together

**The argument:** Peter's context in 1 Peter 2 addresses believers undergoing physical suffering (persecution, beatings, unjust treatment -- see 2:18-20). The "stripes" of Christ are set against the stripes His followers receive. When Peter says "by whose stripes ye were healed," the healing encompasses the whole person. The spiritual dimension (dead to sins, alive to righteousness) is primary, but it is not the whole picture. Peter is describing the total restoration that Christ's suffering accomplishes -- a restoration that includes the spiritual, the moral, and ultimately the physical.

**Strongest evidence for this view:**
- The broader context of 1 Peter addresses physical suffering extensively (2:18-20, servants beaten; 3:14, suffering for righteousness; 4:1, suffering in the flesh; 4:12-13, the fiery trial). Peter is writing to people whose bodies are hurting. "By whose stripes ye were healed" would resonate with their physical condition, not only their spiritual state.
- In the biblical worldview, the human person is a unity of body and soul, not a Greek dualism of body versus spirit. Healing of the "whole person" would naturally include both dimensions. The sharp modern distinction between "spiritual healing" and "physical healing" may impose a division that the biblical authors would not have recognized.
- The word ἰάομαι (*iaomai*) resists being limited to spiritual healing alone. When Peter chose this word (or when the Holy Spirit guided his choice), the physical connotations were present. A first-century reader would hear "healing" as encompassing the body.
- Isaiah 53:4-5, Peter's source text, encompasses both physical and spiritual restoration. Peter's application draws primarily on the spiritual, but the physical dimension remains part of the prophetic passage he is quoting.

**Advocates of this view include:** Many in the Pentecostal and charismatic traditions, and some holistic theologians who emphasize the unity of the human person.

## The Aorist Tense: "Ye Were Healed"

A grammatical detail worth noting: Peter uses the aorist passive ἰάθητε (*iathēte*), "ye *were* healed." This points to a completed, past event -- the cross. The healing, whatever it encompasses, has already been accomplished in Peter's theology. Believers have been healed; they are not waiting to be healed.

This creates an interesting tension in the discussion:

- Those who emphasize **spiritual healing** point out that the aorist fits: when Christ died, our spiritual healing was accomplished. We are already healed from sin's dominion.
- Those who emphasize **physical healing in the atonement** argue that the aorist shows healing was purchased at the cross -- it is a done deal, a completed provision that believers can now appropriate by faith.
- Those who hold the **already/not yet** view note that the aorist describes what Christ accomplished, but the full outworking unfolds progressively -- just as our justification is past tense (Rom 5:1, "we *have* been justified") while our glorification is still future (Rom 8:30, though also expressed in the aorist).

## Peter and Matthew Together: Two Witnesses, Two Dimensions

Perhaps the most instructive way to approach 1 Peter 2:24 is to read it alongside Matthew 8:17. Both are inspired Scripture. Both quote Isaiah 53. Both use the passage to describe what Jesus accomplished. Yet they emphasize different things:

- **Matthew** applies Isaiah 53:4 to physical healing during Jesus's earthly ministry.
- **Peter** applies Isaiah 53:5 to spiritual healing accomplished at the cross.

What does this tell us?

At minimum, it tells us that Isaiah 53 is a rich passage with multiple dimensions -- physical and spiritual, present and future, bodily and moral. Neither Matthew nor Peter claims to give the exhaustive meaning. Together, they reveal a Servant whose redemptive work addresses the whole scope of human brokenness.

How far that work extends into physical healing for believers today -- whether as a guaranteed provision, a gracious gift, or a future hope -- is the question this study continues to explore.
