This is the passage at the heart of the entire study. The phrase "by his stripes we are healed" comes from Isaiah 53:5, and the question of what that healing includes --- spiritual restoration, physical healing, or both --- has been debated by faithful believers for centuries. What follows is a careful examination of the text itself, in its original Hebrew, across major English translations, and in the context of the full Servant Song.

---

## The Full Text (KJV)

The Fourth Servant Song begins at Isaiah 52:13 and runs through 53:12. Here is the complete passage in the King James Version:

> **52:13** Behold, my servant shall deal prudently, he shall be exalted and extolled, and be very high.
>
> **52:14** As many were astonied at thee; his visage was so marred more than any man, and his form more than the sons of men:
>
> **52:15** So shall he sprinkle many nations; the kings shall shut their mouths at him: for that which had not been told them shall they see; and that which they had not heard shall they consider.
>
> **53:1** Who hath believed our report? and to whom is the arm of the LORD revealed?
>
> **53:2** For he shall grow up before him as a tender plant, and as a root out of a dry ground: he hath no form nor comeliness; and when we shall see him, there is no beauty that we should desire him.
>
> **53:3** He is despised and rejected of men; a man of sorrows, and acquainted with grief: and we hid as it were our faces from him; he was despised, and we esteemed him not.
>
> **53:4** Surely he hath borne our griefs, and carried our sorrows: yet we did esteem him stricken, smitten of God, and afflicted.
>
> **53:5** But he was wounded for our transgressions, he was bruised for our iniquities: the chastisement of our peace was upon him; and with his stripes we are healed.
>
> **53:6** All we like sheep have gone astray; we have turned every one to his own way; and the LORD hath laid on him the iniquity of us all.
>
> **53:7** He was oppressed, and he was afflicted, yet he opened not his mouth: he is brought as a lamb to the slaughter, and as a sheep before her shearers is dumb, so he openeth not his mouth.
>
> **53:8** He was taken from prison and from judgment: and who shall declare his generation? for he was cut off out of the land of the living: for the transgression of my people was he stricken.
>
> **53:9** And he made his grave with the wicked, and with the rich in his death; because he had done no violence, neither was any deceit in his mouth.
>
> **53:10** Yet it pleased the LORD to bruise him; he hath put him to grief: when thou shalt make his soul an offering for sin, he shall see his seed, he shall prolong his days, and the pleasure of the LORD shall prosper in his hand.
>
> **53:11** He shall see of the travail of his soul, and shall be satisfied: by his knowledge shall my righteous servant justify many; for he shall bear their iniquities.
>
> **53:12** Therefore will I divide him a portion with the great, and he shall divide the spoil with the strong; because he hath poured out his soul unto death: and he was numbered with the transgressors; and he bare the sin of many, and made intercession for the transgressors.

## The Structure of the Servant Song

The Fourth Servant Song has a recognizable literary structure. Understanding this structure helps locate verses 4--5 within the larger argument:

| Section | Verses | Theme |
|---|---|---|
| **Exaltation announced** | 52:13--15 | The Servant will be exalted --- but first, astonishingly disfigured. Nations and kings will be stunned. |
| **Rejection described** | 53:1--3 | The Servant is despised, rejected, a man of sorrows. No one esteems him. |
| **Substitutionary suffering** | 53:4--6 | The Servant bears *our* griefs, *our* sorrows, *our* transgressions, *our* iniquities. This is vicarious --- he suffers in our place. |
| **Silent submission** | 53:7--9 | The Servant does not resist. Like a lamb to slaughter, he is silent. He dies and is buried. |
| **Vindication and reward** | 53:10--12 | The LORD's purpose prospers through him. He justifies many. He is given a portion with the great. |

The central section --- verses 4--6 --- is where the key claims about healing appear. It sits at the theological heart of the passage, framed by the Servant's rejection (before) and his silent submission to death (after). The surrounding context is dominated by sin, transgression, and iniquity language, which is important for interpretation.

## Verse-by-Verse Analysis: Isaiah 53:4--5

These two verses are the epicenter of the healing-in-the-atonement debate. Every word matters.

### Isaiah 53:4

> "Surely he hath borne our griefs, and carried our sorrows: yet we did esteem him stricken, smitten of God, and afflicted." --- KJV

#### The Hebrew Text

The Hebrew of Isaiah 53:4 reads:

> אָכֵ֤ן חֳלָיֵ֙נוּ֙ ה֣וּא נָשָׂ֔א וּמַכְאֹבֵ֖ינוּ סְבָלָ֑ם וַאֲנַ֣חְנוּ חֲשַׁבְנֻ֔הוּ נָג֛וּעַ מֻכֵּ֥ה אֱלֹהִ֖ים וּמְעֻנֶּֽה

#### Key Words

**חֳלִי (choliy)** --- Strong's H2483

- **KJV translation in this verse:** "griefs"
- **Literal/primary meaning:** sickness, disease, illness
- **Semantic range:** The word derives from the root חָלָה (chalah, H2470, "to be weak, sick"). *BDB* defines it as "sickness, disease." In the KJV, H2483 is translated as "sickness" 12 times, "disease" 7 times, "grief" 4 times, and "sick" once.
- **Physical sickness usage:** Deuteronomy 7:15 --- "And the LORD will take away from thee all sickness [choliy]"; Deuteronomy 28:61 --- "Also every sickness [choliy], and every plague"; 2 Kings 1:2 --- Ahaziah's physical illness; 2 Kings 8:8 --- "Shall I recover of this disease [choliy]?"; 2 Chronicles 21:15 --- "great sickness [choliy] by disease of thy bowels"; Isaiah 38:9 --- Hezekiah's writing "when he had been sick [choliy]"
- **Non-physical usage:** Ecclesiastes 6:2 --- "this is vanity, and it is an evil disease [choliy]"; Hosea 5:13 --- metaphorical sickness of Ephraim and Judah; Jeremiah 10:19 --- "Woe is me for my hurt [choliy]! my wound is grievous"
- **The critical point:** The KJV translates choliy as "griefs" in Isaiah 53:4 --- but the same word overwhelmingly means physical sickness elsewhere in the Old Testament. The NASB (2020) translates it as "sicknesses," and the CSB translates it as "sicknesses." Neither rendering is illegitimate. The Hebrew word genuinely encompasses both physical disease and broader suffering.

**נָשָׂא (nasa)** --- Strong's H5375

- **KJV translation in this verse:** "borne"
- **Meaning:** to lift, to bear, to carry. A common Hebrew verb with a wide range. In this context, it conveys the idea of bearing a heavy load --- taking something upon oneself that belongs to another. The same verb appears in Leviticus 16:22 where the scapegoat "shall bear [nasa] upon him all their iniquities," establishing a clear substitutionary pattern.

**מַכְאוֹב (makob)** --- Strong's H4341

- **KJV translation in this verse:** "sorrows"
- **Literal/primary meaning:** pain, sorrow, anguish
- **Semantic range:** *BDB* defines it as "pain" (physical or mental). The word comes from the root כָּאַב (kaab, H3510, "to be in pain, to suffer"). It can refer to physical pain (Job 33:19 --- "He is chastened also with pain [makob] upon his bed") or emotional anguish (Jeremiah 30:15 --- "Why criest thou for thine affliction? thy sorrow [makob] is incurable"). The NASB (2020) translates it "pains" here; the NIV renders it "suffering."
- **The critical point:** Like choliy, makob can refer to physical pain or to emotional/spiritual anguish. The KJV's "sorrows" leans toward the emotional; the NASB's "pains" leans toward the physical. The Hebrew supports both.

**סָבַל (sabal)** --- Strong's H5445

- **KJV translation in this verse:** "carried"
- **Meaning:** to bear (as a burden), to carry a heavy load. This is a strong verb implying laborious, weighty bearing --- not casual carrying, but the shouldering of a crushing burden. It reinforces the substitutionary image: the Servant does not merely encounter our suffering; he *carries* it as his own burden.

#### What Verse 4 Says

The verse makes a clear claim: the Servant has *borne* and *carried* something that belongs to *us*. The action is substitutionary --- he takes our load upon himself. The question is what exactly that load is. The Hebrew words choliy and makob can refer to:

- Physical sickness and physical pain (their most common usage elsewhere)
- Emotional grief and sorrow (as the KJV translates them here)
- Or both --- a comprehensive bearing of human suffering in all its dimensions

The second half of the verse adds an important note: "yet we did esteem him stricken, smitten of God, and afflicted." The onlookers misread the Servant's suffering. They assumed God was punishing *him* --- when in reality, he was bearing what belonged to *them*.

### Isaiah 53:5

> "But he was wounded for our transgressions, he was bruised for our iniquities: the chastisement of our peace was upon him; and with his stripes we are healed." --- KJV

#### The Hebrew Text

> וְהוּא֙ מְחֹלָ֣ל מִפְּשָׁעֵ֔נוּ מְדֻכָּ֖א מֵעֲוֹנֹתֵ֑ינוּ מוּסַ֤ר שְׁלֹומֵ֙נוּ֙ עָלָ֔יו וּבַחֲבֻרָתֹ֖ו נִרְפָּא־לָֽנוּ

#### Key Words

**מְחֹלָל (mecholal)** --- from חָלַל (chalal), Strong's H2490

- **KJV translation:** "wounded"
- **Meaning:** pierced, pierced through, profaned, wounded. The ESV and NIV translate this as "pierced" --- a stronger, more violent image than "wounded." The word implies a penetrating wound, not a superficial injury.

**פֶּשַׁע (pesha)** --- Strong's H6588

- **KJV translation:** "transgressions"
- **Meaning:** rebellion, transgression, willful violation. This is one of the strongest Hebrew words for sin, emphasizing deliberate revolt against God's authority.

**מְדֻכָּא (medukka)** --- from דָּכָא (daka), Strong's H1792

- **KJV translation:** "bruised"
- **Meaning:** crushed, broken in pieces. The ESV and NIV translate this as "crushed" --- again a more severe image. The Servant is not merely hurt; he is *crushed*.

**עָוֹן (avon)** --- Strong's H5771

- **KJV translation:** "iniquities"
- **Meaning:** iniquity, guilt, punishment for iniquity. This word carries the sense of moral crookedness and its consequences.

**מוּסָר (musar)** --- Strong's H4148

- **KJV translation:** "chastisement"
- **Meaning:** discipline, correction, chastisement, instruction. *BDB* defines it as "discipline, chastening, correction." The word is often used for the discipline a parent gives a child (Proverbs 1:2, 8; 3:11) or the corrective discipline God brings. Here, "the chastisement of our peace" means the punishment that produces our peace/wholeness (Hebrew shalom) was placed on the Servant.

**שָׁלוֹם (shalom)** --- Strong's H7965

- **KJV translation:** "peace"
- **Meaning:** peace, completeness, soundness, welfare, wholeness. Shalom is one of the richest words in Hebrew. It does not mean merely the absence of conflict but encompasses total well-being --- physical, spiritual, relational, material. The chastisement that produces our *shalom* was placed on the Servant. This is significant: shalom is a whole-person word.

**חַבּוּרָה (chaburah)** --- Strong's H2250

- **KJV translation:** "stripes"
- **Meaning:** stripe, wound, bruise, blow-mark. The word refers to the mark or wound left by a blow --- a welt, a stripe on the body. It appears also in Genesis 4:23 ("a young man to my hurt") and Exodus 21:25 ("stripe for stripe"). The ESV and NIV render this "wounds"; the NASB uses "scourging" or "wounds."

**נִרְפָּא (nirpa)** --- from רָפָא (rapha), Strong's H7495

- **KJV translation:** "we are healed"
- **Meaning:** This is the Niphal (passive) perfect of rapha --- "to heal, to cure, to restore, to make whole." The passive voice means "healing has been done to us"; the perfect tense indicates a completed action.
- **Physical healing usage:** The word rapha is used extensively for physical healing in the Old Testament. God heals Abimelech's household (Genesis 20:17). God promises to heal diseases (Exodus 15:26 --- "I am the LORD that healeth [rapha] thee"). God heals Hezekiah's terminal illness (2 Kings 20:5 --- "I will heal [rapha] thee"). Naaman's leprosy is healed (2 Kings 5:3, 6, 7, 10, 11). Waters are healed (2 Kings 2:21--22).
- **Spiritual/metaphorical healing usage:** Rapha also appears in clearly metaphorical contexts. Hosea 6:1 --- "Come, and let us return unto the LORD: for he hath torn, and he will heal [rapha] us." Jeremiah 3:22 --- "Return, ye backsliding children, and I will heal [rapha] your backslidings." Psalm 41:4 --- "LORD, be merciful unto me: heal [rapha] my soul; for I have sinned against thee." Isaiah 6:10 --- "lest they see with their eyes... and convert, and be healed [rapha]."
- **The critical point:** The word rapha is used for *both* physical healing and spiritual restoration throughout the Old Testament. In Isaiah 53:5, the immediate context is dominated by sin-language (transgressions, iniquities), which pushes toward a spiritual/metaphorical reading. But the word itself does not require this limitation, and the preceding verse (53:4) uses words that can refer to physical sickness.

#### What Verse 5 Says

Verse 5 is structured in four parallel lines, each describing the Servant's suffering and its benefit to "us":

| The Servant's suffering | Our benefit |
|---|---|
| He was wounded/pierced | for our transgressions |
| He was bruised/crushed | for our iniquities |
| The chastisement of our peace was upon him | (our peace/shalom is the result) |
| By his stripes | we are healed |

The first two lines are unambiguously about sin: transgressions (pesha) and iniquities (avon). The third line introduces shalom --- wholeness, well-being, completeness. The fourth line uses rapha --- healed.

The question is whether the movement from "transgressions" and "iniquities" in lines 1--2 to "peace/shalom" and "healed/rapha" in lines 3--4 represents:

- A **narrowing** --- all four lines refer to sin and its spiritual consequences (healed = spiritually restored)
- A **broadening** --- the passage moves from the root cause (sin) to the comprehensive result (shalom = total wholeness, including physical)
- A **poetic parallelism** --- all four lines say the same thing in different ways (standard Hebrew poetic technique)

All three readings have competent advocates. The text alone does not settle the question definitively, which is why the debate persists.

### What the Comparison Reveals

Several observations emerge from this comparison:

**1. The biggest divergence is in verse 4, not verse 5.** Every major English translation renders rapha as "healed" in verse 5. The debate is not about what rapha means in isolation --- everyone agrees it means "healed." The debate is about what *kind* of healing the context demands.

**2. Verse 4 is where translations split dramatically.** The KJV, ESV, NASB (1995), and LSB all render choliy as "griefs" --- pushing the reader toward an emotional or spiritual reading. The NASB (2020) and CSB render it "sicknesses" --- pushing the reader toward a physical reading. Neither is a mistranslation; both are within the semantic range of the Hebrew.

**3. The NASB changed its translation between 1995 and 2020.** This is remarkable. The same translation committee, working from the same Hebrew text, moved from "griefs and sorrows" to "sicknesses and pains." This shift reflects a growing recognition that the physical meaning of choliy and makob deserves representation in the English text.

**4. The LXX introduces a different word entirely.** Where the Hebrew has choliy (sickness/grief), the Septuagint has hamartias --- *sins*. This is not a translation of the Hebrew; it is an interpretation. The LXX translators read Isaiah 53:4 as being about sin-bearing, not sickness-bearing. This matters enormously for the New Testament question, as we will see below.

## The LXX Factor: Why the Septuagint Matters

The relationship between the Hebrew Masoretic Text (MT), the Greek Septuagint (LXX), and the New Testament quotations of Isaiah 53 is one of the most important --- and most overlooked --- elements of this debate.

### The LXX Rendering of Isaiah 53:4

The Septuagint translates Isaiah 53:4 as:

> οὗτος τὰς ἁμαρτίας ἡμῶν φέρει καὶ περὶ ἡμῶν ὀδυνᾶται
>
> "This one bears our **sins** and suffers pain for us"

Compare the Hebrew (MT):

> "Surely he has borne our **sicknesses/griefs** and carried our **pains/sorrows**"

The LXX replaces choliy ("sickness/grief") with hamartias ("sins"). This is a significant interpretive move. The LXX translators understood Isaiah 53:4 as being about sin-bearing --- which is certainly part of what the passage teaches --- but in doing so, they removed the physical-sickness dimension that the Hebrew word choliy carries.

### Matthew 8:17 --- A Different Translation

When Matthew quotes Isaiah 53:4, he does *not* quote the LXX. Instead, his Greek reads:

> Αὐτὸς τὰς ἀσθενείας ἡμῶν ἔλαβεν καὶ τὰς νόσους ἐβάστασεν
>
> "He himself took our **infirmities** and bore our **diseases**"

Matthew's key terms:
- **ἀσθενείας (astheneias)** --- weaknesses, infirmities, sicknesses
- **νόσους (nosous)** --- diseases, illnesses

These are explicitly physical-sickness words. Matthew did not use the LXX's hamartias ("sins"). He appears to have translated directly from the Hebrew, and he chose Greek words that emphasize physical illness --- precisely in a context where Jesus is physically healing the sick.

### Why This Matters

The LXX-versus-Matthew divergence creates a tension that runs through the entire debate:

- If you follow the **LXX**, Isaiah 53:4 is about sin-bearing. The physical dimension is absent from the Greek text the early church most commonly read.
- If you follow **Matthew's rendering**, Isaiah 53:4 explicitly includes physical sickness, and Jesus' healing ministry is presented as a fulfillment of this prophecy.
- If you go back to the **Hebrew**, both readings are possible --- the words choliy and makob encompass physical and non-physical suffering.

No responsible interpretation can ignore this three-way textual reality. The reader must weigh all three witnesses: the Hebrew original, the LXX interpretation, and Matthew's application.

## Context: The Fourth Servant Song in Isaiah

### Who Is the Servant?

The identity of the Servant in Isaiah's Servant Songs (42:1--4; 49:1--6; 50:4--9; 52:13--53:12) has been debated for millennia:

- **Messianic reading:** The Servant is an individual --- the coming Messiah who suffers vicariously for his people. This is the dominant Christian reading and was also held by some pre-Christian Jewish interpreters (see Chapter 3 on Antiquity). The New Testament consistently applies Isaiah 53 to Jesus (Matthew 8:17; Acts 8:32--35; 1 Peter 2:24).

- **Corporate Israel reading:** The Servant is the nation of Israel, suffering among the nations. This reading became dominant in Jewish interpretation after the medieval period, particularly through Rashi (c. 1040--1105 AD), though it has earlier roots. In this reading, the "we" of Isaiah 53 are the Gentile nations who witness Israel's suffering.

- **Both/And reading:** Some scholars hold that the Servant Songs operate on multiple levels --- the Servant is both Israel and an individual within Israel who represents and redeems the nation. Isaiah himself may have understood the Servant as Israel, while the passage's fullest meaning is realized in the Messiah.

For the healing question specifically, the identity of the Servant matters: if the Servant is an individual Messiah whose suffering is substitutionary, the question of what his suffering accomplishes (spiritual healing, physical healing, or both) is a natural one. If the Servant is corporate Israel, the "healing" language may refer to national restoration rather than individual healing of any kind.

### The Broader Context of Isaiah 40--55

The Fourth Servant Song sits within "Second Isaiah" (chapters 40--55), a section focused on Israel's comfort, redemption, and restoration after exile. The dominant themes are:

- God's sovereignty over nations and history
- The promise of return from Babylonian exile
- The coming of salvation and restoration
- The Servant as God's agent of redemption

The healing language in 53:5 resonates with Isaiah's broader vision of restoration. In Isaiah 35:5--6, the messianic age is described with physical healing imagery: "Then the eyes of the blind shall be opened, and the ears of the deaf shall be unstopped. Then shall the lame man leap as an hart, and the tongue of the dumb sing." Whether this is literal or metaphorical (or both) is itself debated, but the connection to 53:5 is natural.

## The Interpretive Spectrum

Faithful interpreters have read Isaiah 53:4--5 in four major ways regarding physical healing. Each position is presented here with its strongest arguments.

### View 1: Physical and Spiritual Healing

**The claim:** Isaiah 53:4--5 teaches that the Servant's substitutionary suffering addresses both physical sickness and spiritual sin. Healing of the body is a provision of the atonement, available to believers now by faith.

**Strongest arguments:**

- **The Hebrew words mean physical sickness.** Choliy (H2483) is translated "sickness" or "disease" in the majority of its Old Testament occurrences. Makob (H4341) frequently means physical pain. The KJV's "griefs and sorrows" is a legitimate but less common rendering. When the text says the Servant bore our choliy, the most natural reading --- based on the word's primary usage --- includes physical sickness.

- **Matthew 8:17 applies it to physical healing.** Matthew, writing under the inspiration of the Holy Spirit, quotes Isaiah 53:4 in direct connection with Jesus physically healing the sick: "Himself took our infirmities, and bare our sicknesses" (KJV). Matthew uses the Greek words astheneias ("infirmities") and nosous ("diseases") --- unambiguously physical terms. If the inspired New Testament author applies this verse to physical healing, this is strong evidence that the verse includes physical healing.

- **Rapha covers the whole person.** The Hebrew word rapha is used for physical healing throughout the Old Testament (Exodus 15:26; 2 Kings 20:5; Psalm 103:3). Its use in 53:5 need not be limited to spiritual metaphor, especially when paired with the physical-sickness language of 53:4.

- **Shalom is comprehensive.** The "peace" (shalom) that the Servant's chastisement produces is not merely spiritual tranquility but total well-being --- wholeness of body, mind, and spirit. The atonement addresses the whole person.

**Advocates of this view:** Andrew Murray (*Divine Healing*, 1884), A.B. Simpson (*The Gospel of Healing*, 1915), and much of the Pentecostal and charismatic tradition.

---

### View 2: Primarily Spiritual, with Physical Implications

**The claim:** The primary meaning of Isaiah 53:5 is spiritual healing --- restoration from sin and its consequences. However, because the atonement addresses the whole person, physical healing flows from it as a secondary benefit. Healing is real and available, but it operates within the "already/not yet" framework: believers may experience physical healing now as a foretaste of the coming resurrection, but complete physical wholeness is guaranteed only at the return of Christ.

**Strongest arguments:**

- **The context of verse 5 is dominated by sin-language.** The four parallel lines of 53:5 mention "transgressions," "iniquities," "chastisement," and then "healed." The movement is from sin to its remedy. Reading "healed" as primarily spiritual fits the immediate literary context.

- **The already/not yet framework explains the evidence.** Romans 8:22--23 teaches that believers "groan within ourselves, waiting for the adoption, to wit, the redemption of our body" (KJV). The body is not yet fully redeemed. Physical healing in this age is a genuine but partial experience of the atonement's benefits --- a down payment, not the full inheritance.

- **Matthew 8:17 shows a pattern, not a guarantee.** Matthew's fulfillment citation shows that Jesus' healing ministry demonstrates the Servant's identification with human suffering. It does not necessarily mean every believer can claim physical healing as a present right. The "already/not yet" tension accounts for both the reality of healing and the reality of ongoing suffering.

- **Both physical and spiritual dimensions are honored.** This view does not deny that God heals physically. It affirms healing as part of the atonement's scope while acknowledging that the full realization awaits glorification. It avoids both extremes: denying healing altogether, or guaranteeing it in every case.

**Advocates of this view:** Wayne Grudem and many evangelical scholars who affirm the continuation of healing gifts while recognizing the eschatological tension.

---

### View 3: Spiritual/Metaphorical Healing Only

**The claim:** "Healed" in Isaiah 53:5 is a metaphor for spiritual restoration from sin. The verse is not about physical healing at all. The atonement deals with sin and its spiritual consequences; physical healing, while God certainly can and does grant it, is not a provision of the atonement per se.

**Strongest arguments:**

- **1 Peter 2:24 interprets the healing as spiritual.** Peter quotes Isaiah 53:5 directly: "Who his own self bare our sins in his own body on the tree, that we, being dead to sins, should live unto righteousness: by whose stripes ye were healed" (KJV). Peter's context is entirely about sin and righteousness. He understood "healed" to mean spiritual restoration. If an inspired apostle interprets the verse this way, this is strong evidence for the spiritual reading.

- **The KJV's "griefs and sorrows" is defensible.** While choliy can mean "sickness," it can also mean "grief" --- and the KJV translators, who were excellent Hebraists, chose "griefs." This was not ignorance; it was a considered judgment about the context. The surrounding verses (53:5--6) are about transgressions and iniquities, not diseases.

- **Metaphorical healing is well established in the prophets.** Isaiah himself uses healing language metaphorically. Isaiah 1:5--6 describes Israel's spiritual condition as wounds and bruises. Hosea 6:1 and Jeremiah 3:22 use rapha for spiritual restoration. The prophetic tradition uses disease/healing as a metaphor for sin/forgiveness.

- **Matthew 8:17 quotes verse 4, not verse 5.** Matthew applies Isaiah 53:4 --- "griefs and sorrows" / "sicknesses and pains" --- to physical healing. He does not quote verse 5 ("by his stripes we are healed"). The distinction matters: verse 4 may describe the Servant's compassionate identification with human suffering (fulfilled in Jesus' healing ministry), while verse 5 describes the atonement for sin (a different, though related, aspect of the Servant's work).

**Advocates of this view:** B.B. Warfield (*Counterfeit Miracles*, 1918) and many Reformed and cessationist scholars.

---

### View 4: National Restoration

**The claim:** In its original context, Isaiah 53 describes the restoration of Israel as a nation. The "healing" is the healing of the nation --- not individual physical healing and not even individual spiritual salvation in the New Testament sense. The passage was later applied christologically, but its original referent was corporate.

**Strongest arguments:**

- **The Servant Songs address Israel's national situation.** Isaiah 40--55 is addressed to exiled Israel, promising restoration. The "healing" of 53:5 fits naturally as national restoration after the suffering of exile.

- **"We" is corporate, not individual.** The speakers in Isaiah 53 ("we did esteem him," "our griefs," "our transgressions") are a group reflecting on the Servant's suffering. If the Servant is Israel (or a righteous remnant within Israel), the "healing" is the restoration of the community.

- **Rapha is used for national healing.** Jeremiah 30:17 --- "For I will restore health unto thee, and I will heal [rapha] thee of thy wounds, saith the LORD." Hosea 14:4 --- "I will heal [rapha] their backsliding." These uses of rapha describe the restoration of the nation, not individual physical healing.

- **This was the dominant pre-Christian Jewish reading (in some traditions).** While some ancient Jewish sources read Isaiah 53 messianically (see Chapter 3), the corporate-Israel reading has deep roots and deserves consideration as one way the original audience may have understood the passage.

**Advocates of this view:** Some Jewish interpreters (see Rashi and later tradition) and some critical Old Testament scholars who read Isaiah 53 in its original historical context before considering its New Testament application.

## What Is Clear and What Is Debated

After examining the Hebrew text, the translation history, the LXX divergence, and the full range of interpretive options, we can identify what is settled and what remains genuinely open.

### What is clear

- **Isaiah 53 describes substitutionary, vicarious suffering.** The Servant bears what belongs to others. This is not disputed by any major interpretive tradition.

- **The Hebrew word choliy (H2483) CAN refer to physical sickness.** This is its most common meaning in the Old Testament. No competent Hebrew scholar denies this.

- **The Hebrew word makob (H4341) CAN refer to physical pain.** This is well within its semantic range and is the rendering chosen by several major translations.

- **The Hebrew word rapha (H7495) CAN mean physical healing.** It is used this way extensively in the Old Testament. No one disputes this.

- **Matthew 8:17 applies Isaiah 53:4 to physical healing.** Matthew quotes this verse in direct connection with Jesus healing the physically sick. The Greek words he uses (astheneias, nosous) are unambiguously physical.

- **1 Peter 2:24 applies Isaiah 53:5 to spiritual healing.** Peter quotes this verse in a context focused on sin and righteousness. His application is spiritual.

- **The LXX translates choliy as hamartias ("sins"), not as a word for physical sickness.** Matthew does not follow the LXX here; he uses physical-sickness terminology.

### What is debated

- **Whether the atonement described in Isaiah 53 INCLUDES physical healing as a provision.** The Hebrew permits it; the question is whether the context demands it, suggests it, or excludes it.

- **How to reconcile Matthew 8:17 and 1 Peter 2:24.** Matthew applies Isaiah 53:4 to physical healing; Peter applies Isaiah 53:5 to spiritual healing. Are these complementary applications of a multifaceted passage? Or does Peter's interpretation limit the meaning to spiritual healing?

- **Whether Matthew 8:17 is a "healing-in-atonement" proof text or a "pattern of fulfillment" text.** Matthew says Jesus' healings "fulfilled" Isaiah 53:4. Does "fulfilled" mean the atonement provides healing? Or does it mean Jesus' ministry of compassion fulfills the Servant's role of bearing human suffering --- a role that culminates in, but is not limited to, the cross?

- **Whether choliy and makob in Isaiah 53:4 should be translated with physical-sickness words or emotional-suffering words.** The NASB changed its translation between 1995 and 2020, moving from "griefs/sorrows" to "sicknesses/pains." Both are within the semantic range. The translation choice is itself an interpretation.

- **The scope of shalom in Isaiah 53:5.** If the "chastisement of our peace [shalom]" was upon the Servant, and shalom encompasses total well-being (physical, spiritual, relational), does the atonement address the whole person? Or is shalom being used in a narrower sense here?

## Summary

Isaiah 52:13--53:12 is one of the most profound and most debated passages in all of Scripture. At its center, verses 4--5 describe a Servant who bears the suffering of others and whose wounds bring healing. The Hebrew words behind the English translations are genuinely capable of referring to both physical sickness and spiritual sin, to both bodily healing and spiritual restoration.

The evidence does not permit a simple, one-sentence answer to the question "Does Isaiah 53:5 promise physical healing?" The Hebrew is broader than any single English translation can capture. The New Testament applies the passage in both physical (Matthew 8:17) and spiritual (1 Peter 2:24) directions. The Septuagint offers yet a third perspective.

What we can say with confidence is this: the passage describes real, substitutionary suffering by the Servant, undertaken for the benefit of others, resulting in healing. The nature and scope of that healing --- whether it is spiritual only, spiritual and physical, or comprehensive wholeness --- is where faithful interpreters part ways.

The remaining sections of this chapter examine the New Testament texts that quote Isaiah 53, each of which adds further evidence to this picture.
