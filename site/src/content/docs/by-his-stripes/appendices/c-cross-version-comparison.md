This appendix provides a side-by-side comparison of the four key passages in the healing-in-the-atonement debate across seven major English translations and the Septuagint. For each passage, the comparison is followed by notes on key divergences, translation philosophy, and significance for the debate.

The translations compared are:

| Translation | Date | Philosophy | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **KJV** | 1611 | Formal equivalence | Primary translation for this study |
| **ESV** | 2001 | Essentially literal | Widely used in Reformed and evangelical circles |
| **NASB (1995)** | 1995 | Formal equivalence | Known for strict literalness |
| **NASB (2020)** | 2020 | Formal equivalence (updated) | Same committee; significant changes in Isa 53:4 |
| **LSB** | 2021 | Formal equivalence | Produced by The Master's Seminary; cessationist/Reformed perspective |
| **NIV** | 2011 | Optimal equivalence | Thought-for-thought; the world's most widely read English Bible |
| **NLT** | 2015 | Dynamic equivalence | Prioritizes readability and clarity of thought |
| **LXX** | c. 3rd--1st century BC | Interpretive translation | The Greek OT; critical for understanding NT quotations |

## Passage 1: Isaiah 53:4

> "Surely he hath borne our griefs, and carried our sorrows: yet we did esteem him stricken, smitten of God, and afflicted." --- KJV

### Comparison Table

| Translation | First clause | Second clause | Third clause |
|---|---|---|---|
| **KJV** | Surely he hath borne our **griefs** | and carried our **sorrows** | yet we did esteem him stricken, smitten of God, and afflicted |
| **ESV** | Surely he has borne our **griefs** | and carried our **sorrows** | yet we esteemed him stricken, smitten by God, and afflicted |
| **NASB (1995)** | Surely our **griefs** He Himself bore | and our **sorrows** He carried | yet we ourselves esteemed Him stricken, smitten of God, and afflicted |
| **NASB (2020)** | However, it was our **sicknesses** that He Himself bore | and our **pains** that He carried | yet we ourselves assumed that He had been afflicted, struck down by God, and humiliated |
| **LSB** | Surely our **griefs** He Himself bore | and our **sorrows** He carried | yet we ourselves esteemed Him stricken, struck down by God, and afflicted |
| **NIV** | Surely he took up our **pain** | and bore our **suffering** | yet we considered him punished by God, stricken by him, and afflicted |
| **NLT** | Yet it was our **weaknesses** he carried; it was our **sorrows** that weighed him down | | And we thought his troubles were a punishment from God, a punishment for his own sins! |
| **LXX** | This one bears our **sins** (ἁμαρτίας) | and suffers pain (ὀδυνᾶται) for us | yet we supposed him to be in toil and in affliction and in ill-treatment |

### Key Divergences

**1. The critical split: "griefs/sorrows" vs. "sicknesses/pains."** The Hebrew words choli and makob can mean either "sicknesses/pains" (their primary OT usage) or "griefs/sorrows" (a secondary meaning). The KJV, ESV, NASB (1995), and LSB all render these as "griefs/sorrows." The NASB (2020) renders them as "sicknesses/pains." The NIV uses "pain/suffering" --- a middle position. The NLT uses "weaknesses/sorrows."

**2. The NASB changed its translation between 1995 and 2020.** This is one of the most significant translation shifts in modern Bible publishing. The same translation committee, working from the same Hebrew text, moved from "griefs and sorrows" to "sicknesses and pains." This reflects a growing scholarly consensus that choli and makob should be rendered with their primary meanings. The change does not represent a theological agenda; it represents a textual judgment about the best rendering of the Hebrew.

**3. The LXX introduces an entirely different word.** Where the Hebrew has choli ("sickness/grief"), the Septuagint has hamartias ("sins"). This is not a translation; it is an interpretation. The LXX translators read Isaiah 53:4 as being about sin-bearing. Matthew 8:17 explicitly rejects this rendering, choosing astheneia ("infirmity") and nosos ("disease") instead.

**4. The LSB retains "griefs/sorrows."** The Legacy Standard Bible, produced by The Master's Seminary under the leadership of John MacArthur, retains the KJV/NASB (1995) rendering. This is consistent with a cessationist/Reformed hermeneutic that reads Isaiah 53:4 primarily in terms of spiritual suffering rather than physical sickness. The LSB's rendering is a legitimate translation choice, but it is worth noting that it diverges from the NASB (2020) --- the very translation the LSB was built upon.

## Passage 2: Isaiah 53:5

> "But he was wounded for our transgressions, he was bruised for our iniquities: the chastisement of our peace was upon him; and with his stripes we are healed." --- KJV

### Comparison Table

| Translation | Line 1 | Line 2 | Line 3 | Line 4 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **KJV** | he was **wounded** for our transgressions | he was **bruised** for our iniquities | the chastisement of our **peace** was upon him | with his **stripes** we are **healed** |
| **ESV** | he was **pierced** for our transgressions | he was **crushed** for our iniquities | upon him was the chastisement that brought us **peace** | with his **wounds** we are **healed** |
| **NASB (1995)** | He was **pierced through** for our transgressions | He was **crushed** for our iniquities | the chastening for our **well-being** fell upon Him | by His **scourging** we are **healed** |
| **NASB (2020)** | He was **pierced through** for our transgressions | He was **crushed** for our iniquities | the punishment for our **well-being** was on Him | by His **wounds** we are **healed** |
| **LSB** | He was **pierced through** for our transgressions | He was **crushed** for our iniquities | the chastening for our **well-being** fell upon Him | by His **wounds** we are **healed** |
| **NIV** | he was **pierced** for our transgressions | he was **crushed** for our iniquities | the punishment that brought us **peace** was on him | by his **wounds** we are **healed** |
| **NLT** | he was **pierced** for our rebellion | **crushed** for our sins | He was **beaten** so we could be **whole** | He was **whipped** so we could be **healed** |
| **LXX** | he was **wounded** (ἐτραυματίσθη) for our sins | was **weakened** (μεμαλάκισται) for our sins | the discipline of our **peace** (εἰρήνης) was upon him | by his **wound** (μώλωπι) we were **healed** (ἰάθημεν) |

### Key Observations

**1. All translations agree on "healed."** Every version --- KJV, ESV, NASB, LSB, NIV, NLT, and LXX --- renders rapha/iaomai as "healed." There is no disagreement about the translation of this word. The debate is entirely about the *scope* of the healing, not the word itself.

**2. "Wounded" vs. "pierced."** The KJV renders mecholal as "wounded"; modern translations prefer "pierced" or "pierced through." The modern rendering is more vivid and violent, evoking the specific wounds of crucifixion. Both are within the semantic range of the Hebrew chalal.

**3. "Stripes" vs. "wounds" vs. "scourging."** The KJV's "stripes" has become the most iconic English rendering. The ESV, NASB (2020), LSB, and NIV use "wounds." The NASB (1995) used "scourging" --- a more interpretive choice that specifically evokes the Roman flogging of Jesus. The variety reflects the range of the Hebrew chaburah: a mark left by a blow.

**4. "Peace" vs. "well-being."** The KJV, ESV, NIV, and LXX render shalom as "peace." The NASB (both editions) and LSB render it as "well-being" --- a broader translation that captures more of shalom's comprehensive range. The NLT renders the concept dynamically: "beaten so we could be whole."

**5. The NLT's dynamic rendering is striking.** The NLT breaks Isaiah 53:5 into two statements: "He was beaten so we could be whole. He was whipped so we could be healed." This rendering makes the healing language maximally clear and accessible, but it also makes interpretive choices that more literal translations leave to the reader.

## Passage 3: Matthew 8:17

> "That it might be fulfilled which was spoken by Esaias the prophet, saying, Himself took our infirmities, and bare our sicknesses." --- KJV

### Comparison Table

| Translation | Fulfillment formula | Quotation |
|---|---|---|
| **KJV** | That it might be fulfilled which was spoken by Esaias the prophet, saying | Himself took our **infirmities**, and bare our **sicknesses** |
| **ESV** | This was to fulfill what was spoken by the prophet Isaiah | "He took our **illnesses** and bore our **diseases**" |
| **NASB (1995)** | This was to fulfill what was spoken through Isaiah the prophet | "He Himself took our **infirmities** and carried away our **diseases**" |
| **NASB (2020)** | This was to fulfill what was spoken through Isaiah the prophet | "He Himself took our **infirmities** and carried away our **diseases**" |
| **LSB** | so that what was spoken through Isaiah the prophet would be fulfilled | "He Himself took our **infirmities** and carried away our **diseases**" |
| **NIV** | This was to fulfill what was spoken through the prophet Isaiah | "He took up our **infirmities** and bore our **diseases**" |
| **NLT** | This fulfilled the word of the Lord through the prophet Isaiah, who said | "He took our **sicknesses** and removed our **diseases**" |

### Key Observations

**1. Every translation uses physical-sickness language.** Without exception, every major English translation renders Matthew 8:17 in terms of physical illness: "infirmities/sicknesses" and "diseases." This is because Matthew's Greek (astheneia and nosos) is unambiguously physical. There is no divergence here.

**2. The contrast with Isaiah 53:4 renderings is revealing.** The same translations that render choli as "griefs" in Isaiah 53:4 (KJV, ESV, NASB 1995, LSB) render Matthew's quotation of that verse using physical-sickness language. This creates an interesting situation: the reader encounters "griefs and sorrows" in Isaiah 53:4 and "infirmities and sicknesses" in Matthew 8:17 --- and yet Matthew is quoting Isaiah 53:4. The English translations make the two passages look more different than they are in the original languages.

**3. The NASB (2020) creates consistency.** Because the NASB (2020) now renders Isaiah 53:4 as "sicknesses and pains," the connection between Isaiah 53:4 and Matthew 8:17 is more immediately visible. The reader can see that Matthew is quoting the same verse, using the same categories.

**4. The NLT's "removed" is interpretive.** Where other translations say Jesus "bore" or "carried away" diseases, the NLT says He "removed" them. This rendering emphasizes the *result* of Jesus's action (the diseases are gone) rather than the *process* (bearing/carrying). It reflects a legitimate reading of bastazō in a disease context, where the word can carry the sense of removal.

## Passage 4: 1 Peter 2:24

> "Who his own self bare our sins in his own body on the tree, that we, being dead to sins, should live unto righteousness: by whose stripes ye were healed." --- KJV

### Comparison Table

| Translation | What Christ bore | Purpose | Healing clause |
|---|---|---|---|
| **KJV** | bare our **sins** in his own body on the tree | that we, being dead to sins, should live unto righteousness | by whose **stripes** ye were **healed** |
| **ESV** | bore our **sins** in his body on the tree | that we might die to sin and live to righteousness | By his **wounds** you have been **healed** |
| **NASB (1995)** | bore our **sins** in His body on the cross | so that we might die to sin and live to righteousness | for by His **wounds** you were **healed** |
| **NASB (2020)** | bore our **sins** in His body on the cross | so that we might die to sin and live for righteousness | by His **wounds** you were **healed** |
| **LSB** | bore our **sins** in His body on the tree | so that we might die to sin and live to righteousness | for by His **wounds** you were **healed** |
| **NIV** | bore our **sins** in his body on the cross | so that we might die to sins and live for righteousness | by his **wounds** you have been **healed** |
| **NLT** | personally carried our **sins** in his body on the cross | so that we can be dead to sin and live for what is right | By his **wounds** you are **healed** |

### Key Observations

**1. Every translation renders hamartias as "sins."** Peter's language is unambiguous: Christ bore our *sins*, not our sicknesses. This is the opposite of Matthew 8:17, where the language is unambiguously about physical sickness. The divergence between the two passages --- both quoting Isaiah 53 --- is the heart of the debate.

**2. "Stripes" (KJV) vs. "wounds" (all others).** The KJV stands alone in using "stripes" --- its most familiar rendering. All modern translations use "wounds." The underlying Greek (mōlōps) supports both.

**3. Tense variation: "were healed" vs. "have been healed" vs. "are healed."** The KJV, NASB, and LSB use "were healed" (simple past). The ESV and NIV use "have been healed" (present perfect, emphasizing ongoing result). The NLT uses "are healed" (simple present). The Greek aorist passive (iathēte) points to a completed past event, but English translators make different choices about how to convey that sense. All three renderings are defensible.

**4. The spiritual context is consistent across all translations.** No translation introduces physical-healing language into 1 Peter 2:24. The context is uniformly about sin, death to sin, and living for righteousness. The "healing" clause is embedded within this spiritual framework in every version.

## Translation Philosophy and the Healing Debate

The cross-version comparison reveals that translation philosophy plays a role in how readers encounter the healing question:

### Formal Equivalence (KJV, ESV, NASB, LSB)

These translations prioritize word-for-word accuracy. They tend to preserve ambiguity where the original is ambiguous, allowing the reader to interpret. In Isaiah 53:4, the KJV/ESV/LSB rendering of "griefs and sorrows" preserves a certain ambiguity --- the words could be physical or emotional. The NASB (2020)'s shift to "sicknesses and pains" represents a judgment that the Hebrew's *primary* meaning should take priority.

### Dynamic Equivalence (NLT)

The NLT prioritizes clarity and readability. Its renderings make interpretive choices that more literal translations leave to the reader. In Isaiah 53:5, the NLT's "He was beaten so we could be whole. He was whipped so we could be healed" is a vivid, accessible rendering --- but it also makes the healing promise more prominent than in literal translations.

### The LSB and Theological Perspective

The Legacy Standard Bible, produced under the oversight of The Master's Seminary, represents a cessationist/Reformed theological perspective. Its translation choices in Isaiah 53 are worth noting:

- It retains "griefs and sorrows" in Isaiah 53:4 (against the NASB 2020's "sicknesses and pains")
- It renders shalom as "well-being" in Isaiah 53:5 (broader than "peace")
- It follows the NASB closely in all other respects

The LSB's retention of "griefs/sorrows" in Isaiah 53:4 is a defensible translation choice, but the reader should be aware that it diverges from the very translation it is based on (the NASB, which changed to "sicknesses/pains" in its 2020 update). This choice has the effect of softening the connection between Isaiah 53:4 and Matthew 8:17, which every translation renders in physical-sickness terms.

### The LXX as Interpretive Witness

The Septuagint is not a modern English translation, but it functions as the earliest interpretive witness to the Hebrew text. Its rendering of choli as hamartias ("sins") in Isaiah 53:4 represents a pre-Christian Jewish judgment that the verse is about sin-bearing. Matthew's rejection of this rendering in favor of physical-sickness language is one of the most important textual observations in the entire study (see Appendix B for the full analysis).

## Summary: What the Cross-Version Evidence Shows

| Observation | Significance |
|---|---|
| All translations agree on "healed" in Isa 53:5 | The debate is not about the word; it is about the scope |
| Translations split on "griefs" vs. "sicknesses" in Isa 53:4 | The translation choice shapes the reader's perception of the entire passage |
| The NASB changed between 1995 and 2020 | Reflects growing scholarly recognition of choli's primary meaning |
| Matthew 8:17 is unanimously physical | No translation renders Matthew's quotation in spiritual terms |
| 1 Peter 2:24 is unanimously spiritual in context | No translation introduces physical healing into Peter's application |
| The LXX renders choli as "sins" | The spiritual reading has pre-Christian Jewish roots |
| Matthew rejects the LXX rendering | The physical reading has inspired apostolic support |

The cross-version comparison demonstrates that the healing debate is not a product of English translation ambiguity. It is embedded in the original languages, in the LXX's interpretive choices, and in the New Testament authors' own divergent applications of Isaiah 53. Faithful translations reflect this complexity; they do not resolve it.
