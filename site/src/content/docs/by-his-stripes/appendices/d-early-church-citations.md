This appendix compiles the most significant early church quotations related to Isaiah 53 and the healing question, as referenced in the Antiquity chapter (Chapter 3) of this study. For each source, the full quotation is given (as complete as surviving texts allow), along with the date, work, and a brief contextual note explaining how the citation functions in the larger study.

These quotations are provided so the reader can evaluate the primary sources directly, rather than relying solely on the summary analysis in the main text. All translations follow standard scholarly editions as noted.

---

## 1. Clement of Rome --- 1 Clement 16:3--14 (c. 96 AD)

**Source:** *First Epistle of Clement to the Corinthians*, chapter 16
**Date:** c. 96 AD
**Translation:** Following Lightfoot/Holmes

This is the most extensive quotation of Isaiah 53 in any post-apostolic document. Clement introduces the passage to exhort the divided Corinthian church toward humility, citing Christ's example:

> For Christ is with those who are humble-minded, not with those who exalt themselves over His flock. The sceptre of the majesty of God, our Lord Christ Jesus, did not come in the pomp of pride or arrogance, although He might have done so, but in a lowly condition, as the Holy Spirit had declared regarding Him. For He says:
>
> "Lord, who hath believed our report, and to whom is the arm of the Lord revealed? We have declared in His presence: He is, as it were, a child, and like a root in thirsty ground; He has no form nor glory, yea, we saw Him, and He had no form nor comeliness; but His form was without honour, failing beyond the form of men; a man in stripes and toil, and acquainted with the bearing of sickness: for His face was turned away, and He was dishonoured, and not esteemed.
>
> He bears our sins and is pained for us; yet we supposed that He was in toil, and in stripes, and in affliction. But He was wounded for our transgressions, and bruised for our iniquities. The chastisement of our peace was upon Him, and by His stripes we were healed. All we like sheep have gone astray; each one went astray in his own way; and the Lord gave Him up for our sins, while He in the midst of His sufferings openeth not His mouth. He was brought as a sheep to the slaughter, and as a lamb before its shearer is dumb, so He openeth not His mouth. In His humiliation His judgment was taken away; who shall declare His generation? For His life is taken from the earth. For the transgressions of my people was He brought to death. And I will give the wicked for His burial, and the rich for His death; because He did no iniquity, neither was guile found in His mouth. And the Lord is pleased to purify Him from His stripes. If ye make an offering for sin, your soul shall see a long-lived seed. And the Lord is pleased to take away from the toil of His soul, to show Him light, and to form Him with understanding; to justify the Just One who is a good servant to many. And He shall bear their sins. On this account He shall inherit many, and shall divide the spoils of the strong; because His soul was delivered to death, and He was reckoned among the transgressors; and He bare the sins of many, and for their sins was He delivered up."
>
> And again He saith: "I am a worm, and no man; a reproach of men, and despised of the people."
>
> --- 1 Clement 16:3--14

**Contextual note:** Clement's application is ethical and christological: Christ came in humility, not pomp, and believers should follow this pattern. The phrase "by His stripes we were healed" is included in the quotation but receives no independent commentary connecting it to physical healing. Clement reads the passage as a narrative of humility and substitutionary suffering for sin. This is the earliest extensive post-apostolic engagement with Isaiah 53.

## 2. Epistle of Barnabas 5:1--2 (c. 70--132 AD)

**Source:** *Epistle of Barnabas*, chapter 5
**Date:** c. 70--132 AD (date debated; most scholars place it between 96--130 AD)
**Translation:** Following Lightfoot/Holmes

The author reads Christ's suffering through a sacrificial and typological lens:

> For to this end the Lord endured to deliver up His flesh to corruption, that we might be sanctified through the remission of sins, which is effected by His blood of sprinkling. For the Scripture concerning Him relates partly to Israel, and partly to us; and says thus: "He was wounded for our transgressions, and bruised for our iniquities; by His stripes we are healed. He was brought as a sheep to the slaughter, and as a lamb which is dumb before its shearer."
>
> We ought therefore to give great thanks to the Lord, that He has both given us knowledge of the past, and wisdom regarding the present, and that with respect to future events we are not without understanding.
>
> --- Epistle of Barnabas 5:1--2

**Contextual note:** The author of Barnabas quotes Isaiah 53:5 and 53:7 together in a context focused on Christ's sacrificial death for sin. The categories are soteriological --- sanctification, remission of sins, the blood of sprinkling. The "healed" language is quoted but not developed independently. The author's interest is in typological fulfillment (Christ as the reality behind OT sacrificial types), not physical healing.

## 3. Ignatius of Antioch --- Epistle to the Smyrnaeans 1--2 (c. 107 AD)

**Source:** *Epistle to the Smyrnaeans*, chapters 1--2
**Date:** c. 107 AD
**Translation:** Following Holmes

Ignatius does not quote Isaiah 53 directly, but his letter to the Smyrnaeans contains a christological confession that affirms the reality of Christ's physical suffering:

> I glorify God, even Jesus Christ, who has given you such wisdom. For I have observed that ye are perfected in an immoveable faith, as if ye were nailed to the cross of our Lord Jesus Christ, both in the flesh and in the spirit, and are established in love through the blood of Christ, being fully persuaded with respect to our Lord, that He was truly of the seed of David according to the flesh, and the Son of God according to the will and power of God; that He was truly born of a virgin, was baptized by John, in order that all righteousness might be fulfilled by Him; that He was truly, under Pontius Pilate and Herod the tetrarch, nailed to the cross for us in His flesh.
>
> He suffered truly, even as also He truly raised up Himself, not, as certain unbelievers maintain, that He only seemed to suffer, as they themselves only seem to be Christians. And as they believe, so shall it happen unto them, when they shall be divested of their bodies, and be mere evil spirits.
>
> --- Ignatius, *To the Smyrnaeans* 1--2

**Contextual note:** Ignatius's concern is anti-docetic: he insists that Christ truly suffered in the flesh, against those who claimed His suffering was only an appearance. His theology is deeply relevant to the healing question because it establishes the reality and physicality of Christ's suffering. However, Ignatius does not connect this physical suffering to a provision of physical healing for believers. His letters elsewhere (especially *To the Romans* 4:1, 5:3) reveal a bishop who desired martyrdom and viewed physical suffering as the path to union with Christ, not as something to be escaped through atonement-based healing.

## 4. Polycarp --- Epistle to the Philippians 8:1--2 (c. 110--140 AD)

**Source:** *Epistle to the Philippians*, chapter 8
**Date:** c. 110--140 AD (date debated)
**Translation:** Following Holmes

Polycarp draws directly on 1 Peter 2:22--24, which itself draws on Isaiah 53:

> "Who his own self bare our sins in his own body on the tree, who did no sin, neither was guile found in his mouth," but endured all things for our sakes, that we might live in Him. Let us therefore become imitators of His patience, and if we suffer for His name's sake, let us glorify Him. For He gave us this example in Himself, and we have believed that this is so.
>
> --- Polycarp, *To the Philippians* 8:1--2

**Contextual note:** Polycarp's application follows Peter's exactly: Christ bore our sins and demonstrated patient endurance under suffering. Believers are to imitate this pattern. The categories are moral and spiritual --- sin-bearing, patient suffering, ethical imitation. Physical healing does not appear in Polycarp's application. As a man who may have known the Apostle John personally, Polycarp's testimony carries particular weight as evidence of how the earliest post-apostolic generation understood the Isaiah 53 tradition.

## 5. Justin Martyr --- *Dialogue with Trypho* 13, 42, 89 (c. 155--160 AD)

**Source:** *Dialogue with Trypho*
**Date:** c. 155--160 AD
**Translation:** Following Roberts-Donaldson (ANF)

Justin Martyr engages with Isaiah 53 more extensively than any other Ante-Nicene writer. His purpose is apologetic: he is arguing with a Jewish interlocutor named Trypho that the Suffering Servant is Jesus.

### Dialogue with Trypho, Chapter 13

> For the whole human race will be found to be under a curse. For it is written in the law of Moses, "Cursed is every one that continueth not in all things that are written in the book of the law to do them." And no one has accurately done all... but if the Father of all wished His Christ to take upon Himself the curses of all, knowing that after He had been crucified and was dead He would raise Him up, why do you argue about Him who was willing to suffer these things according to the Father's will, as if He were accursed, and do not rather bewail yourselves?

### Dialogue with Trypho, Chapter 42

> And that He should be led as a sheep to the slaughter, and that He was to be dumb as a lamb before his shearer, and that He should bear the sins of many, and that He should make many righteous...

### Dialogue with Trypho, Chapter 89

> And by other words He declares that these nations were found, not by seeking after Him, but by seeking after God; and He plainly states that the very nations who were not looking for Him shall worship Him. "Behold, my Servant whom I uphold, mine Elect in whom my soul delighteth. I have given my Spirit upon Him; He shall bring forth judgment to the Gentiles."

**Contextual note:** Justin's sustained engagement with Isaiah 53 is focused entirely on two points: (1) the Servant is the Messiah, and (2) the Messiah was prophesied to suffer. He does not develop any theology of physical healing from the passage. However, Justin does acknowledge elsewhere that Christians practice healing:

> For numberless demoniacs throughout the whole world, and in your city, many of our Christian men, exorcising them in the name of Jesus Christ, who was crucified under Pontius Pilate, have healed and do heal, rendering helpless and driving the possessing devils out of the men.
>
> --- Justin Martyr, *Second Apology* 6

Justin thus affirms the practice of healing while not connecting it to Isaiah 53 or the atonement.

## 6. Melito of Sardis --- *Peri Pascha* 68, 86, 100--103 (c. 160--170 AD)

**Source:** *Peri Pascha* (*On the Pascha*)
**Date:** c. 160--170 AD
**Translation:** Following Stuart Hall (1979)

Melito was bishop of Sardis in Asia Minor. His *Peri Pascha*, an Easter homily discovered among the Bodmer papyri in 1940, contains the most comprehensive "exchange" language of any pre-Nicene document.

### Peri Pascha 68 --- Deliverance from the Curse

> This is the one who covered death with shame and who plunged the devil into mourning as Moses did Pharaoh. This is the one who smote lawlessness and deprived injustice of its offspring, as Moses deprived Egypt. This is the one who delivered us from slavery into freedom, from darkness into light, from death into life, from tyranny into an eternal kingdom...
>
> --- Melito, *Peri Pascha* 68

### Peri Pascha 86 --- Healing Among God's Acts

> This is the one who came to you, the one who healed your suffering ones and who resurrected your dead.
>
> --- Melito, *Peri Pascha* 86

### Peri Pascha 100--103 --- The Great Exchange

> When the Lord had clothed himself with humanity, and had suffered for the sake of the sufferer, and had been bound for the sake of the imprisoned, and had been judged for the sake of the condemned, and buried for the sake of the one who was buried, he rose up from the dead, and cried aloud with this voice:
>
> Who is he who contends with me? Let him stand in opposition to me. I set the condemned man free; I gave the dead man life; I raised up the one who had been entombed.
>
> Who is my opponent? I, he says, am the Christ. I am the one who destroyed death, and triumphed over the enemy, and trampled Hades under foot, and bound the strong one, and carried off man to the heights of heaven, I, he says, am the Christ.
>
> Therefore, come, all families of men, you who have been befouled with sins, and receive forgiveness for your sins. I am your forgiveness, I am the passover of your salvation, I am the lamb which was sacrificed for you, I am your ransom, I am your light, I am your saviour, I am your resurrection, I am your king, I am leading you up to the heights of heaven, I will show you the eternal Father, I will raise you up by my right hand.
>
> --- Melito, *Peri Pascha* 100--103

**Contextual note:** Melito's exchange language is the broadest of any pre-Nicene writer. Section 86 explicitly mentions healing alongside resurrection as acts of Christ. Sections 100--103 describe Christ's work in terms that encompass the entire human condition: judgment, death, burial, slavery, darkness, tyranny --- all reversed. The "I am" declarations claim forgiveness, ransom, resurrection, and kingship. The scope is total. However, Melito does not build a theology of healing in the atonement. His language is doxological, not systematic --- a hymn of praise, not a doctrinal treatise. The healing-in-the-atonement position can find raw materials here, but the finished argument is not present.

## 7. Odes of Solomon --- Ode 25 (c. 1st--2nd century AD)

**Source:** Odes of Solomon, Ode 25
**Date:** c. 1st--2nd century AD (debated)
**Translation:** Following Charlesworth

The Odes of Solomon are a collection of early Christian hymns, probably composed in Syriac. Their dating and authorship are debated, but they represent some of the earliest surviving Christian worship poetry.

> I was rescued from my bonds and unto thee, my God, I fled: for thou art the right hand of my Salvation and my helper. Thou hast restrained those that rise up against me, and I shall see him no more: because thy face was with me, which saved me by thy grace. But I was despised and rejected in the eyes of many: and I was in their eyes like lead, and strength was mine from thyself and help. Thou didst set me a lamp at my right hand and at my left: and in me there shall be nothing that is not bright: and I was clothed with the covering of thy Spirit, and thou didst remove from me my raiment of skin. For thy right hand lifted me up and removed sickness from me: and I became mighty in the truth, and holy by thy righteousness; and all my adversaries were afraid of me; and I became admirable by the name of the Lord, and was justified by His gentleness, and His rest is for ever and ever. Hallelujah.
>
> --- Odes of Solomon 25:1--11

**Contextual note:** The phrase "removed sickness from me" (verse 9) places physical healing within a hymn celebrating the totality of God's redemptive work. The "raiment of skin" (verse 8) echoes Genesis 3:21 --- the garments God made for Adam and Eve after the fall --- suggesting that the removal of sickness is part of the reversal of the fall's consequences. This is an early Christian hymn, sung in worship, that connects bodily healing to redemption. It is not a doctrinal statement, but it reflects the devotional instincts of early believers.

## 8. Epistle to Diognetus --- Chapter 9 (c. 130--200 AD)

**Source:** Epistle to Diognetus, chapter 9
**Date:** c. 130--200 AD (debated)
**Translation:** Following Lightfoot/Harmer (1891)

The Epistle to Diognetus is an anonymous early Christian apology. Chapter 9 contains one of the most famous "exchange" passages in early Christian literature.

> And when our iniquity had been fully accomplished, and it had been made perfectly manifest that punishment and death were expected as its recompense, and the season came which God had ordained, when henceforth He should manifest His goodness and power (O the exceeding great kindness and love of God), He hated us not, neither rejected us, nor bore us malice, but was long-suffering and patient, and in pity for us took upon Himself our sins, and Himself parted with His own Son as a ransom for us, the holy for the lawless, the guileless for the evil, the just for the unjust, the incorruptible for the corruptible, the immortal for the mortal. For what else but His righteousness would have covered our sins? In whom was it possible for us lawless and ungodly men to have been justified, save only in the Son of God? O the sweet exchange, O the inscrutable creation, O the unexpected benefits; that the iniquity of many should be concealed in One Righteous Man, and the righteousness of One should justify many that are iniquitous!
>
> --- Epistle to Diognetus 9:2--5

**Contextual note:** The *admirabile commercium* --- "the sweet exchange" --- became a foundational concept in later Christian theology. In this passage, the exchange is focused on sin and righteousness: Christ's righteousness covers human wickedness. Physical healing is not mentioned. Yet the exchange framework itself --- Christ takes what is ours and gives us what is his --- has the logical capacity to extend beyond sin. Notably, Diognetus 9:6 proceeds to call God "nurse, father, teacher, counsellor, physician, mind, light, honour, glory, strength and life" --- the title "physician" (*iatros*) appears alongside the exchange, echoing Ignatius's "one Physician" christology.

## 9. Ignatius of Antioch --- *To the Ephesians* 7:2 (c. 108 AD)

**Source:** *Epistle to the Ephesians*, chapter 7
**Date:** c. 108 AD
**Translation:** Following Lightfoot

> There is one Physician who is possessed both of flesh and spirit; both made and not made; God existing in flesh; true life in death; both of Mary and of God; first passible and then impassible --- even Jesus Christ our Lord.
>
> --- Ignatius, *To the Ephesians* 7:2

**Contextual note:** "One Physician" (*eis iatros*) is one of the earliest christological titles in post-apostolic literature. The medical metaphor bridges the human experience of sickness and the divine reality of wholeness. Ignatius does not develop this into a healing-in-the-atonement theology --- his other letters reveal a bishop who desired martyrdom and viewed physical suffering as the path to union with Christ. But the "Physician" title shows that the earliest post-apostolic generation instinctively used healing language to describe Christ's person and work.

## 10. Irenaeus of Lyon --- *Against Heresies* III.16.9, V.12.6 (c. 180 AD)

**Source:** *Against Heresies* (*Adversus Haereses*)
**Date:** c. 180 AD
**Translation:** Following Roberts-Donaldson (ANF)

### Against Heresies III.16.9

> But according to none of the heretics was the Word of God made flesh. For if anyone examines their systems, he will find that the Word of God is made by all of them to be without flesh and incapable of suffering... But our Lord is alone truly Master; and good is truly the Son of God; and He is patient, the Word of God the Father made the Son of man. He fought and conquered; for He was man contending for the fathers, and through obedience doing away with disobedience completely: for He bound the strong man, and set free the weak, and endowed His own handiwork with salvation, by destroying sin. For He is a most holy and merciful Lord, and loves the human race.

### Against Heresies V.12.6

> For as the flesh is capable of corruption, so is it also of incorruption; and as it is of death, so is it also of life. These two do mutually give way to each other; and both cannot remain in the same place, but the one is driven out by the other, and the presence of the one destroys that of the other. If then death, when it obtains the power, takes possession of the mortal body, much more shall life, when it obtains the power, take possession of that which is mortal, and so drive out death.

**Contextual note:** Irenaeus's recapitulation theology --- the idea that Christ "sums up" and redeems all of human experience --- is the one pre-Nicene framework with the theoretical capacity to support a healing-in-the-atonement doctrine. If the whole person fell in Adam, the whole person is redeemed in Christ. However, Irenaeus himself locates the body's full redemption in the future resurrection, not in a present guarantee of physical healing. His theology provides the raw materials from which a healing-in-the-atonement position could be constructed, but he does not construct it himself.

## 11. Origen --- *Contra Celsum* I.54--55 (c. 248 AD)

**Source:** *Contra Celsum* (*Against Celsus*)
**Date:** c. 248 AD
**Translation:** Following Chadwick

Origen quotes Isaiah 53 extensively in his defense of Christianity against the pagan critic Celsus:

> But we say to each of those who object against us: "Who hath believed our report, and to whom is the arm of the LORD revealed?" And in Isaiah too: "He hath no form nor comeliness, and when we see Him, there is no beauty that we should desire Him. He was despised and rejected of men, a man of sorrows and acquainted with grief." And that the Servant of God bears our sins, and suffers on our behalf --- "He was wounded for our transgressions, He was bruised for our iniquities, the chastisement of our peace was upon Him, and by His stripes we are healed."
>
> --- Origen, *Contra Celsum* 1.54--55

**Contextual note:** Origen's reading of Isaiah 53 is christological and apologetic: the passage proves that the Messiah was destined to suffer. Consistent with his allegorical method, Origen reads the healing language as referring to spiritual restoration. In his *Homilies on Leviticus* (8.1), he writes: "Every soul that has sinned is sick. It is the Saviour who heals the sick soul, not the sick body. Or rather, He does heal the body too --- but the body in that it is an image of the soul." Physical language points to spiritual reality in Origen's hermeneutic.

Origen does, however, acknowledge that Christians in his day experienced divine healing:

> And some give evidence of their having received through this faith a marvellous power by the cures which they perform, invoking no other name over those who need their help than that of the God of all things, and of Jesus, along with a mention of His history. For by these means we too have seen many persons freed from grievous calamities, and from distractions of mind, and madness, and countless other ills.
>
> --- Origen, *Contra Celsum* 3.24

Origen thus affirms both the spiritual reading of Isaiah 53 and the reality of healing in the church, without connecting the two.

## 12. Tertullian --- *An Answer to the Jews* (*Adversus Judaeos*) 10 (c. 197 AD)

**Source:** *An Answer to the Jews* (*Adversus Judaeos*), chapter 10
**Date:** c. 197 AD
**Translation:** Following Roberts-Donaldson (ANF)

Tertullian engages with Isaiah 53 in his anti-Jewish polemic, arguing that the Messiah was prophesied to suffer:

> Come, now, if you read in the prophet Isaiah of "a Branch from the root of Jesse," which is Christ, and "a flower which ascended from his root," this flower is the one on which "the spirit of God rested," --- the spirit of wisdom and understanding, the spirit of counsel and might, the spirit of knowledge and piety, the spirit of the fear of God. For to none of the men of old did the completeness of the spirit belong, as it did to Christ... And then He says: "Who has believed our report, and to whom is the arm of the Lord revealed? We made proclamation before Him as a child, as a root in a thirsty land; and there was in Him neither form nor glory; and we saw Him, and He had neither form nor beauty; but His form was without honour and deficient beyond all men; a man in suffering and acquainted with the bearing of sickness..."
>
> --- Tertullian, *Adversus Judaeos* 10 (abridged)

**Contextual note:** Tertullian's engagement follows the established pattern: Isaiah 53 predicts a suffering Messiah, against Jewish interpreters who expected a triumphant one. His categories are messianic and soteriological. He does not develop any connection between "bearing of sickness" and a provision of physical healing for believers. Even in his later Montanist phase, which was open to charismatic experience, Tertullian did not ground physical healing in Isaiah 53:5.

## 13. Cyprian of Carthage --- *Testimonies Against the Jews* II.13; *De Mortalitate* (c. 250 AD)

**Source:** *Testimonies Against the Jews* (*Ad Quirinum*), Book II, Chapter 13; *De Mortalitate* (*On the Plague*)
**Date:** c. 248--252 AD
**Translation:** Following Deferrari/Roberts-Donaldson

### Testimonies Against the Jews II.13

In this catena of proof texts, Cyprian cites Isaiah 53 to demonstrate that Christ's passion was prophesied:

> Also in Isaiah: "Who hath believed our report? and to whom is the arm of the Lord revealed? We have announced before Him, He is as a child, as a root in a thirsty ground. There is no form nor glory in Him; and we saw Him, and He had no form nor beauty; but His appearance was without honour and deficient more than all men; a man in suffering, and acquainted with the endurance of sickness; because His face is turned away, He was dishonoured and was not esteemed. He bears our sins and suffers for us; and we thought that He was in sorrow, and in suffering, and in affliction. But He was wounded for our transgressions, and was weakened for our sins; the discipline of our peace was upon Him, and by His bruises we are healed."
>
> --- Cyprian, *Testimonies Against the Jews* II.13

### De Mortalitate (On the Plague)

Written during the devastating Plague of Cyprian (c. 252 AD), this pastoral letter addresses mass physical suffering among Christians. Cyprian's response does not appeal to healing as an atonement provision:

> What a grandeur of spirit it is to struggle with all the powers of an unshaken mind against so many onsets of devastation and death! What sublimity, to stand erect among the desolation of the human race, and not to lie prostrate with those who have no hope in God! ... Our brethren who have been freed from the world by the summons of the Lord should not be mourned, since we know that they are not lost but sent before.
>
> --- Cyprian, *De Mortalitate* 2

And further:

> How suitable, how necessary it is that this plague and pestilence, which seems horrible and deadly, searches out the righteousness of each one, and examines the minds of the human race, to see whether they who are in health tend the sick; whether relatives affectionately love their kindred; whether masters pity their languishing servants; whether physicians do not forsake the beseeching patients...
>
> --- Cyprian, *De Mortalitate* 16

**Contextual note:** Cyprian provides the most instructive pastoral test case among the Ante-Nicene Fathers. In a document specifically addressing mass physical suffering, written by a major bishop to a suffering congregation, there is no appeal to healing as a provision of the atonement. There is no invocation of Isaiah 53:5 as a promise of physical deliverance. Instead, Cyprian counsels endurance, compassion, and eschatological hope. Christians are distinguished not by their exemption from suffering but by how they respond to it.

## Summary: The Patristic Pattern

The early church citations collected here reveal a consistent pattern across the first three centuries of Christianity:

| Source | Date | How Isaiah 53 Is Used | Physical Healing Mentioned? |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 Clement | c. 96 AD | Humility and substitutionary suffering | No |
| Epistle of Barnabas | c. 70--132 AD | Sacrificial typology; remission of sins | No |
| Ignatius | c. 107--108 AD | Reality of Christ's physical suffering (anti-docetic); "one Physician" | Christ as Physician, but desires martyrdom |
| Polycarp | c. 110--140 AD | Sin-bearing and patient endurance (via 1 Peter 2:24) | No |
| Justin Martyr | c. 155--160 AD | Messianic apologetics; the Servant is the Messiah | Healing acknowledged but not connected to Isa 53 |
| Melito of Sardis | c. 160--170 AD | Comprehensive exchange language; broadest redemptive scope | Yes --- "healed your suffering ones" (§86) |
| Odes of Solomon | c. 1st--2nd c. AD | Hymn of redemption; reversal of the fall | Yes --- "removed sickness from me" (25:9) |
| Epistle to Diognetus | c. 130--200 AD | "Sweet exchange" --- sin/righteousness focus | God called "physician" (9:6), but healing not developed |
| Irenaeus | c. 180 AD | Recapitulation theology; body's redemption at resurrection | Framework could include it; explicit connection absent |
| Origen | c. 248 AD | Allegorical/spiritual reading; healing = soul's restoration | Healing acknowledged but not connected to Isa 53 |
| Tertullian | c. 197 AD | Messianic apologetics against Jewish interpretation | No |
| Cyprian | c. 250 AD | Proof text for Christ's passion; pastoral endurance under plague | No --- counsels endurance, not healing claims |

The reader should note both what this evidence shows and what it does not show. It shows that the earliest Christian interpreters consistently read Isaiah 53 as a prophecy of Christ's substitutionary suffering for sin. It does not show that they denied God heals --- several of them affirm the reality of healing in the church. What they did not do is connect the practice of healing to Isaiah 53:5 or to a systematic theology of healing in the atonement.

What the new entries reveal is a second layer: the devotional and liturgical instincts of the early church did reach beyond sin alone. Melito's exchange is total in scope. The Odes of Solomon place healing within worship. Diognetus and Ignatius use medical language for Christ. These are seeds, not doctrines --- but they show that the earliest believers' worship instincts connected Christ's work to the whole person, including the body.
