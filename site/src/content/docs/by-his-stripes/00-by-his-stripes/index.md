## The Question That Won't Go Away

> "But he was wounded for our transgressions, he was bruised for our iniquities: the chastisement of our peace was upon him; and with his stripes we are healed."
> --- Isaiah 53:5 (KJV)

These are some of the most beautiful words in the Bible. They have been read at bedsides and in cathedrals, whispered in prayer and declared in song. They belong to every Christian who has ever leaned on the promises of God.

But they also carry a question --- one that has been asked for centuries and has never produced an easy answer: **Does "by his stripes we are healed" mean physical healing?**

The question matters. It is not a footnote in a theology textbook. It is not an argument to be won at a conference. It shapes how we pray when a loved one is diagnosed with cancer. It shapes what we say --- and what we dare not say --- to a mother whose child was born with a disability. It shapes what we expect from God, and what we do with our disappointment when healing does not come.

If physical healing is in the atonement, then the cross has purchased something we can reach for right now --- something as real and available as the forgiveness of our sins. If it is not, then asking for healing is still good and right, but it is a request, not a claim. And if the truth lies somewhere in between --- if healing is real but not guaranteed in this life --- then we need a framework sturdy enough to hold both the miracles and the mystery.

This study examines the question from every angle: the original Hebrew of Isaiah 53, the way the New Testament authors applied the passage, what Jesus himself did and taught, what the earliest Christians believed, and how the three major views held by the church today handle the evidence. It does not pick a winner. It presents the strongest case for each position, names the honest challenges each one faces, and trusts you to weigh the evidence for yourself.

I should say up front: I have my own convictions on this question. But the purpose of this study is not to tell you what I think. It is to lay out the evidence so you can see it clearly, and then to step out of the way. The person most qualified to decide what you believe about healing and the atonement is you --- with a Bible in your hands, the Spirit in your heart, and a willingness to follow the evidence wherever it leads.

The deeper chapters and appendices of this project provide the encyclopedic detail for readers who want to trace every word study, every ancient source, and every theological argument to its roots. What you are reading now is the whole picture in one sitting. If this is all you read, you will have enough to think carefully and prayerfully about where you land.

Let us begin with the text itself.

## What Scripture Says

### The Heart of Isaiah 53

Isaiah 53 is one of the most profound chapters in the Bible. It describes a Servant of God who suffers --- not for his own sins, but for ours. He is rejected, wounded, crushed, and ultimately killed. And through his suffering, something is accomplished for the people he represents. Christians have always understood this Servant to be Jesus Christ. The passage was read as messianic long before Jesus was born --- the ancient Aramaic Targum opens it with the words "Behold, my servant the Messiah shall prosper." The New Testament applies it to Christ repeatedly (Matthew 8:17; Acts 8:32--35; 1 Peter 2:24).

The heart of the debate is found in two verses --- Isaiah 53:4 and 53:5. Here they are together:

> "Surely he hath borne our griefs, and carried our sorrows: yet we did esteem him stricken, smitten of God, and afflicted. But he was wounded for our transgressions, he was bruised for our iniquities: the chastisement of our peace was upon him; and with his stripes we are healed."
> --- Isaiah 53:4--5 (KJV)

Most English readers see "griefs," "sorrows," and "healed" and hear the language of emotional and spiritual comfort. And that is a perfectly reasonable reading of the English. But the Hebrew underneath these words tells a more complex story.

The word the KJV translates as **"griefs"** in verse 4 is the Hebrew word *choliy*. In the vast majority of its appearances in the Old Testament, *choliy* means **sickness** or **disease**. It is the word used when Hezekiah falls terminally ill (Isaiah 38:9), when Ahaziah inquires about his physical disease (2 Kings 1:2), and when God promises to take away "all sickness" from Israel (Deuteronomy 7:15). The KJV's "griefs" is not wrong --- the word can carry that meaning --- but it is the less common translation. The 2020 update of the NASB changed its rendering from "griefs" to **"sicknesses,"** reflecting a growing recognition among scholars that the physical meaning deserves representation in the English text.

The word the KJV translates as **"sorrows"** is *makob*. Its primary meaning is **pain** --- physical or emotional. Job 33:19 uses it for a man chastened "with pain upon his bed." The NASB (2020) translates it as **"pains."**

And the word translated **"healed"** in verse 5 is *rapha* --- one of the most common words for **physical healing** in the entire Old Testament. God uses it when He says, "I am the LORD that healeth thee" (Exodus 15:26). It is the word used for the healing of Naaman's leprosy, for Hezekiah's recovery from terminal illness, and for the restoration of barren land. But *rapha* is also used in clearly spiritual contexts --- "heal my soul; for I have sinned" (Psalm 41:4) and the prophets' frequent use of healing language for national and spiritual restoration (Jeremiah 3:22; Isaiah 6:10).

There is one more word in verse 5 that deserves attention. The "chastisement of our **peace**" --- the Hebrew word is *shalom*. It does not mean merely the absence of conflict. *Shalom* is one of the richest words in the Hebrew language: it encompasses completeness, soundness, well-being --- physical, spiritual, relational. The punishment that produces our *shalom* was placed on the Servant. That word alone suggests the scope of what his suffering accomplishes is broader than any single category can contain.

Now look at the structure of verse 5 itself. It is built in four parallel lines:

- He was wounded --- for **our transgressions**
- He was bruised --- for **our iniquities**
- The chastisement of **our peace** was upon him
- By his stripes --- **we are healed**

The first two lines are unambiguously about sin: transgressions and iniquities. The third introduces *shalom* --- wholeness, well-being. The fourth uses *rapha* --- healed. The question is whether the passage narrows (all four lines mean the same thing --- spiritual restoration from sin) or broadens (moving from the root cause in lines 1--2 to the comprehensive result in lines 3--4). Both readings have serious, thoughtful advocates. The text alone does not settle it, which is why the debate continues.

So the Hebrew words in Isaiah 53:4--5 genuinely carry **both** physical and spiritual meaning. The text is broader than any single English translation can capture. This is not a modern discovery --- the ancient translators wrestled with the same tension, as we will see.

### Matthew's Move

Now watch what happens when the New Testament picks up these verses. In Matthew 8, Jesus has just healed Peter's mother-in-law of a fever, and crowds are bringing the sick to him:

> "When the even was come, they brought unto him many that were possessed with devils: and he cast out the spirits with his word, and healed all that were sick: That it might be fulfilled which was spoken by Esaias the prophet, saying, Himself took our infirmities, and bare our sicknesses."
> --- Matthew 8:16--17 (KJV)

Matthew quotes Isaiah 53:4 --- and he applies it to **physical healing**. The Greek words he uses are *astheneia* ("infirmity, weakness") and *nosos* ("disease") --- words that refer unmistakably to bodily illness. *Nosos* is used exclusively for physical disease everywhere it appears in the New Testament.

Here is something most readers do not know: Matthew did not use the Greek Old Testament (the Septuagint) for his quotation. The Septuagint had translated *choliy* --- the Hebrew word for "sickness" --- as *hamartias*, meaning **"sins."** The Septuagint translators, working centuries before Christ, had already made an interpretive choice: they read the sickness language as a reference to sin-bearing. Matthew rejected that rendering. He went back to the Hebrew and chose Greek words that mean physical sickness. Under the guidance of the Holy Spirit, he wanted his readers to see that Isaiah 53:4 speaks about bodily ailment --- and that Jesus was fulfilling it by healing the sick.

There is also a timing question that sharpens the debate. The healings in Matthew 8 take place **before the crucifixion**. Jesus has not yet gone to the cross. If Matthew is saying that these healings "fulfill" Isaiah 53:4, and if Isaiah 53 is about what the atonement accomplishes, how can healings before the atonement fulfill it? Those who see healing as a provision of the atonement must explain how the provision could be accessed before the cross. Those who deny healing in the atonement must explain why Matthew uses his standard fulfillment formula --- the same formula he uses for the virgin birth (Matthew 1:22), the flight to Egypt (Matthew 2:15), and other genuine messianic fulfillments --- to connect Isaiah 53 to physical healing.

But there is a counter-observation that weakens the timing objection considerably: **the same problem applies to forgiveness, and no one finds it troubling.** David was forgiven before the cross (2 Samuel 12:13). Abraham was justified by faith before the cross (Genesis 15:6). Paul explains that God "passed over" former sins because the atonement, though future in time, was certain in God's purposes (Romans 3:25). If forgiveness before the cross was grounded in the future atonement, why can't healing before the cross be grounded the same way? The timing objection, pressed to its logical conclusion, would undermine pre-cross forgiveness --- and no one is willing to follow that logic. Jesus himself connects an Old Testament physical healing --- Moses lifting up the bronze serpent to heal the Israelites' snake bites --- directly to the cross: "As Moses lifted up the serpent in the wilderness, even so must the Son of man be lifted up" (John 3:14). The timing question does not settle the debate, but the forgiveness parallel is a point the reader should weigh carefully.

### Peter's Application

But Peter takes the passage in a different direction. Writing to persecuted believers about following Christ's example of suffering, he says:

> "Who his own self bare our sins in his own body on the tree, that we, being dead to sins, should live unto righteousness: by whose stripes ye were healed. For ye were as sheep going astray; but are now returned unto the Shepherd and Bishop of your souls."
> --- 1 Peter 2:24--25 (KJV)

Peter quotes Isaiah 53:5 --- "by whose stripes ye were healed" --- but his context is entirely about **sin and spiritual restoration**. Christ bore "our sins" (not our sicknesses) on the tree. The purpose is that we might be "dead to sins" and "live unto righteousness." The verse before it is about sinlessness; the verse after it is about sheep who went astray and returned to their Shepherd. There is no mention of physical illness anywhere in the passage.

### The Tension

So here we stand: two inspired New Testament authors, both quoting Isaiah 53, both writing under the guidance of the Holy Spirit --- and they apply the passage in **different directions**. Matthew applies Isaiah 53:4 to physical healing. Peter applies Isaiah 53:5 to spiritual healing from sin.

Are they contradicting each other? Almost certainly not. But they are drawing out different dimensions of a passage that is richer than either application alone can exhaust. The question is how those dimensions fit together --- and that is where the debate begins.

There is one more textual detail worth noting. Matthew quotes verse 4 of Isaiah 53. Peter quotes verse 5. Neither quotes the other's verse. Some interpreters see this as significant: verse 4 (about bearing sickness and pain) may describe one aspect of the Servant's work, while verse 5 (about being wounded for transgressions and iniquities) may describe another. Others see the two verses as a single, inseparable unit in the original Hebrew that should be read together. The reader must weigh whether the distinction between verse 4 and verse 5 is meaningful or artificial.

One more text deserves mention. James, writing to the church, gives this instruction:

> "Is any sick among you? let him call for the elders of the church; and let them pray over him, anointing him with oil in the name of the Lord: And the prayer of faith shall save the sick, and the Lord shall raise him up; and if he have committed sins, they shall be forgiven him."
> --- James 5:14--15 (KJV)

James connects healing and forgiveness of sins in a single pastoral instruction --- addressed to the church, not just to the apostles, with no indication that the practice is temporary.

Even here, however, faithful interpreters disagree about what James means. Some read "sick" (*astheneo*) as spiritual weakness --- the Greek word can carry that sense --- and the anointing with oil as the Spirit's refreshing ministry rather than a physical remedy. In this reading, the elders are restoring a discouraged, sin-burdened believer, not treating a physical illness. This is a sincere and carefully reasoned position, held by respected scholars.

Most interpreters, however --- across all three views on healing in the atonement --- read James 5 as referring to physical illness. The word *astheneo* is used for bodily sickness throughout the Gospels and Acts (Matthew 10:8; John 11:1--3; Acts 9:37). The instruction to anoint with oil reflects a practice associated with physical care in the ancient world (Mark 6:13; Luke 10:34). And the promise that "the Lord shall raise him up" uses language (*egeiro*) that elsewhere describes physical restoration. The passage also says "if he have committed sins, they shall be forgiven him" --- treating the forgiveness as a conditional addition, which would be strange if the entire passage were already about sin.

On the other end of the spectrum, some in the Word of Faith tradition affirm the passage as physical healing but view calling the elders as a provision for believers whose personal faith has not yet matured to the point of claiming healing directly. In this reading, the mature believer exercises authority over sickness; the elders' prayer serves those who need help.

The disagreement over James 5 mirrors the larger disagreement over Isaiah 53: What does healing mean? Who is it for? How is it received? James 5 is not the central text of this study, but it shows that the question of physical healing in the life of the church runs deeper than any single passage. *(Chapter 1 provides a fuller treatment of James 5 alongside the detailed exegesis of Isaiah 53, Matthew 8:16--17, and 1 Peter 2:24.)*

## What Jesus Showed Us

Jesus healed people. This is one of the least disputed facts of his earthly ministry. The Gospels describe it not as occasional or incidental but as central --- by some counts, nearly one-fifth of the Gospel narratives involve healing. The summary statements are sweeping:

> "And Jesus went about all Galilee, teaching in their synagogues, and preaching the gospel of the kingdom, and healing all manner of sickness and all manner of disease among the people."
> --- Matthew 4:23 (KJV)

He healed lepers, paralytics, the blind, the deaf, the demon-possessed. He healed with a touch, with a word, and sometimes from a distance. And he did it out of **compassion** --- the Gospels repeatedly use the Greek word *splanchnizomai*, a visceral, gut-level response to human pain:

> "And Jesus went forth, and saw a great multitude, and was moved with compassion toward them, and he healed their sick."
> --- Matthew 14:14 (KJV)

He connected his healings to the kingdom of God. When he cast out demons, he said, "If I cast out devils by the Spirit of God, then the kingdom of God is come unto you" (Matthew 12:28). When he sent his disciples to heal, he told them to say, "The kingdom of God is come nigh unto you" (Luke 10:9). The healings were not separate from the kingdom's arrival --- they were part of it.

And when John the Baptist, imprisoned and doubting, sent messengers to ask, "Art thou he that should come, or do we look for another?" --- Jesus answered by pointing to the healings:

> "The blind receive their sight, and the lame walk, the lepers are cleansed, and the deaf hear, the dead are raised up, and the poor have the gospel preached to them."
> --- Matthew 11:5 (KJV)

The healings were messianic credentials. They identified who Jesus was.

But Jesus also taught that suffering is part of the life of faith. On the night before his crucifixion, he told his disciples plainly:

> "In the world ye shall have tribulation: but be of good cheer; I have overcome the world."
> --- John 16:33 (KJV)

That word "shall" is not conditional. Tribulation is promised --- not as a punishment, but as a reality of life in a world that is not yet fully redeemed.

When his disciples assumed that a man's blindness was punishment for sin, Jesus corrected them directly:

> "Neither hath this man sinned, nor his parents: but that the works of God should be made manifest in him."
> --- John 9:3 (KJV)

Sickness is not always the result of sin. It is not always a sign of faithlessness. Sometimes it exists so that God's power and purposes can be displayed.

And in Gethsemane, on the night of his arrest, Jesus himself modeled something every suffering believer needs to see. He fell on his face and prayed:

> "O my Father, if it be possible, let this cup pass from me: nevertheless not as I will, but as thou wilt."
> --- Matthew 26:39 (KJV)

He prayed three times. The cup did not pass. The Father's answer was not removal of suffering but strength to endure it. If the Son of God himself asked for suffering to be removed and was told "no" --- and if his submission to that "no" was the greatest act of faith in human history --- then the believer who prays for healing and does not receive it is not demonstrating a lack of faith. They are walking the path their Lord walked first.

One of the most striking features of Jesus's vocabulary is a single Greek word: **sozo**. It means both "to save" (in the eternal, spiritual sense) and "to heal" or "to make whole" (in the physical sense). When the woman with the issue of blood touched his garment, Jesus said, "Thy faith hath made thee whole" --- and "made thee whole" is *sozo* (Matthew 9:22). When he came to Zacchaeus's house, he said, "The Son of man is come to seek and to save that which was lost" --- and "save" is the same word, *sozo* (Luke 19:10). Whether this shared vocabulary reveals that saving and healing are two dimensions of one redemptive work --- or whether *sozo* is simply a versatile word that shifts meaning with context --- is a question the evidence does not settle with certainty.

There is a striking pattern in the Gospels that deserves attention: **the Gospels do not record a single case of an individual coming to Jesus in faith for physical healing and being turned away.** The woman with the issue of blood, blind Bartimaeus, the centurion's servant, the Syrophoenician woman --- every person who sought healing from Jesus in faith received it. Even the Syrophoenician woman, whom Jesus initially seemed to refuse, was ultimately healed when she persisted (Matthew 15:22--28). The Nazareth episode (Mark 6:5--6) is sometimes cited as an exception, but the problem there was the absence of faith, not its presence. During Jesus's earthly ministry, faith for healing was always met with healing.

And then there is Paul. The apostle who healed others through the power of the Spirit (Acts 19:11--12) prayed three times for his "thorn in the flesh" to be removed:

> "For this thing I besought the Lord thrice, that it might depart from me. And he said unto me, My grace is sufficient for thee: for my strength is made perfect in weakness."
> --- 2 Corinthians 12:8--9 (KJV)

God's answer was not removal but grace. This is the passage most often cited against the healing-in-atonement position --- but it deserves a closer look. **The text does not tell us what Paul's thorn was.** Many modern readers assume it was a physical illness --- perhaps an eye disease (Galatians 4:15) or chronic pain. But the language Paul uses points in another direction: he calls it "a messenger [*angelos*] of Satan to buffet [*kolaphizō*] me." The word *angelos* almost always means a personal being in the New Testament --- an angel or a human agent. The word *kolaphizō* means "to strike with the fist" --- it is the same word used for the soldiers beating Jesus at his trial (Matthew 26:67). Early church fathers like Chrysostom read the thorn as Paul's human opponents --- the persistent persecution and opposition that followed him everywhere. If the thorn was persecution rather than sickness, this passage has no direct bearing on the healing question at all.

The honest answer is that we do not know what Paul's thorn was. If it was physical illness, it is the clearest New Testament example of a faithful believer praying for healing and being told no. If it was not, the healing-in-atonement position remains unchallenged on this specific point. The reader must weigh the evidence. *(Chapter 1 and Chapter 2 provide the full analysis of the thorn's possible identifications.)*

The relationship between faith and healing, it turns out, is real but not mechanical. Jesus sometimes healed in response to the sick person's faith ("Thy faith hath made thee whole," Matthew 9:22). Sometimes he healed in response to the faith of others --- the centurion's servant was healed because of the centurion's faith, not the servant's (Matthew 8:5--13). Sometimes he healed without any mention of faith at all --- the man at the Pool of Bethesda did not even know who Jesus was (John 5:5--13). During his earthly ministry, faith for healing always resulted in healing. In the post-ascension period, the picture grows more complex --- Paul's thorn, Trophimus left sick at Miletus (2 Timothy 4:20), and Epaphroditus near death (Philippians 2:27) all introduce questions that the Gospels alone do not raise. There is no simple formula. There is a Person who responds to people in their specific situations, according to the Father's will, with compassion and purpose.

The pattern that emerges from Jesus's life and teaching is this: **he heals freely and compassionately, but he does not promise that every believer will be healed in this life.** He overcomes the world --- but he also promises tribulation in it. He bridges saving and healing in his vocabulary --- but he also calls his followers to take up their cross (Matthew 16:24).

When Jesus spoke directly about the purpose of his death, his words centered on sin:

> "Even as the Son of man came not to be ministered unto, but to minister, and to give his life a ransom for many."
> --- Matthew 20:28 (KJV)

> "For this is my blood of the new testament, which is shed for many for the remission of sins."
> --- Matthew 26:28 (KJV)

His explicit atonement language is about ransom and the forgiveness of sins. But his broader ministry --- healing the sick, casting out demons, giving sight to the blind --- addresses the whole person. And his promise of "abundant life" (John 10:10) opens a door that is wider than forgiveness alone. The tension between the narrow atonement language and the broad redemptive ministry is at the center of this debate.

## What the Ancient Church Believed

If the earliest Christians --- the ones who lived one or two generations from the apostles --- understood Isaiah 53 as teaching physical healing in the atonement, that would be powerful evidence. If they did not, that silence tells us something too.

The evidence from the first three centuries is remarkably consistent. The writers we call the Apostolic Fathers --- Clement of Rome (c. 96 AD), the author of the Epistle of Barnabas (c. 100 AD), Ignatius of Antioch (c. 110 AD), and Polycarp of Smyrna (c. 155 AD) --- all read Isaiah 53 as a prophecy of Christ's suffering for sin. Clement quotes Isaiah 53 at length in a letter to the Corinthians. He applies every line to Christ's humility, his innocent suffering, and the forgiveness he won for sinners. He does not connect "by his stripes we are healed" to physical healing.

The pattern continues with the major theologians of the next two centuries. Justin Martyr (c. 165 AD), the church's first great defender of the faith, argued from Isaiah 53 at length in his dialogue with a Jewish scholar named Trypho. His concern was to prove that the Messiah was destined to suffer --- not to develop a theology of physical healing. Origen (c. 250 AD), the most brilliant biblical scholar of the early church, read the healing language of Isaiah 53 as spiritual and metaphorical, consistent with his approach to all prophetic imagery. Cyprian of Carthage (c. 252 AD), writing to his congregation during a devastating plague that was killing Christians and pagans alike, did not appeal to Isaiah 53:5 as a promise of physical deliverance. His counsel was endurance, compassion, and the hope of resurrection.

**And yet --- the early church absolutely practiced healing.** Justin Martyr testified that Christians in his day healed the sick and cast out demons "in the name of Jesus Christ." Origen acknowledged "marvellous cures" performed through faith. The *Apostolic Tradition* (c. 215 AD) included specific provisions for recognizing gifts of healing in the community. Healing was happening. It just was not being grounded in Isaiah 53 or in a theology of the atonement.

There is, however, a second layer of early evidence that complicates the picture. **Melito of Sardis** (c. 160--170 AD) wrote a Passover sermon called *Peri Pascha* that contains the most comprehensive exchange language of any pre-Nicene document. In it, Christ declares: "I set the condemned man free; I gave the dead man life; I raised up the one who had been entombed... I am your forgiveness, I am the passover of your salvation, I am the lamb which was sacrificed for you, I am your ransom, I am your life, I am your resurrection, I am your king." And in one line he says: "It is he... who healed your suffering ones." Melito does not build a systematic theology of healing in the atonement --- but his instinct is to see Christ's redeeming work as encompassing the whole person: body, soul, freedom, life, and healing together. The raw materials for a healing-in-the-atonement theology are present in Melito, even if the formal structure is not.

This distinction is important for both sides of the debate. The early church could affirm that God heals **and** that healing is not an atonement provision. Those two ideas are not contradictory. God's ability and willingness to heal does not depend on whether healing is "in the atonement." The early church appears to have held precisely this position: God heals, but the atonement addresses sin.

One of the most instructive test cases is Cyprian. Here was a major bishop, writing to a suffering congregation during a literal pandemic, with people dying around him. If there were ever a moment to invoke "by his stripes we are healed" as a promise of physical deliverance, this was it. Cyprian did not. His counsel was to care for the dying, to endure with faith, and to trust in the resurrection. The theology of his response was not healing-in-the-atonement; it was endurance, compassion, and eschatological hope. That pastoral choice, in that extreme circumstance, tells us something about how the pre-Nicene church understood the passage.

On the Jewish side, the interpretive history is equally instructive. The passage was originally read as messianic. The Targum Jonathan --- an ancient Aramaic translation of the Prophets --- opens Isaiah 52:13 with the words "Behold, my servant **the Messiah** shall prosper," inserting the word "Messiah" directly into the text. The Babylonian Talmud preserves a striking tradition in which the Messiah is called "the leper scholar" --- a figure sitting among the sick, his own wounds bound, identified with physical illness, quoting Isaiah 53:4 (Sanhedrin 98b).

The shift to reading the passage as describing "suffering Israel" rather than a suffering Messiah came later --- reaching its decisive expression with the medieval commentator Rashi (c. 1040--1105 AD), the most influential Jewish biblical scholar in history. In Rashi's reading, the Servant is the nation of Israel suffering among the Gentile nations, and the "healing" is the nations' eventual recognition of Israel's role.

This shift correlates with Christianity's use of Isaiah 53 as its central proof text for Jesus as the Messiah. The earlier Jewish readings were messianic. The later readings moved away from that --- partly for internal textual reasons (Israel is called God's "servant" repeatedly throughout Isaiah 40--55), and partly in response to Christian claims. The trajectory itself is a piece of evidence, though it speaks to the identity of the Servant, not directly to the nature of the healing. A reader could fully accept the messianic reading and still hold any of the three views on what "healed" means.

## Three Views the Church Holds Today

Faithful, Bible-believing Christians who love the Lord and honor the Scriptures have arrived at three major positions on this question. Each deserves to be heard at its best --- presented as its own advocates would present it, with its strongest arguments and its honest challenges.

*(Chapter 4 presents each view at full length with complete documentation.)*

### View 1: Physical Healing Is in the Atonement

**The case:** The Hebrew words of Isaiah 53:4 mean sickness and pain. Matthew, under the guidance of the Holy Spirit, applies them to physical healing and deliberately rejects the Septuagint's spiritual rendering. Just as the Servant bore "our transgressions" and "our iniquities" (verse 5), he also bore "our sicknesses" and "our pains" (verse 4). The cross addresses the whole person --- body and soul. The same faith that receives forgiveness can receive healing, because both flow from the same atoning work.

The key texts for this view include Isaiah 53:4--5 with Matthew 8:17, Psalm 103:3 ("Who forgiveth all thine iniquities; who healeth all thy diseases"), and James 5:14--15 ("the prayer of faith shall save the sick, and the Lord shall raise him up"). Exodus 15:26 reveals God's character as healer: "I am the LORD that healeth thee." This is not a minor thread in Scripture --- it is woven through the whole story of God's relationship with his people.

There is a further biblical argument that strengthens this case considerably. When the law pronounces its curses in Deuteronomy 28, it lists diseases by name: "The LORD shall smite thee with a consumption, and with a fever, and with an inflammation" (Deuteronomy 28:22). The chapter culminates: "Also every sickness, and every plague, which is not written in the book of this law, them will the LORD bring upon thee" (Deuteronomy 28:61). The Hebrew word for "sickness" in that verse is *choliy* --- the same word used in Isaiah 53:4, "Surely he hath borne our griefs [*choliy*]." Paul writes: "Christ hath redeemed us from the curse of the law, being made a curse for us: for it is written, Cursed is every one that hangeth on a tree" (Galatians 3:13). The logic is direct: if the curse of the law includes disease, and Christ redeemed us from the curse, then the redemption includes healing from disease. Scholars who hold this view, including Derek Prince and T.L. Osborn, trace a complete arc from Genesis 3 (the curse introduced) through Deuteronomy 28 (the curse codified, with disease named) to Galatians 3:13 (the curse borne by Christ) to Revelation 22:3 ("there shall be no more curse"). Those who disagree counter that Paul's argument in Galatians 3 is specifically about justification --- how Gentiles are included in Abraham's blessing through faith --- not about physical healing. Both readings are exegetically defensible. *(Chapter 1 provides the full curse-and-redemption analysis with all the key passages.)*

This view has deep roots in respected 19th-century evangelicalism. A.J. Gordon, a Baptist pastor and the founder of what became Gordon-Conwell Theological Seminary, wrote *The Ministry of Healing* in 1882 --- a careful, scholarly defense of divine healing. A.B. Simpson, the Presbyterian pastor who founded the Christian and Missionary Alliance, made healing one of four pillars of the gospel. Andrew Murray, the beloved South African devotional writer in the Dutch Reformed tradition, taught that sickness is part of the curse and healing is part of the atonement's provision. Today, more than 600 million Pentecostal and charismatic Christians worldwide hold some form of this conviction. It has fueled some of the most vibrant missionary and church-planting movements in history.

**The honest challenge:** What happens when a sincere, faith-filled believer prays and is not healed? Paul prayed three times for his thorn to be removed, and God said no (2 Corinthians 12:8--9). Paul left his companion Trophimus behind at Miletus because he was sick --- no explanation given, no lack of faith mentioned (2 Timothy 4:20). Epaphroditus "was sick nigh unto death," and Paul attributes his recovery to God's *mercy*, not to faith claimed or a promise exercised (Philippians 2:27). If healing is a provision of the atonement available to all who believe, why were these men --- whose faith cannot be questioned --- not healed?

And when the theology is applied without care, the damage is real. Sick believers have been told that their illness is evidence of insufficient faith, compounding guilt upon suffering. The best advocates of this view --- Gordon, Simpson, Murray --- never taught that. But the distortion has caused real harm, and honesty requires naming it.

### View 2: The Healing Is Spiritual

**The case:** Peter, an apostle who walked with Jesus, quotes Isaiah 53:5 --- "by whose stripes ye were healed" --- and applies it to spiritual healing from sin (1 Peter 2:24). His context is entirely about sin, righteousness, and restoration to the Shepherd of our souls. If the inspired apostle tells us what the verse means, that carries great weight. The immediate context of Isaiah 53:5 supports this reading: the four parallel lines move from "transgressions" to "iniquities" to "peace" to "healed" --- all addressing the spiritual condition of God's people.

A key argument from this tradition concerns the purpose of the miraculous signs. Physical healing during the apostolic era served a specific function: it confirmed the message and authenticated the messengers. Two passages carry the weight of this argument:

> "And they went forth, and preached every where, the Lord working with them, and confirming the word with signs following."
> --- Mark 16:20 (KJV)

> "How shall we escape, if we neglect so great salvation; which at the first began to be spoken by the Lord, and was confirmed unto us by them that heard him; God also bearing them witness, both with signs and wonders, and with divers miracles, and gifts of the Holy Ghost, according to his own will?"
> --- Hebrews 2:3--4 (KJV)

The argument is straightforward: signs had a specific purpose --- confirming the word. The past tense of Hebrews 2:3--4 ("was confirmed") indicates that confirmation has been accomplished. The word has been confirmed. The confirming signs have done their work. God can still heal --- he is sovereign over all things --- but healing is a gift of grace, not a covenant right purchased at the cross.

This position has been held by some of the finest theological minds in church history. B.B. Warfield, the Princeton theologian who cared for his invalid wife for decades, articulated it with both rigor and tenderness in *Counterfeit Miracles* (1918). John MacArthur and the broader Reformed and cessationist tradition carry it forward today. The dispensational and Plymouth Brethren traditions have developed the "confirming the word" argument with particular precision. And this tradition has produced some of the richest theology of suffering in Christian history --- the conviction that God is present in suffering, that suffering produces endurance and character and hope (Romans 5:3--4), and that the ultimate resolution of all pain is not in this life but in the resurrection.

**The honest challenge:** Matthew 8:17 is difficult for this view. An inspired author takes a passage from Isaiah 53, uses his standard fulfillment formula, and applies it to physical healing --- using Greek words that are unambiguously about bodily illness. The cessationist must explain why Matthew's plain language does not mean what it appears to mean. James 5:14--15 presents a further difficulty: it instructs the *church* (not just the apostles) to pray for healing, with no indication that the practice is temporary, and uses confident language --- "the prayer of faith *shall save* the sick, and the Lord *shall raise* him up."

And the global church presents a practical challenge: hundreds of millions of Christians worldwide testify to genuine healings. Craig Keener's two-volume study *Miracles* (2011) documents many such cases with medical corroboration. When healing is relegated entirely to the apostolic past, the lived experience of the majority of the world's Christians requires a comprehensive explanation.

### View 3: Already, but Not Yet

**The case:** The best framework for holding all the evidence together may be the one the New Testament itself provides for the kingdom of God: it is **already here** and **not yet complete**. Christ's atoning work has secured the full redemption of the whole person --- body and soul. But the benefits of that work unfold over time. Forgiveness is received now. Sanctification unfolds throughout the believer's life. Bodily resurrection comes at Christ's return. Physical healing in this present age is a genuine foretaste of the coming restoration --- real, but not guaranteed in every case.

Think of it this way. The benefits of the atonement follow a pattern: justification is past (we have been declared righteous), sanctification is present (we are being made holy), and glorification is future (we will be transformed at the resurrection). Healing follows the same pattern: accomplished at the cross, genuinely available now as the Spirit wills, and fully consummated when Christ returns. The healings we experience in this life are real, but they are firstfruits --- a down payment on the full harvest.

Paul makes this explicit:

> "And not only they, but ourselves also, which have the firstfruits of the Spirit, even we ourselves groan within ourselves, waiting for the adoption, to wit, the redemption of our body."
> --- Romans 8:23 (KJV)

Believers who have the Spirit --- who are genuinely saved and indwelt by God --- are still groaning. They are still waiting for the "redemption of our body." The body is not yet fully redeemed. But it **will** be:

> "It is sown in corruption; it is raised in incorruption: It is sown in dishonour; it is raised in glory: it is sown in weakness; it is raised in power."
> --- 1 Corinthians 15:42--43 (KJV)

The healings we see now are real --- genuine signs that the kingdom has broken in. But they are foretastes, not the full harvest. The full harvest --- permanent, complete, universal freedom from sickness and death --- comes at the resurrection.

This view is held by scholars like D.A. Carson, Wayne Grudem, and Gordon Fee --- a lifelong Pentecostal and world-class New Testament scholar who affirmed that God heals while sharply rejecting the teaching that healing is a guaranteed right. Fee's book *The Disease of the Health and Wealth Gospels* (1985) was a critique that came from *inside* the Pentecostal tradition, not from outside it. He had seen God heal. He also refused to turn that experience into a formula. For Fee, the key text was James 5:14--16: pray for the sick with genuine expectation, but do not make promises that God has not made.

**The honest challenge:** Critics from both sides level the same charge: this view tries to have it both ways. If healing is "available but not guaranteed," how does a believer know when to keep praying and when to accept God's answer? The framework is theologically coherent, but it can feel like a non-answer at two in the morning when the diagnosis is grim and the prayers seem unanswered. And in practice, some churches that officially hold this view rarely pray for healing with genuine expectation --- the "not yet" side dominates, and the "already" side fades into theory.

## What We All Agree On

Before the disagreements loom too large, it is worth standing on the ground that all three views share. This common ground is not trivial. It is substantial, foundational, and pastorally significant.

**God is compassionate toward the sick.** His heart is moved by human suffering. Jesus healed because he was "moved with compassion" (Matthew 14:14). The Psalms declare:

> "The LORD is nigh unto them that are of a broken heart; and saveth such as be of a contrite spirit."
> --- Psalm 34:18 (KJV)

No responsible voice in this debate --- from any tradition --- suggests that God is indifferent to pain. The Servant of Isaiah 53 --- whom Christians have always recognized as Christ, the Son of God --- is "a man of sorrows, and acquainted with grief" (Isaiah 53:3). In the Son, God does not stand at a distance from human suffering. He enters into it.

This is the starting point for all of us, no matter where we land on the question of healing and the atonement.

**Prayer for the sick is biblical and commanded.** James 5:14--15 instructs the church to pray for the sick, and that instruction is addressed to every generation of believers without qualification. The manner and expectation of the prayer may differ between traditions, but the practice itself is non-negotiable.

**God can and does heal.** Even the most careful cessationist pastor does not say "God doesn't heal anymore." God is sovereign. He can heal anyone, at any time. The debate is about the theological framework that explains when and why --- not about whether God has the power or the willingness to act.

**Suffering is not a sign of faithlessness.** Jesus himself settled this: "Neither hath this man sinned, nor his parents" (John 9:3). Paul called suffering a grace-gift (Philippians 1:29). Peter told believers not to be surprised by "the fiery trial" but to see it as participation in Christ's sufferings (1 Peter 4:12--13). The equation of sickness with spiritual failure is contradicted by Christ, by Paul, and by Peter.

**Ultimate bodily healing is guaranteed at the resurrection.** Every tradition --- Pentecostal, Reformed, charismatic, cessationist --- affirms this promise:

> "And God shall wipe away all tears from their eyes; and there shall be no more death, neither sorrow, nor crying, neither shall there be any more pain: for the former things are passed away."
> --- Revelation 21:4 (KJV)

The body will be raised in glory: "It is sown in corruption; it is raised in incorruption: It is sown in dishonour; it is raised in glory: it is sown in weakness; it is raised in power" (1 Corinthians 15:42--43). For the believer who is not healed in this life, this is not a consolation prize. It is the main event. It is the certain, unshakable hope toward which all of redemption is moving --- purchased at the same cross that is at the center of this entire study.

**The person who disagrees with you is not your enemy.** They are your brother or sister, wrestling with the same Scriptures, praying to the same God, sitting at the same bedsides. They have arrived at a different conclusion --- not because they are unfaithful, but because the question is genuinely difficult and the evidence genuinely complex.

## A Word About How We Treat Each Other

This study exists because real people are asking a real question that touches their real lives. And because the stakes are so personal, the potential for harm is high --- on every side.

**The damage of telling the sick they lack faith.** When a believer suffering from cancer is told that their tumor is evidence of insufficient faith, the theology has moved from encouragement to cruelty. When parents of a child with a disability are told that their child's condition reflects their lack of faith, the spiritual damage is devastating. Guilt is compounded on suffering. The church that was supposed to be a refuge becomes a source of shame. It must be said clearly: the best advocates of healing in the atonement do not do this. But the distortion is widespread enough, and its consequences devastating enough, that it must be named.

**The damage of telling the sick God does not heal anymore.** When a suffering believer goes to their pastor hoping for prayer and the anointing described in James 5, and the functional message they receive is "Don't get your hopes up" --- something precious has been lost. The theology technically affirms that God can heal, but when healing is never prayed for with genuine expectation, the sick believer is left with diminished hope. That is a different kind of wound, but it is real.

**The damage of theological ambiguity at the bedside.** And the already/not yet view carries its own pastoral risk. If "healing is available but not guaranteed" becomes a way of saying "I don't really know what to expect," the sick believer receives neither the bold faith of the Pentecostal tradition nor the sturdy comfort of the Reformed tradition. Theological balance is a virtue in a study; it can become a liability in a crisis if it offers sophistication where a person needs a hand to hold and a voice that knows what to say.

Every view, when applied with love and wisdom, produces good fruit. Every view, when applied with arrogance or insensitivity, produces harm. The test is not only whether a theology is correct but whether it leaves the suffering more burdened or more loved.

**A plea for love and humility.** Hold your convictions. Study the Scriptures. Come to your conclusions with the best scholarship and the most honest reading you can bring. But hold those convictions **without contempt** for the brother or sister who has studied the same passages and landed somewhere different. The person in the hospital bed does not need to hear why your theological position is correct. They need to know they are not alone. They need to know they are loved. They need to know that God has not abandoned them.

> "And now abideth faith, hope, charity, these three; but the greatest of these is charity."
> --- 1 Corinthians 13:13 (KJV)

Let love be the first word and the last word. Let theology be the servant of love, not its master.

## A Final Word

The question this study has examined is a real one, and it matters. But the Person at the center of the question matters more.

We have traced the Hebrew words of Isaiah 53. We have followed Matthew into the healing rooms and Peter into the persecuted church. We have listened to the earliest Christians and to the ancient Jewish scholars. We have heard the strongest case for each of three views and named the honest challenges each one faces.

And at the end of all the evidence, the question remains open. It remains open not because the evidence is thin --- it is remarkably rich --- but because it points in more than one direction, and no human interpreter can claim to have cornered the whole truth of a passage this deep.

That is not a failure of this study. It is a reflection of the depth of what happened on the cross. The atonement is not a simple transaction. It is the moment when the eternal Son of God took the full weight of a broken world upon himself --- and the reverberations of that moment are still unfolding in the lives of his people.

Whatever you conclude about Isaiah 53:5 --- whether you believe physical healing is a provision of the cross, a sovereign gift of God's grace, or a genuine foretaste of the coming kingdom --- the Servant who was wounded for your transgressions is not a theological category. He is the living Christ. He is the one who was moved with compassion when he saw the sick. He is the one who wept at the tomb of his friend. He is the one who bore our sins in his own body on the tree. And he is the one who will, one day, wipe every tear from every eye.

Whatever you conclude, do these three things. They are not tied to any one position. They belong to every believer who takes the Scriptures seriously and who loves the people God has placed around them.

**Pray for the sick.** It is commanded. It is an act of love. And the God who hears your prayer is the same God who healed lepers, gave sight to the blind, and raised the dead. Do not let your theology --- whatever it is --- become a reason to stop praying. James did not say, "Is any sick among you? Let him consult his systematic theology." He said, "Let him call for the elders of the church; and let them pray over him" (James 5:14). Pray. Pray with faith. Pray with love. And leave the results to God.

**Love the suffering.** Be present. Weep with those who weep. Bring meals and sit in silence and hold hands in hospital rooms. Do not rush to explain. Do not offer your position paper when what they need is your presence. Sometimes the most Christ-like thing the church can do is not explain but simply show up. The theology can wait. The love cannot.

**Hold fast to the hope of the resurrection.** This is the ground beneath every position in this study. Even if healing does not come in this life, the day is coming when it will come fully, finally, and forever. The body will be raised. Death will be swallowed up in victory. And the former things --- every sickness, every sorrow, every tear --- will pass away. This is the hope that belongs to every believer, from every tradition, without exception. It is grounded in the same cross that is at the center of this whole discussion.

> "And God shall wipe away all tears from their eyes; and there shall be no more death, neither sorrow, nor crying, neither shall there be any more pain: for the former things are passed away."
> --- Revelation 21:4 (KJV)

Until that day, we walk by faith, not by sight. We pray with hope. We hold our convictions with humility. And we love one another --- across every theological boundary --- with the love of the One whose stripes are at the center of this whole discussion.

> "For I reckon that the sufferings of this present time are not worthy to be compared with the glory which shall be revealed in us."
> --- Romans 8:18 (KJV)
