> "Surely he hath borne our griefs, and carried our sorrows: yet we did esteem him stricken, smitten of God, and afflicted. But he was wounded for our transgressions, he was bruised for our iniquities: the chastisement of our peace was upon him; and with his stripes we are healed."
> --- Isaiah 53:4--5 (KJV)

## Why the Earliest Voices Matter

In the SCAR Quadrilateral, Antiquity occupies a distinct role. It does not override Scripture --- nothing does. But it provides something that later centuries cannot: proximity. The Christians who wrote between roughly 96 AD and 325 AD were separated from the apostles by one or two generations at most. Some of them knew apostles personally. They read Isaiah 53 in the same Greek Septuagint that the New Testament authors used. They worshipped in communities that still remembered the apostolic voice firsthand.

Their testimony matters not because they are infallible, but because they are *early*. If the earliest post-apostolic Christians understood Isaiah 53 as teaching physical healing in the atonement, that would be significant evidence in favor of the doctrine. If they understood it primarily as teaching sin-bearing and spiritual restoration, that would also be significant. And if they did not address the question directly --- if they were asking different questions entirely --- that silence, too, tells us something.

The Council of Nicaea (325 AD) provides a natural boundary for this chapter. By the time of Nicaea, Christianity had become the religion of the Roman Empire, and theological discourse had shifted toward the great christological and trinitarian controversies. The writers before Nicaea --- the Apostolic Fathers and the Ante-Nicene Fathers --- operated in a different world: a persecuted, minority church, often under threat of martyrdom, still close to its Jewish roots.

## Two Streams of Interpretation

This chapter examines two streams of evidence:

### 1. Early Christian Writers

The first stream traces how the earliest Christian writers after the apostles understood and applied Isaiah 53. Did they connect the passage to physical healing? Did they see the Servant's suffering as addressing the whole person --- body and soul --- or primarily the soul? Did they develop anything resembling a "healing in the atonement" doctrine?

This stream is divided into two periods:

- **The Apostolic Fathers (c. 100--150 AD)** --- writers like Clement of Rome, Ignatius of Antioch, Polycarp of Smyrna, and the authors of the Epistle of Barnabas and the Didache. These are the earliest post-New Testament Christian voices.
- **The Ante-Nicene Fathers (c. 150--325 AD)** --- writers like Justin Martyr, Irenaeus, Origen, Tertullian, and Cyprian. These are the major theological voices of the pre-Nicene church.

### 2. Jewish and Rabbinic Interpretation

The second stream traces how Jewish interpreters understood Isaiah 53 across three distinct eras:

- **Before Jesus (Second Temple period)** --- What did the Dead Sea Scrolls and intertestamental literature tell us about how Isaiah 53 was read before Christianity existed?
- **At Jesus's time** --- How was the passage understood in the first century? What does the Targum Jonathan reveal? What does the Ethiopian eunuch's question in Acts 8 tell us about the live debate?
- **After Jesus** --- How did Jewish interpretation of Isaiah 53 shift after Christianity adopted the passage as its central proof text for the Messiah?

This rabbinic thread is especially significant because it traces how the *same* passage was interpreted across three eras by readers who shared the same linguistic and cultural heritage as the original author. The trajectory of that interpretive shift --- from messianic readings before and during Jesus's time to the corporate-Israel reading that became dominant afterward --- is itself a piece of evidence, though one that must be handled with care and fairness.

## What This Chapter Can and Cannot Do

**What it can do:** Present the earliest available evidence for how Isaiah 53 was understood, with particular attention to whether the healing language of verses 4--5 was connected to physical healing.

**What it cannot do:** Override Scripture. The Apostolic Fathers, the Ante-Nicene Fathers, and the rabbinic sages were not writing inspired Scripture. They were human interpreters, shaped by their own contexts and concerns. Their testimony informs our reading of the text; it does not determine it.

A reader who finds that the earliest Christians did not teach "healing in the atonement" as a doctrine may conclude that the doctrine is a later development without apostolic support. Another reader may conclude that the early church simply had not yet developed the theological categories to articulate what the text implies. Both responses are reasonable; neither is settled by the historical evidence alone.

This chapter is organized into five sections:

## A Word Before We Begin

The reader should approach this chapter with the same posture of honest inquiry that has governed the study so far. The evidence from antiquity is neither as clean nor as decisive as we might wish. Ancient writers were not addressing our question. They had their own theological concerns --- persecution, heresy, ecclesiology, ethics --- and Isaiah 53 entered their writings in service of those concerns. We are, in a sense, asking their texts to answer a question they were not designed to address.

That limitation is itself instructive. The absence of a clear early-church teaching on "healing in the atonement" may reflect the doctrine's absence from apostolic thought. Or it may reflect the fact that a persecuted church facing martyrdom had more pressing concerns than systematizing the theology of physical healing. The reader must weigh both possibilities.

What follows is the evidence. The reader decides what it means.
