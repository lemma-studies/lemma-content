The Ante-Nicene Fathers represent the next generation of Christian theological reflection. Where the Apostolic Fathers were pastors and bishops writing occasional letters, the Ante-Nicene writers include the first systematic theologians, the first apologists who engaged with pagan philosophy and Jewish objections, and the first commentators who attempted sustained biblical interpretation. Their engagement with Isaiah 53 is more developed, more varied, and --- for our purposes --- more instructive than what we find in the earlier period.

The question remains the same: How did these writers understand the healing language of Isaiah 53:4--5? Did any of them connect "with his stripes we are healed" to physical healing? Did they develop anything resembling a "healing in the atonement" theology? And what was the actual practice of the pre-Nicene church regarding healing?

---

## Justin Martyr (c. 100--165 AD)

### The Man and His Context

Justin Martyr was born in Samaria, educated in Greek philosophy, and converted to Christianity as a young man. He became Christianity's most important early apologist --- that is, a defender of the faith against both pagan critics and Jewish objections. He was martyred in Rome under Emperor Marcus Aurelius around 165 AD.

Justin's most significant work for our study is the *Dialogue with Trypho*, a lengthy account of a conversation between Justin and a Jewish interlocutor named Trypho. In this dialogue, Justin argues at length that Jesus is the Messiah foretold in the Jewish Scriptures. Isaiah 53 is central to his argument.

### Justin's Use of Isaiah 53

Justin quotes and discusses Isaiah 53 more extensively than any other Ante-Nicene writer. His purpose is polemical and apologetic: he is arguing against Trypho's Jewish reading of the passage and insisting that the Suffering Servant is the Messiah --- Jesus of Nazareth.

In *Dialogue with Trypho*, chapter 13, Justin writes:

> For the whole human race will be found to be under a curse. For it is written in the law of Moses, "Cursed is every one that continueth not in all things that are written in the book of the law to do them." And no one has accurately done all... but if the Father of all wished His Christ to take upon Himself the curses of all, knowing that after He had been crucified and was dead He would raise Him up, why do you argue about Him who was willing to suffer these things according to the Father's will, as if He were accursed, and do not rather bewail yourselves? For although His Father caused Him to suffer these things on behalf of the human family, yet you did not do it as carrying out the counsel of God.
>
> --- Justin Martyr, *Dialogue with Trypho* 95 (translation following Roberts-Donaldson)

And in his discussion of the Servant Song specifically:

> The passages, then, which I shall quote from the prophets to prove that Jesus Christ is the Son of God and His Apostle, being first Word, and appearing sometimes in the form of fire, and sometimes in the likeness of angels... and also that He should become passible, and that He suffered --- I shall now proceed to set forth...
>
> "Behold, my Servant shall deal prudently, and shall be exalted, and shall be glorified exceedingly. As many were astonished at Thee --- so marred was His visage before men, and His form before the sons of men --- so shall He sprinkle many nations..."
>
> --- Justin Martyr, *Dialogue with Trypho* 13 (quoting Isaiah 52:13--15)

Justin proceeds through the entire Servant Song, arguing verse by verse that the passage describes Jesus's crucifixion, death, and vindication. His categories are consistently messianic and soteriological: the Servant is the Messiah; the Messiah suffers for the sins of others; the suffering produces justification.

### What Justin Does and Does Not Say About Healing

Justin's extensive engagement with Isaiah 53 is focused entirely on proving two points: (1) the Servant is the Messiah, and (2) the Messiah was prophesied to suffer. He does not develop any theology of physical healing from the passage. The phrase "with his stripes we are healed" is part of the text he quotes, but his commentary connects it to the substitutionary and messianic dimensions of the Servant's work, not to physical healing.

This is significant because Justin is the first Christian writer to systematically *argue* about the meaning of Isaiah 53. He is not merely quoting it in passing (like Clement) or alluding to it typologically (like Barnabas). He is engaging in sustained exegesis --- and in that exegesis, physical healing does not appear.

However, Justin does acknowledge the reality of healing in the Christian community. In his *Second Apology*, he mentions that Christians perform exorcisms and healings:

> For numberless demoniacs throughout the whole world, and in your city, many of our Christian men, exorcising them in the name of Jesus Christ, who was crucified under Pontius Pilate, have healed and do heal, rendering helpless and driving the possessing devils out of the men.
>
> --- Justin Martyr, *Second Apology* 6 (translation following Roberts-Donaldson)

Justin thus affirms that Christians heal --- but he does not connect this practice to Isaiah 53 or to the atonement. Healing happens; but in Justin's theology, it is not grounded in "by his stripes we are healed."

## Melito of Sardis (c. 160--170 AD)

### The Man and His Context

Melito was bishop of Sardis in Asia Minor --- one of the seven churches addressed in Revelation (Rev 3:1--6). Eusebius lists him among the most respected bishops of the late second century and records that Melito made a pilgrimage to the Holy Land and compiled an early list of the Old Testament canon. He was a prolific writer, but most of his works were lost for centuries.

In 1940, a near-complete text of Melito's *Peri Pascha* (*On the Pascha*) was discovered among the Bodmer papyri. This Easter homily, probably delivered around 160--170 AD, proved to be one of the most remarkable documents of early Christianity --- a dramatic, poetic meditation on the Passover and the cross that contains the most comprehensive "exchange" language of any pre-Nicene document.

### The Great Exchange

The climax of Melito's homily is a dramatic catalogue of what Christ accomplished at the cross. The language is breathtaking in its scope --- Christ exchanges the human condition for divine life:

> This is the one who covered death with shame and who plunged the devil into mourning as Moses did Pharaoh. This is the one who smote lawlessness and deprived injustice of its offspring, as Moses deprived Egypt. This is the one who delivered us from slavery into freedom, from darkness into light, from death into life, from tyranny into an eternal kingdom...
>
> --- Melito of Sardis, *Peri Pascha* 68 (translation following Hall)

The four exchanges --- slavery to freedom, darkness to light, death to life, tyranny to eternal kingdom --- map onto the broadest possible understanding of redemption. Melito does not limit Christ's work to the forgiveness of sins. The deliverance encompasses the entire human condition.

Later, in the climactic "I am" declaration, Melito places these words in Christ's mouth:

> When the Lord had clothed himself with humanity, and had suffered for the sake of the sufferer, and had been bound for the sake of the imprisoned, and had been judged for the sake of the condemned, and buried for the sake of the one who was buried, he rose up from the dead, and cried aloud with this voice:
>
> Who is he who contends with me? Let him stand in opposition to me. I set the condemned man free; I gave the dead man life; I raised up the one who had been entombed.
>
> Who is my opponent? I, he says, am the Christ. I am the one who destroyed death, and triumphed over the enemy, and trampled Hades under foot, and bound the strong one, and carried off man to the heights of heaven, I, he says, am the Christ.
>
> Therefore, come, all families of men, you who have been befouled with sins, and receive forgiveness for your sins. I am your forgiveness, I am the passover of your salvation, I am the lamb which was sacrificed for you, I am your ransom, I am your light, I am your saviour, I am your resurrection, I am your king, I am leading you up to the heights of heaven, I will show you the eternal Father, I will raise you up by my right hand.
>
> --- Melito of Sardis, *Peri Pascha* 100--103 (translation following Hall)

### Melito and the Healing Question

Earlier in the homily, in a section indicting Israel for rejecting the one who had blessed them, Melito includes healing among God's acts:

> This is the one who came to you, the one who healed your suffering ones and who resurrected your dead.
>
> --- Melito of Sardis, *Peri Pascha* 86

This brief but significant line places healing alongside resurrection as acts of the Christ. The "suffering ones" who were healed are Israel's sick --- the same people whose diseases fill the healing narratives of the Gospels.

### Why Melito Matters for This Discussion

Melito's *Peri Pascha* is the most important early liturgical text for the healing-in-the-atonement discussion --- not because it teaches the doctrine directly, but because its exchange language is more comprehensive than any other pre-Nicene document. Consider what Melito's Christ claims:

- **"I set the condemned man free"** --- liberation from judgment
- **"I gave the dead man life"** --- victory over death
- **"I raised up the one who had been entombed"** --- resurrection
- **"I am your forgiveness"** --- remission of sins
- **"I am your ransom"** --- substitutionary atonement
- **"I am your resurrection"** --- bodily transformation
- **"I am your king"** --- lordship over all of life

The scope is total. There is no area of the human condition that Melito's Christ does not claim to address. When combined with section 86's explicit mention of healing, the *Peri Pascha* provides the earliest post-apostolic voice that describes the cross's work in terms broad enough to encompass physical healing.

However --- and this distinction matters --- Melito does not build a theology of healing in the atonement. He does not quote Isaiah 53:5 and argue that "by his stripes we are healed" refers to physical healing available now. His language is doxological, not systematic. He is writing a hymn of praise, not a doctrinal treatise. The healing-in-the-atonement position can find raw materials in Melito's comprehensive exchange, but the finished building is not there.

The reader must decide: Does Melito's breathtaking scope point toward a redemption that includes the body now? Or does his exchange language describe the full scope of what will be realized at the resurrection? Both readings are defensible.

## Other Early Liturgical Evidence

The *Peri Pascha* is the most dramatic example, but it is not the only early text that uses exchange language or connects Christ's work to the whole person. Three additional sources deserve brief mention.

### Odes of Solomon, Ode 25 (c. 1st--2nd century AD)

The Odes of Solomon are a collection of early Christian hymns, probably composed in Syriac between the late first and early second century. Their dating and authorship are debated, but they represent some of the earliest surviving Christian worship poetry. Ode 25 contains an explicit reference to healing:

> I was rescued from my bonds and unto thee, my God, I fled: for thou art the right hand of my Salvation and my helper...
>
> I was clothed with the covering of thy Spirit, and thou didst remove from me my raiment of skin. For thy right hand lifted me up and removed sickness from me: and I became mighty in the truth, and holy by thy righteousness; and all my adversaries were afraid of me.
>
> --- Odes of Solomon 25:1--2, 8--10 (translation following Charlesworth)

The phrase "removed sickness from me" places physical healing within a hymn celebrating the totality of God's redemptive work. The "raiment of skin" echoes Genesis 3:21 --- the garments God made for Adam and Eve after the fall --- suggesting that the removal of sickness is part of the reversal of the fall's consequences.

This is significant: an early Christian hymn, sung in worship, includes bodily healing as part of God's saving work. It is not a systematic doctrinal statement, but it reflects the devotional instincts of early believers --- and those instincts connected healing to redemption.

### Epistle to Diognetus (c. 130--200 AD)

The Epistle to Diognetus is an anonymous early Christian apology whose date is debated (estimates range from 130 to 200 AD). Chapter 9 contains one of the most famous exchange passages in early Christian literature:

> He gave His own Son as a ransom for us, the holy One for transgressors, the blameless One for the wicked, the righteous One for the unrighteous... O sweet exchange! O unsearchable operation! O benefits surpassing all expectation! --- that the wickedness of many should be hid in a single righteous One, and that the righteousness of One should justify many transgressors!
>
> --- Epistle to Diognetus 9:2--5 (translation following Lightfoot)

The *admirabile commercium* --- "the sweet exchange" --- became a foundational concept in later Christian theology. In this passage, the exchange is focused on sin and righteousness: Christ's righteousness covers human wickedness. Physical healing is not mentioned. Yet the exchange framework itself --- Christ takes what is ours and gives us what is his --- has the logical capacity to extend beyond sin to include suffering, sickness, and death. The framework that could include healing exists here in seed form, even if the author does not develop it.

### Ignatius of Antioch, *Ephesians* 7:2 (c. 108 AD)

In his letter to the Ephesians, Ignatius describes Christ with a striking medical metaphor:

> There is one Physician who is possessed both of flesh and spirit; both made and not made; God existing in flesh; true life in death; both of Mary and of God; first passible and then impassible --- even Jesus Christ our Lord.
>
> --- Ignatius, *To the Ephesians* 7:2 (translation following Lightfoot)

"One Physician" (*eis iatros*) is one of the earliest christological titles in post-apostolic literature. Ignatius uses medical language to describe Christ's person and work. The "Physician" who is "first passible and then impassible" --- first capable of suffering and then beyond suffering --- bridges the human experience of sickness and the divine reality of wholeness.

Ignatius does not develop this metaphor into a healing-in-the-atonement theology. His letters elsewhere reveal a bishop who desired martyrdom and viewed physical suffering as the path to union with Christ (see the [Apostolic Fathers](/by-his-stripes/03-antiquity/apostolic-fathers/) chapter for the full treatment). But the "one Physician" title reveals a devotional instinct --- Christ as healer of the whole person --- that persisted from the earliest post-apostolic period.

## Irenaeus of Lyon (c. 130--202 AD)

### The Man and His Context

Irenaeus was bishop of Lyon in Gaul (modern France). As a young man, he had heard Polycarp preach --- giving him a connection to the apostolic generation through Polycarp's relationship with the Apostle John. His major work, *Against Heresies* (*Adversus Haereses*), is a massive refutation of Gnostic heresies, particularly the teachings of Valentinus and Marcion. In the process of refuting heresy, Irenaeus develops the most comprehensive theology of any Ante-Nicene writer.

### Irenaeus and Recapitulation

Irenaeus's most important theological concept is *recapitulation* (Greek: *anakephalaiosis*). He teaches that Christ "recapitulates" --- sums up, gathers together, relives and redeems --- all of human experience. Where Adam fell, Christ succeeds. What humanity lost through sin, Christ restores through His incarnation, life, death, and resurrection.

This concept is relevant to the healing question because recapitulation is inherently *comprehensive*. If Christ recapitulates all of human experience, this could theoretically include physical suffering and sickness. Irenaeus writes:

> For it was for this end that the Word of God was made man, and He who was the Son of God became the Son of man, that man, having been taken into the Word, and receiving the adoption, might become the son of God. For by no other means could we have attained to incorruptibility and immortality, unless we had been united to incorruptibility and immortality. But how could we be joined to incorruptibility and immortality, unless, first, incorruptibility and immortality had become that which we also are, so that the corruptible might be swallowed up by incorruptibility, and the mortal by immortality, that we might receive the adoption of sons?
>
> --- Irenaeus, *Against Heresies* 3.19.1 (translation following Roberts-Donaldson)

And on the comprehensiveness of Christ's work:

> He therefore passed through every age, becoming an infant for infants, thus sanctifying infants; a child for children, thus sanctifying those who are of this age, being at the same time made to them an example of piety, righteousness, and submission; a youth for youths, becoming an example to youths, and thus sanctifying them for the Lord. So likewise He was an old man for old men... then, at last, He came on to death itself, that He might be "the first-born from the dead, that in all things He might have the pre-eminence," the Prince of life, existing before all, and going before all.
>
> --- Irenaeus, *Against Heresies* 2.22.4

And regarding the body's redemption:

> For when the flesh that has been moulded is not yet given life by the Spirit, then it is not man; but when it has received the Spirit, it becomes man... Now the soul and the spirit are certainly a part of the man, but certainly not the man; for the perfect man consists in the commingling and the union of the soul receiving the spirit of the Father, and the admixture of that fleshly nature which was moulded after the image of God.
>
> --- Irenaeus, *Against Heresies* 5.6.1

### Irenaeus and Isaiah 53

Irenaeus refers to Isaiah 53 in the context of Christ's suffering and its redemptive effects, but his primary engagement with the passage is christological: demonstrating that the prophets foretold a suffering Messiah. He writes:

> For the Lord, when He was born, was already the Saviour of all, as the Word of God declared in Isaiah: "He bears our sicknesses and carries our sorrows" --- and this is the character of a healer. Now the healers of those who were sick in the flesh gave them healthful remedies...
>
> --- Irenaeus, *Against Heresies* 3.20.3 (paraphrase; Irenaeus draws on Isaiah 53:4 language in the context of Christ's comprehensive redemptive work)

### Why Irenaeus Matters for This Discussion

Irenaeus is the most important Ante-Nicene writer for the healing-in-the-atonement question --- not because he teaches the doctrine, but because his theological framework has the *capacity* to include it. Recapitulation theory holds that Christ takes up all of human experience --- including, potentially, physical suffering and sickness --- and redeems it. If the whole person fell in Adam, the whole person is restored in Christ. This is the theoretical framework that the healing-in-the-atonement position needs.

However, Irenaeus himself does not make the explicit connection. He does not say, "Because Christ recapitulates human experience, physical healing is a provision of the atonement based on Isaiah 53:5." His focus when engaging with Isaiah 53 is on sin-bearing, messianic fulfillment, and the refutation of Gnostic claims that the material body is irredeemable. The body's redemption, for Irenaeus, is fully realized in the resurrection, not in a present guarantee of physical healing.

The honest assessment is this: Irenaeus provides the theological *raw materials* from which a healing-in-the-atonement theology could be constructed, but he does not construct it himself. The reader must decide whether to see Irenaeus as a seed that later blossomed or as a framework that points in a different direction than the healing-in-the-atonement tradition would take it.

## Origen of Alexandria (c. 184--253 AD)

### The Man and His Context

Origen was the most brilliant and the most controversial theologian of the pre-Nicene church. Born in Alexandria, he was a prolific writer, a daring speculative thinker, and the first Christian to produce a systematic commentary on large portions of Scripture. Some of his later speculations were condemned as heretical (particularly his ideas about the pre-existence of souls and universal restoration), but his biblical scholarship and his influence on subsequent Christian thought are enormous.

### Origen's Allegorical Method

Origen is known above all for his allegorical approach to Scripture. He distinguished three levels of meaning in biblical texts: the literal (or historical), the moral, and the spiritual (or allegorical). When he encounters physical language in the prophets --- including the language of sickness and healing --- he consistently seeks the deeper, spiritual meaning behind the physical imagery.

### Origen on Isaiah 53

Origen engages with Isaiah 53 in his treatise *Contra Celsum* (*Against Celsus*), a defense of Christianity against the pagan critic Celsus. In this work, Origen addresses Celsus's mockery of a crucified God by pointing to Isaiah 53 as prophetic testimony that the Messiah was destined to suffer. He writes:

> But we say to each of those who object against us: "Who hath believed our report, and to whom is the arm of the LORD revealed?" And in Isaiah too: "He hath no form nor comeliness, and when we see Him, there is no beauty that we should desire Him. He was despised and rejected of men, a man of sorrows and acquainted with grief." And that the Servant of God bears our sins, and suffers on our behalf --- "He was wounded for our transgressions, He was bruised for our iniquities, the chastisement of our peace was upon Him, and by His stripes we are healed."
>
> --- Origen, *Contra Celsum* 1.54--55 (adapted from Chadwick translation)

### Origen's Spiritual Reading of Healing

Consistent with his allegorical method, Origen reads the healing language of Isaiah 53 as referring to spiritual restoration. In his understanding, the "sickness" that the Servant bears is the sickness of sin, and the "healing" that results is the healing of the soul from its moral and spiritual disease. He writes in his *Homilies*:

> Every soul that has sinned is sick. It is the Saviour who heals the sick soul, not the sick body. Or rather, He does heal the body too --- but the body in that it is an image of the soul. The diseases of the body represent the diseases of the soul, and the healing of the body represents the healing of the soul.
>
> --- Origen, *Homilies on Leviticus* 8.1 (adapted translation)

For Origen, physical language in Scripture almost always points to spiritual reality. When Isaiah says "by his stripes we are healed," Origen understands this as the healing of the soul from the wounds of sin. Physical healing, while real, is secondary --- a shadow of the deeper, spiritual reality.

### Origen and the Practice of Healing

Despite his strongly spiritual interpretation, Origen acknowledges that Christians in his day practiced prayer for healing and experienced divine healing. In *Contra Celsum*, he writes:

> And some give evidence of their having received through this faith a marvellous power by the cures which they perform, invoking no other name over those who need their help than that of the God of all things, and of Jesus, along with a mention of His history. For by these means we too have seen many persons freed from grievous calamities, and from distractions of mind, and madness, and countless other ills.
>
> --- Origen, *Contra Celsum* 3.24 (translation following Chadwick)

This is important: Origen witnesses to the reality of healing in the third-century church while maintaining a spiritual interpretation of Isaiah 53's healing language. For Origen, the fact that God heals does not mean that Isaiah 53:5 is about physical healing. God heals because God is compassionate and powerful --- not because physical healing is a specific provision of the atonement as described in Isaiah 53.

## Tertullian (c. 155--220 AD)

### The Man and His Context

Tertullian of Carthage was the first major Christian theologian to write in Latin (rather than Greek). A brilliant polemicist and rhetorician, he shaped Western Christian vocabulary --- the Latin word *trinitas* (Trinity) enters theology through Tertullian. Later in life, he was attracted to Montanism, a charismatic movement that emphasized prophetic gifts and strict moral discipline.

### Tertullian on Suffering and the Body

Tertullian's writings touch on suffering, the body, and healing in several contexts. In *De Patientia* (*On Patience*), he develops a theology of patient endurance that reflects the persecution context of North African Christianity:

> If we bear all with patience, blessed shall we be in every way. But who are we to receive patience, unless God first impart it to us? And who more fitted to endure suffering than He who first endured it in order to give it? The Lord Himself suffered every form of contumely and abuse. He was spat upon, He was scourged. Yet He taught patience by His own patience.
>
> --- Tertullian, *De Patientia* 3 (adapted from Holmes translation)

Tertullian also affirmed the goodness and redemption of the body against Gnostic opponents. In *De Resurrectione Carnis* (*On the Resurrection of the Flesh*), he argues at length that the body will be raised and redeemed:

> The flesh is the hinge of salvation. When the soul is enrolled in the service of God, it is the flesh which makes it capable of such service. The flesh, indeed, is washed, that the soul may be cleansed; the flesh is anointed, that the soul may be consecrated; the flesh is signed, that the soul may be guarded; the flesh is shadowed by the imposition of hands, that the soul may be illuminated by the Spirit; the flesh feeds on the body and blood of Christ, that the soul may fatten on God.
>
> --- Tertullian, *De Resurrectione Carnis* 8

### Tertullian and Isaiah 53

Tertullian engages with Isaiah 53 primarily in his anti-Jewish polemical work *Adversus Judaeos* (*Against the Jews*) and in *Adversus Marcionem* (*Against Marcion*). In both, his purpose is to demonstrate that the Messiah was prophesied to suffer --- against Jewish interpreters who expected a triumphant Messiah, and against Marcion who denied the Old Testament's relevance.

His reading of Isaiah 53 follows the established pattern: the passage predicts Christ's suffering, death, and vindication. The categories are messianic and soteriological. He does not develop a healing-in-the-atonement theology from the passage.

### Tertullian's Montanist Phase

Tertullian's later attraction to Montanism is worth noting. The Montanists emphasized the continuing work of the Holy Spirit, including prophetic gifts. While the movement was controversial, its existence demonstrates that charismatic experiences --- including, potentially, healing --- were part of the landscape of the second and third-century church. Tertullian's interest in Montanism suggests he was drawn to a more experiential and Spirit-centered Christianity than the institutional mainstream.

However, even in his Montanist phase, Tertullian did not develop a theology connecting physical healing to the atonement through Isaiah 53. His engagement with the passage remained christological and apologetic.

## Cyprian of Carthage (c. 200--258 AD)

### The Man and His Context

Cyprian was bishop of Carthage in North Africa --- a position of enormous influence in the Western church. He became bishop around 248 AD and was martyred during the Valerian persecution in 258 AD. His writings are deeply pastoral, addressing the practical challenges facing a church under intermittent persecution.

### De Mortalitate (On the Plague)

Cyprian's most relevant work for our study is *De Mortalitate* (*On the Plague* or *On Mortality*), written around 252 AD during the devastating Plague of Cyprian --- a pandemic that may have been smallpox or a hemorrhagic fever. The plague killed vast numbers across the Roman Empire, including Christians. Cyprian's pastoral response to this mass death is one of the most instructive early documents for the healing debate.

Cyprian writes to his congregation:

> This trial, that now the bowels, relaxed into a constant flux, discharge the bodily strength; that a fire originated in the marrow ferments into wounds of the fauces; that the intestines are shaken with a continual vomiting; that the eyes are on fire with the injected blood; that in some cases the feet or some parts of the limbs are taken off by the contagion of diseased putrefaction...
>
> --- Cyprian, *De Mortalitate* 14 (translation following Deferrari)

In the face of this horror, Cyprian does *not* tell his congregation to claim healing through the atonement. He does *not* appeal to Isaiah 53:5 as a promise that believers should expect physical healing. Instead, his pastoral counsel runs in a strikingly different direction:

> What a grandeur of spirit it is to struggle with all the powers of an unshaken mind against so many onsets of devastation and death! What sublimity, to stand erect among the desolation of the human race, and not to lie prostrate with those who have no hope in God! ... Our brethren who have been freed from the world by the summons of the Lord should not be mourned, since we know that they are not lost but sent before.
>
> --- Cyprian, *De Mortalitate* 2

And further:

> How suitable, how necessary it is that this plague and pestilence, which seems horrible and deadly, searches out the righteousness of each one, and examines the minds of the human race, to see whether they who are in health tend the sick; whether relatives affectionately love their kindred; whether masters pity their languishing servants; whether physicians do not forsake the beseeching patients...
>
> --- Cyprian, *De Mortalitate* 16

### Cyprian's Theological Framework

Cyprian's response to the plague reveals his theological framework for suffering and sickness:

1. **Suffering is not punishment for believers.** Cyprian explicitly rejects the idea that the plague represents God's anger against His people. The rain falls on the just and the unjust alike.

2. **Suffering tests and refines faith.** The plague reveals whether Christians truly trust God, whether they practice compassion, and whether their hope extends beyond this life.

3. **Death is liberation, not defeat.** For the Christian, death is departure to be with Christ. Cyprian urges his people not to fear death but to see it as the entrance into the promised inheritance.

4. **Christians are distinguished by how they respond to suffering, not by their exemption from it.** The pagan world fled from the sick. Christians cared for the dying --- often at the cost of their own lives. This compassion, not physical deliverance, was the mark of Christian faithfulness.

Cyprian's *De Mortalitate* is significant precisely because of what it does *not* say. In a document specifically addressing mass physical suffering, written by a major bishop to a suffering congregation, there is no appeal to healing as a provision of the atonement. There is no invocation of Isaiah 53:5 as a promise of physical deliverance. The theological framework is endurance, compassion, hope, and the superiority of eternal life over temporal existence.

## The Practice of Healing Prayer in the Pre-Nicene Church

### Healing Was Practiced

It would be a mistake to conclude from the above survey that the early church did not believe in or practice healing. The evidence is clear: Christians prayed for healing, experienced healings, and understood healing as a work of God through the name of Jesus Christ.

The *Apostolic Tradition* (attributed to Hippolytus of Rome, c. 215 AD) includes specific prayers for the gift of healing:

> If anyone among the laity appears to have received a gift of healing by a revelation, hands shall not be laid on him, because the matter is manifest.
>
> --- *Apostolic Tradition* 15 (adapted from Dix/Chadwick reconstruction)

This passage reveals that healing gifts were recognized in the early third-century church. The community acknowledged individuals who possessed a "gift of healing" and did not require formal ordination for the exercise of that gift. Healing was a recognized charism --- a spiritual gift operative in the community.

### Healing Was Not Grounded in Isaiah 53

The critical observation is this: while the early church clearly practiced healing, it did not ground that practice in a systematic theology of the atonement derived from Isaiah 53:5. Healing was understood as:

- A gift of the Holy Spirit (following 1 Corinthians 12:9)
- An expression of God's compassion and power
- A demonstration of Christ's authority, exercised through His name
- A sign authenticating the Christian message

What it was *not* understood as --- at least not in any surviving pre-Nicene document --- was a guaranteed provision of the atonement, secured by Christ's suffering and available to every believer on the basis of Isaiah 53:5.

This distinction matters: the early church could affirm both that God heals *and* that healing is not an atonement provision. These are not contradictory positions. God's ability and willingness to heal does not depend on whether healing is "in the atonement." The early church appears to have held precisely this position: God heals, but the atonement addresses sin.

## Summary Assessment

### The Consistent Pattern

Like the Apostolic Fathers before them, the Ante-Nicene writers read Isaiah 53 through the lens of sin-bearing and messianic fulfillment, not physical healing:

- **Justin Martyr** uses Isaiah 53 extensively in Jewish-Christian apologetics, always to prove that the Messiah was destined to suffer. Physical healing is not part of his exegesis, though he acknowledges that Christians practice healing.

- **Melito of Sardis** produces the most comprehensive exchange language of any pre-Nicene writer. His *Peri Pascha* describes Christ's work in terms broad enough to encompass physical healing (§86: "healed your suffering ones"), but his language is doxological, not systematic. He does not build a healing-in-the-atonement theology from Isaiah 53:5.

- **The early liturgical evidence** --- the Odes of Solomon ("removed sickness from me"), the Epistle to Diognetus ("O sweet exchange!"), Ignatius's "one Physician" --- reveals devotional instincts that connected Christ's redemptive work to the whole person, including the body. These are seeds, not doctrines. None of these texts constructs a theology of healing in the atonement, but they show that early believers' worship instincts reached beyond sin alone.

- **Irenaeus** develops a comprehensive theology of recapitulation that *could* include physical restoration --- but he does not explicitly connect it to Isaiah 53:5. The body's full restoration, in Irenaeus's framework, belongs to the resurrection.

- **Origen** reads Isaiah 53's healing language as spiritual and allegorical, consistent with his overall hermeneutic. He acknowledges that Christians experience physical healings but does not ground this in the atonement.

- **Tertullian** engages Isaiah 53 apologetically and christologically. Even in his Montanist phase, with its openness to charismatic experience, he does not develop a healing-in-the-atonement theology from the passage.

- **Cyprian**, writing during an actual plague, provides the most instructive pastoral test case. His response to mass sickness and death does not include any appeal to healing as an atonement provision. His framework is endurance, compassion, and eschatological hope.

### The Melito and Irenaeus Exceptions

Two figures stand apart from the pattern. **Melito of Sardis** provides the earliest and most dramatic exchange language --- Christ delivering from slavery, darkness, death, and tyranny, and claiming to be "your forgiveness, your ransom, your resurrection, your king." His *Peri Pascha* is the one pre-Nicene text whose scope explicitly includes healing (§86). **Irenaeus of Lyon** provides the most developed theological framework --- recapitulation, in which Christ takes up and redeems all of human experience. If the whole person fell in Adam, the whole person is restored in Christ.

Together, Melito and Irenaeus provide the raw materials from which a healing-in-the-atonement theology could be constructed. But neither writer builds that structure himself. Melito's language is doxological, not systematic. Irenaeus focuses the body's full restoration on the future resurrection. The reader who wishes to appeal to either writer for the healing-in-the-atonement position is appealing to the *implications* of their theology, not to their *stated conclusions*.

### Practice Versus Theology

The most nuanced point to emerge from this survey is the distinction between *practice* and *theology*. The pre-Nicene church practiced healing. Christians prayed for the sick, exercised gifts of healing, and experienced God's miraculous intervention. This is well-documented and not in dispute.

But the theological grounding for that practice was not Isaiah 53:5. The early church's healing practice was grounded in the gifts of the Spirit, in the authority of Christ's name, and in the compassion of God --- not in a systematic connection between the atonement and physical healing.

This observation is important for both sides of the debate:

- For those who hold that healing is in the atonement: the early church practiced healing without making this theological connection. This does not disprove the connection, but it does suggest that the connection is a later theological development, not an apostolic or immediately post-apostolic one.

- For those who deny that healing is in the atonement: the early church practiced healing vigorously. The absence of a healing-in-the-atonement theology did not produce a church that was indifferent to physical healing. God's healing work was recognized and celebrated, even without being grounded in Isaiah 53.

The evidence from the Ante-Nicene Fathers is a significant piece of the puzzle. It does not resolve the debate, but it sharpens the questions that the reader must answer.
