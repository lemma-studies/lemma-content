The previous two sections traced the Jewish interpretation of Isaiah 53 from the pre-Christian period through the first century. This final section follows that trajectory into the post-Christian era: the Talmudic period (c. 200--600 AD), the medieval Jewish commentators, and the modern scholarly conversation. The story is one of significant shift --- from a messianic reading that was well-attested in earlier sources to a corporate-Israel reading that became dominant in mainstream Judaism. That shift is itself a piece of evidence in the larger debate, though one that must be interpreted with care and fairness.

The question for this section is not simply "who is right?" The question is: what does the trajectory of Jewish interpretation tell us about the passage itself, and does it illuminate the specific question of whether "with his stripes we are healed" refers to physical healing, spiritual healing, or both?

---

## The Talmudic Evidence

### Babylonian Talmud, Sanhedrin 98b: The Leper Scholar

The most remarkable Talmudic passage for our study is found in the Babylonian Talmud, tractate Sanhedrin 98b. In a discussion about the Messiah's name and nature, several rabbis offer different identifications. Then this exchange occurs:

> The Rabbis said: His name is "the leper scholar," as it is written, "Surely he hath borne our griefs, and carried our sorrows: yet we did esteem him stricken, smitten of God, and afflicted" [Isaiah 53:4].
>
> Rav said: [The Messiah] --- what is he like? --- He is like the leper scholar. As it was taught: Rabbi Yehoshua ben Levi found Elijah standing at the entrance to the cave of Rabbi Shimon bar Yochai... He said to him: "When will the Messiah come?" He said to him: "Go and ask him yourself." "Where is he sitting?" "At the entrance to the city." "And what are his signs?" "He is sitting among the poor who suffer from sicknesses. All of them untie and retie [their bandages] all at once, but he unties and reties one at a time, saying: 'Perhaps I will be needed; I must not be delayed.'"
>
> --- Babylonian Talmud, Sanhedrin 98b (adapted from Soncino/Steinsaltz translation)

This passage is extraordinary for several reasons:

**1. It applies Isaiah 53:4 to the Messiah.** The rabbis explicitly quote Isaiah 53:4 --- "Surely he hath borne our griefs, and carried our sorrows: yet we did esteem him stricken, smitten of God, and afflicted" --- and use it to describe the Messiah. This is a *messianic* reading of Isaiah 53 in rabbinic literature, preserved in the authoritative Babylonian Talmud.

**2. It identifies the Messiah with sickness.** The Messiah is called "the leper scholar" --- a figure who is himself afflicted with illness. He sits among the sick, binding his own wounds. The Messiah does not heal sickness from a distance; he is *identified with* sickness. He sits among those who suffer.

**3. The Messiah is ready to act.** The detail about binding his wounds "one at a time" is significant: he is always ready to respond when called. His identification with the sick does not render him passive. He is actively waiting, always prepared to go when needed.

**4. It preserves the physical-sickness dimension.** The passage uses Isaiah 53:4 in a context of physical illness --- leprosy, sickness, bandages. This is not an allegorical or spiritual reading. The rabbis understood the "griefs" and "sorrows" of Isaiah 53:4 as including physical sickness, and they connected this to the Messiah.

### Other Talmudic References

The Sanhedrin 98b passage is not the only Talmudic text that connects the Messiah with suffering or sickness:

**Sanhedrin 93b** discusses the Messiah's ability to judge by scent (rather than by sight), drawing on Isaiah 11:3. In the broader context, the Messiah is presented as a figure of discernment and authority, but the discussion implies that he may not operate by ordinary physical means --- a possible hint at the Messiah's own affliction.

**Sukkah 52a** contains the tradition of Messiah ben Joseph --- the messianic figure who dies in battle. This tradition, discussed in the [previous section on pre-Christian Judaism](/by-his-stripes/03-antiquity/rabbinic-pre-jesus/), preserves a Jewish concept of a dying Messiah, though distinct from the Suffering Servant of Isaiah 53. The death of Messiah ben Joseph is a military death, not a substitutionary atoning death. Nonetheless, the existence of this tradition demonstrates that the concept of a suffering and dying messianic figure was part of the rabbinic theological landscape.

**Berakhot 5a** contains a discussion of "chastisements of love" (*yissurim shel ahavah*) --- the idea that suffering can be a sign of God's special attention and discipline rather than punishment. This concept is relevant because it provides a rabbinic framework for understanding suffering as purposeful and beneficial --- a framework that resonates with the Servant's suffering in Isaiah 53.

### What the Talmudic Evidence Shows

The Talmudic evidence demonstrates that even after Christianity had adopted Isaiah 53 as its central messianic proof text, some rabbinic voices maintained a messianic reading of the passage --- a reading that included the Messiah's identification with sickness and suffering. This is significant because it shows that the messianic interpretation of Isaiah 53 was not simply a Christian invention. Jewish scholars, writing centuries after Jesus and fully aware of Christian claims, still found a messianic reading in the text.

However, the Talmudic reading differs from the Christian reading in important ways. In Sanhedrin 98b, the Messiah is not suffering *for* others in a substitutionary sense --- he is sitting *among* the sick, identified with them. The Talmudic Messiah is compassionate and present with the suffering, not bearing their suffering in their place. The substitutionary dimension that is central to the Christian reading of Isaiah 53 is largely absent from the Talmudic evidence.

For the healing question specifically, the Sanhedrin 98b passage is noteworthy because it uses the physical-sickness language of Isaiah 53:4 in a context of actual physical illness. The rabbis did not spiritualize the "griefs and sorrows" of Isaiah 53:4; they understood them as including physical sickness. This reading aligns more closely with the NASB (2020) rendering ("sicknesses... pains") and with Matthew's application in Matthew 8:17 than with the KJV's "griefs and sorrows" or the LXX's "sins."

## Rashi (1040--1105 AD)

### The Man and His Influence

Rabbi Shlomo Yitzchaki, known universally as Rashi, is the most influential Jewish biblical commentator in history. Born in Troyes, France, he produced commentaries on nearly the entire Hebrew Bible and the Babylonian Talmud that became the standard reference for Jewish learning. His commentary on the Torah and Prophets is printed alongside the biblical text in virtually every edition of the Hebrew Bible used in Jewish study to this day.

Rashi's commentary on Isaiah 53 marks the decisive turning point in mainstream Jewish interpretation of the passage.

### Rashi's Commentary on Isaiah 53

Rashi identifies the Suffering Servant not as the Messiah but as the nation of Israel. In his commentary, Israel is the Servant who suffers among the nations, and the "we" who speak in Isaiah 53 are the Gentile nations who witness Israel's suffering and finally recognize its significance.

On Isaiah 52:13, Rashi writes:

> "Behold, my servant shall prosper" --- this refers to the righteous ones among Israel, or to Israel as a whole in their exile.
>
> --- Rashi on Isaiah 52:13 (adapted translation)

On Isaiah 53:3--4, Rashi explains:

> He was despised --- the people of Israel were despised in the eyes of the nations... "Surely he hath borne our griefs" --- the nations will say of Israel: surely the sickness that should have come upon us, he bore; and the pain that we should have suffered, he endured. But we thought that he was stricken by God for his own sins, not knowing that he was afflicted for our transgressions.
>
> --- Rashi on Isaiah 53:3--4 (adapted translation)

On Isaiah 53:5:

> "But he was wounded for our transgressions" --- the nations will confess that the people of Israel were wounded and crushed because of the sins of the nations. The chastisement that should have come upon the nations, to bring them peace, was laid upon Israel; and through Israel's wounds, the nations were healed.
>
> --- Rashi on Isaiah 53:5 (adapted translation)

### The Logic of Rashi's Reading

Rashi's interpretation is internally coherent. It draws on a legitimate feature of Isaiah 40--55: throughout this section, Israel is repeatedly called God's "servant" (Isaiah 41:8, 44:1, 44:21, 45:4, 48:20, 49:3). The designation of Israel as God's servant is a major theme. Rashi reasons that since the servant in the surrounding context is Israel, the servant in Isaiah 52:13--53:12 should also be Israel.

In Rashi's reading, the passage describes Israel's suffering among the nations during exile. The nations, who once despised Israel and assumed God had abandoned them, will eventually recognize that Israel's suffering was vicarious --- that Israel bore suffering that should have come upon the nations. The "healing" of Isaiah 53:5 is the healing of the nations through Israel's redemptive suffering.

### Rashi's Impact

Rashi's commentary on Isaiah 53 became the dominant Jewish interpretation. Before Rashi, the messianic reading was well-attested in Jewish sources (as we have documented in this chapter). After Rashi, the corporate-Israel reading became mainstream. His influence was so great that subsequent Jewish commentators who disagreed with him on this point often felt compelled to address his interpretation before offering their own.

The corporate-Israel reading remains the standard Jewish interpretation today. It is taught in synagogues, printed in Jewish study Bibles, and presented in Jewish-Christian dialogues as the traditional Jewish understanding of the passage.

## Why the Shift?

### The Role of Christian Apologetics

Was Rashi's shift from a messianic to a corporate reading of Isaiah 53 driven by the Christian use of the passage? This is a historically significant and sensitive question that must be handled with honesty and fairness.

The evidence suggests that Christian apologetic use of Isaiah 53 was a *significant factor* in the shift, though not the *only* factor.

**Evidence that Christian pressure played a role:**

1. **The timing is suggestive.** Rashi lived in eleventh-century France, where Christian-Jewish polemics were intense. The Crusades were beginning. Christian missionaries and polemicists regularly cited Isaiah 53 as proof that Jesus was the prophesied Messiah. Rashi was deeply aware of Christian interpretive claims and explicitly addressed them in his commentaries on other passages.

2. **Earlier Jewish sources maintained the messianic reading.** As documented throughout this chapter, the Targum Jonathan, the Talmud (Sanhedrin 98b), and other pre-Rashi sources identified the Servant of Isaiah 53 as the Messiah. The shift to a corporate reading represents a departure from these earlier traditions, and the most obvious external pressure driving that departure was Christianity.

3. **Rashi explicitly engaged with Christian interpretation elsewhere.** While Rashi does not always state his polemical purpose overtly, scholars have documented numerous places in his commentary where he is responding to Christian readings. His interpretation of Isaiah 53 fits this broader pattern.

**Evidence that internal factors also played a role:**

1. **The corporate-Israel reading has genuine textual support.** Israel is called God's "servant" throughout Isaiah 40--55. The corporate reading is not a forced or artificial interpretation; it draws on real features of the text. Even without Christian pressure, a reader could arrive at this interpretation.

2. **The corporate reading addresses a real interpretive problem.** Isaiah 53 describes the Servant as dying and being buried (53:8--9) and then seeing offspring and prolonging his days (53:10). If the Servant is an individual, this creates a tension: how can a dead person see offspring? The corporate reading resolves this tension naturally: the nation suffers, but the nation endures and is restored.

3. **Jewish interpreters had legitimate theological reasons to resist the messianic reading.** The messianic reading, as developed by Christian interpreters, implied that the Messiah had already come and that the Jewish people had failed to recognize him. For Jewish scholars, rejecting this christological reading was not merely polemical; it was a matter of theological integrity within their own tradition.

### A Fair Assessment

The honest conclusion is that both internal and external factors contributed to the shift. The corporate-Israel reading is a legitimate interpretive option with real textual support. But the timing, the historical context, and the pattern of Rashi's other commentaries strongly suggest that Christian apologetic pressure was a significant catalyst for the shift from a messianic to a corporate reading.

This conclusion must be stated carefully. It does not mean the corporate reading is "wrong" simply because it arose partly in response to Christianity. Nor does it mean the messianic reading is "right" simply because it was earlier. But it does suggest that the interpretive trajectory --- from messianic readings before and during the time of Jesus, to corporate readings after Christianity adopted the passage --- is a piece of evidence that the reader should weigh.

## Modern Jewish Scholarship

### The Range of Views

Modern Jewish scholarship on Isaiah 53 is more nuanced and varied than the simple messianic-vs-corporate dichotomy might suggest. Several positions are represented in contemporary academic and religious discourse:

**1. The traditional corporate reading (Rashi's position).** This remains the dominant interpretation in mainstream Judaism. The Servant is Israel. The nations are the speakers. The suffering is Israel's exile and persecution. The healing is the nations' eventual recognition of Israel's role. This reading is presented in standard Jewish commentaries and in most Jewish-Christian dialogues.

**2. Acknowledgment of the messianic elements.** Some modern Jewish scholars, while maintaining the corporate reading as their primary interpretation, acknowledge that the passage contains features that resist a purely corporate reading. The Servant's sinlessness (53:9 --- "he had done no violence, neither was any deceit in his mouth"), his silent submission (53:7), and his death and burial (53:8--9) are difficult to map onto the nation of Israel as a whole. These scholars may hold that the passage has both corporate and individual dimensions.

**3. The academic middle ground.** In the world of academic biblical scholarship (which includes both Jewish and Christian scholars), there is a growing recognition that Isaiah 53 may operate on multiple levels. The Servant may be both Israel and an individual within Israel --- a representative figure who embodies the nation's calling and suffering. This reading allows for both the corporate and the messianic dimensions without requiring an either-or choice.

**4. Historical-critical readings.** Some scholars read Isaiah 53 purely in its historical context, without reference to either messianic or corporate-Israel theology. The Servant may be an anonymous prophetic figure, or the prophet Isaiah himself, or an unknown historical individual. This approach brackets the theological question entirely and focuses on the passage's original literary and historical setting.

### The Jewish-Christian Dialogue

The academic Jewish-Christian dialogue on Isaiah 53 has become significantly more nuanced in recent decades. Both Jewish and Christian scholars increasingly recognize that the passage is genuinely complex and that neither community's traditional reading exhausts its meaning. This does not mean the two communities have reached agreement --- they have not --- but it does mean that the conversation has moved beyond simple polemics.

For the healing question specifically, the modern scholarly conversation offers little direct help. Neither Jewish nor Christian academic scholars have focused extensively on whether "with his stripes we are healed" refers to physical or spiritual healing. The scholarly debate has centered on the Servant's identity (individual vs. corporate) and the passage's historical context, not on the specific nature of the "healing."

## The Interpretive Trajectory as Evidence

### Tracing the Arc

The evidence from the three rabbinic sections of this chapter traces a clear trajectory:

1. **Pre-Christian Judaism** (Second Temple period): The Dead Sea Scrolls and related literature reveal messianic expectations that include healing and restoration. The concept of vicarious, atoning suffering was recognized. The text of Isaiah 53 was preserved accurately and presumably studied. A fully developed "suffering Messiah" doctrine is not clearly attested, but the individual components are present.

2. **At Jesus's time** (first century): The Targum Jonathan identifies Isaiah 53 as messianic but removes the suffering from the Messiah. The Ethiopian eunuch's question shows the passage was debated. The LXX translators spiritualized the physical-sickness language. First-century Jews expected the Messiah to heal.

3. **After Jesus** (Talmudic period): The Babylonian Talmud preserves a messianic reading that includes the Messiah's identification with sickness (Sanhedrin 98b). But the broader trajectory moves toward Rashi's corporate-Israel reading, which becomes dominant.

4. **Medieval and modern Judaism**: The corporate-Israel reading dominates mainstream Jewish interpretation. The messianic reading is acknowledged by some scholars but is not the standard view.

### What the Trajectory Proves

**It proves that the messianic reading is ancient.** The identification of Isaiah 53's Servant with the Messiah did not originate with Christianity. It is attested in the Targum Jonathan, in the Talmud, and (arguably) in pre-Christian sources. When Christians applied Isaiah 53 to Jesus, they were drawing on an existing Jewish interpretive tradition, not inventing one.

**It proves that the shift away from the messianic reading correlates with Christianity's use of the passage.** The timing is unmistakable: the corporate-Israel reading became dominant after Christianity adopted the messianic reading as its central proof text. This does not prove that the shift was *caused* by Christianity (as discussed above, internal factors also contributed), but the correlation is strong.

**It suggests that the original, pre-Christian understanding of Isaiah 53 was messianic.** The convergence of early evidence --- Targum, Talmud, Qumran --- points toward a messianic reading as the earlier and more widespread interpretation. The corporate reading, while textually defensible, appears to be a later development that gained dominance partly in response to Christian claims.

### What the Trajectory Does Not Prove

**It does not prove that "healing is in the atonement."** Even if the original reading of Isaiah 53 was messianic --- even if the passage describes a Messiah who suffers vicariously for others --- this does not automatically resolve the physical-vs-spiritual healing question. A messianic reading of Isaiah 53 is compatible with a purely spiritual understanding of "healed" in verse 5. The messianic identification of the Servant tells us *who* suffers; it does not, by itself, tell us what the *healing* includes.

**It does not prove that the corporate-Israel reading is wrong.** The corporate reading has genuine textual support (the servant-Israel language throughout Isaiah 40--55) and addresses real interpretive challenges. The fact that it gained prominence partly due to Christian pressure does not invalidate it as an interpretation.

**It does not resolve the healing debate.** The trajectory from messianic to corporate readings is relevant to the *identity* of the Servant, not to the *nature* of the healing. A reader could accept the messianic reading of Isaiah 53 (the Servant is the Messiah) and still hold any of the three positions on healing: spiritual only, physical and spiritual, or already/not yet.

## Rabbinic Thought on Healing and Atonement

### Yissurim shel Ahavah: Chastisements of Love

One of the most distinctive contributions of rabbinic theology to the suffering question is the concept of *yissurim shel ahavah* --- "chastisements of love." This teaching, found in the Babylonian Talmud (Berakhot 5a), holds that suffering can be a sign of God's special love and attention rather than His punishment:

> Rava, and some say Rav Chisda, said: If a person sees that suffering comes upon him, let him examine his deeds... If he examined his deeds and found no sin, let him attribute it to neglect of Torah study... And if he attributed it to that and still did not find cause, they are surely chastisements of love, as it is said: "For whom the LORD loveth he correcteth" (Proverbs 3:12).
>
> --- Babylonian Talmud, Berakhot 5a (adapted from Steinsaltz translation)

This concept is relevant to our study because it provides an alternative theological framework for understanding suffering --- one that neither attributes suffering to personal sin nor promises healing as a right. In the *yissurim shel ahavah* framework, suffering is purposeful, beneficial, and an expression of God's love. It refines the soul, deepens faith, and prepares the sufferer for greater spiritual reward.

This rabbinic teaching resonates with several New Testament passages that present suffering in a positive light (Romans 5:3--5; James 1:2--4; 1 Peter 1:6--7). It also provides a counter-framework to any theology that treats all sickness as either sin's penalty or faith's failure.

### Sickness, Sin, and the Messianic Age

Rabbinic theology does connect sickness and sin --- to a degree. The Talmud contains discussions about whether suffering corresponds to specific sins, whether illness can be understood as divine discipline, and whether the righteous suffer more or less than the wicked. The relationship between sickness and sin in rabbinic thought is not simple or mechanical; it is nuanced, debated, and sometimes paradoxical.

What rabbinic theology does *not* develop is a specific link between the Messiah's *atoning suffering* and *physical healing* in the way that some Christians read Isaiah 53:5. The rabbinic tradition connects the messianic age with healing (as documented in the [pre-Christian section](/by-his-stripes/03-antiquity/rabbinic-pre-jesus/)), and it connects the Messiah with sickness (Sanhedrin 98b). But it does not develop the specific claim that the Messiah's wounds *produce* healing for others --- that is, it does not read Isaiah 53:5 as a mechanism by which the Messiah's suffering results in the physical healing of individuals.

This absence is worth noting, but it should not be overstated. Rabbinic theology was not asking the question that the healing-in-the-atonement debate asks. The rabbis were addressing different concerns: the nature of suffering, the identity of the Messiah, the relationship between Israel and the nations. The absence of a specific teaching on "healing in the atonement" does not mean the rabbis rejected the concept; it means they were not engaged with it.

## Summary

The post-Jesus rabbinic evidence completes the three-part trajectory that this chapter has traced:

1. **The Talmud preserves messianic readings of Isaiah 53** that include the Messiah's identification with sickness and suffering. Sanhedrin 98b's "leper scholar" passage is the most striking example, using Isaiah 53:4 to describe a Messiah who sits among the sick.

2. **Rashi's commentary marks the decisive shift** in mainstream Jewish interpretation: the Servant is Israel, not the Messiah. This reading became dominant and remains so today. The shift was driven by both internal textual considerations and external Christian apologetic pressure.

3. **The interpretive trajectory** from messianic to corporate readings is itself evidence that the messianic reading was earlier and more natural. But this trajectory proves something about the *identity* of the Servant, not about the *nature* of the healing. It supports the Christian application of Isaiah 53 to Christ but does not resolve whether "healed" means physically, spiritually, or both.

4. **Rabbinic theology connects sickness and sin** but does not develop a specific link between messianic atonement and physical healing. The concept of *yissurim shel ahavah* (chastisements of love) provides an alternative framework in which suffering is purposeful and beneficial rather than a sign of inadequate faith.

5. **Modern Jewish scholarship** reflects a range of views, with growing academic nuance and acknowledgment that the passage operates on multiple levels.

The evidence from antiquity --- both Christian and Jewish --- is now before the reader. The Apostolic Fathers and Ante-Nicene Fathers consistently read Isaiah 53 as a prophecy of Christ's substitutionary suffering for sin, without connecting it to physical healing. The Jewish interpretive trajectory reveals a messianic reading that predates Christianity, was challenged by the Targum's removal of suffering, and was eventually replaced by Rashi's corporate reading. Both streams of evidence are significant, and neither stream resolves the healing question by itself.

The reader now carries this evidence forward into [Chapter 4: Reality](/by-his-stripes/04-reality/), where the three major views on healing in the atonement are presented in full, each with its strongest arguments and its most serious challenges.
