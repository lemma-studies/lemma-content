The first two sections of this chapter examined how the earliest *Christians* read Isaiah 53. This section turns to a different stream of evidence: how *Jewish* interpreters understood the passage before Jesus of Nazareth was born. This evidence is critical because it tells us what Isaiah 53 meant to readers who had no Christian theological agenda --- readers who shared the linguistic and cultural heritage of the original author and who read the text in its original Hebrew.

The question for this section is not whether these interpreters were "right" or "wrong." The question is what their readings tell us about the range of meanings the text was understood to carry before Christianity entered the conversation. Did pre-Christian Jewish readers understand Isaiah 53 as messianic? Did they connect the passage to healing? Did they envision a suffering figure whose wounds would benefit others?

The answers are more complex --- and more interesting --- than either side of the modern debate usually acknowledges.

## The Dead Sea Scrolls (c. 150 BC -- 68 AD)

### The Discovery and Its Significance

The Dead Sea Scrolls, discovered beginning in 1947 in caves near Qumran on the northwestern shore of the Dead Sea, constitute one of the most important archaeological finds in history. The scrolls were produced by a Jewish sectarian community (usually identified as Essenes or a related group) and date from roughly the second century BC to 68 AD --- precisely the period leading up to and overlapping with Jesus's lifetime.

For our study, the scrolls are important for three reasons: they preserve the oldest known manuscripts of Isaiah, they reveal the messianic expectations of at least one strand of Second Temple Judaism, and they illuminate the broader theological world in which Jesus and the earliest Christians operated.

### 1QIsa-a: The Great Isaiah Scroll

The Great Isaiah Scroll (designated 1QIsa-a) is the most famous of the Dead Sea Scrolls. It is a complete copy of the book of Isaiah, dating to approximately 125 BC --- making it roughly a thousand years older than the oldest previously known Hebrew manuscript of Isaiah (the Masoretic Text manuscripts dating to the ninth and tenth centuries AD).

The significance for our study is twofold.

**First, the text of Isaiah 53 in the Great Isaiah Scroll is remarkably similar to the Masoretic Text.** The key verses --- including the critical phrases about bearing sickness, being wounded for transgressions, and healing through stripes --- are present and substantially identical. The passage was not significantly altered over the course of a millennium of copying. This means that the text we are studying in the Masoretic Text is essentially the same text that Second Temple Jews were reading.

**Second, there are minor textual variants that are worth noting.** The most discussed variant in Isaiah 53 appears in verse 11, where the Great Isaiah Scroll reads "he shall see light" (adding the word *or*, "light") --- a phrase absent from the Masoretic Text. The Septuagint also includes this reading ("he shall see light"), suggesting that the Qumran text may preserve an earlier reading. This variant does not directly affect the healing question, but it demonstrates that the text was transmitted with minor variations that can carry theological significance.

In Isaiah 53:4, the Great Isaiah Scroll preserves the same Hebrew words --- choliy ("sickness/grief") and makob ("pain/sorrow") --- that the Masoretic Text contains. The physical-sickness language was present in the text as read at Qumran. Whether the Qumran community understood those words as referring to physical sickness or to spiritual suffering is a separate question --- one the scrolls do not directly answer.

### 11Q13: The Melchizedek Scroll

The Melchizedek Scroll (11Q13 or 11QMelch) is a fragmentary text found in Cave 11 at Qumran. It describes an eschatological figure --- Melchizedek --- who will appear at the end of days to proclaim liberty to captives, execute judgment, and bring atonement. The text draws heavily on Isaiah 61:

> And as for what he said: "In this year of jubilee, you shall return, each one, to his respective property" (Lev 25:13), concerning which he said: "This is the manner of the release: every creditor shall release what he lent to his neighbour. He shall not coerce his neighbour or his brother, for the release of God has been proclaimed" (Deut 15:2). Its interpretation for the last days refers to the captives... And the herald is the anointed one of the spirit, of whom Daniel said: "Until an anointed prince, there shall be seven weeks" (Dan 9:25). And the messenger who brings good news, who announces salvation, is the one of whom it is written: "How beautiful upon the mountains are the feet of the messenger who announces peace, of the messenger of good news who announces salvation, saying to Zion: your God reigns" (Isa 52:7).
>
> --- 11Q13 (11QMelchizedek), Column II (adapted from Vermes/Martinez translation)

The significance of this text is that it connects the jubilee year (liberation, restoration, release from bondage) with an eschatological messianic figure, and it does so using language from Isaiah 52 --- the chapter that immediately precedes the Fourth Servant Song. The Qumran community associated the arrival of the messianic age with liberation and restoration. The language of Isaiah 52--53 was part of their eschatological vocabulary.

Moreover, the concept of eschatological *healing* was embedded in the Qumran community's messianic expectations. The fragment 4Q521 (sometimes called the "Messianic Apocalypse") describes the messianic age in terms that strikingly parallel Jesus's response to John the Baptist:

> ... for the heavens and the earth shall listen to His Messiah... For the Lord will consider the pious, and call the righteous by name. Over the meek His spirit will hover, and the faithful He will restore by His power. He shall glorify the pious on the throne of the eternal kingdom, releasing captives, giving sight to the blind, straightening the bent... He shall heal the wounded, and revive the dead, and bring good news to the poor...
>
> --- 4Q521, Fragment 2, Column II (adapted from Vermes translation)

This is a remarkable text. It describes the messianic age in terms of physical healing --- the blind seeing, the wounded healed, the dead revived --- using language drawn from Isaiah 35:5--6 and Isaiah 61:1. The Qumran community expected the Messiah's coming to be accompanied by healing. This expectation existed independently of Christianity.

### The Qumran Community's Messianic Expectations

The Dead Sea Scrolls reveal a community with complex messianic expectations. Several features are relevant to our study:

**Two Messiahs.** The Qumran community appears to have expected two messianic figures: a priestly Messiah (the "Messiah of Aaron") and a royal Messiah (the "Messiah of Israel"). The priestly Messiah was often given precedence. This dual-Messiah expectation is different from the standard Christian understanding of a single Messiah, but it demonstrates that messianic categories were diverse and active in Second Temple Judaism.

**A Suffering Righteous One.** Several Qumran texts describe a righteous figure who suffers unjustly and whose suffering has significance for the community. The Hodayot (Thanksgiving Hymns, 1QH) contain passages where the speaker describes intense suffering in language that echoes Isaiah 53:

> I give thanks to you, O Lord, for you have sustained me with your strength. You have spread your Holy Spirit over me so that I shall not stumble. You have strengthened me before the battles of wickedness... For I was despised, and they did not esteem me...
>
> --- 1QH Column XII (adapted from Vermes translation)

Whether these hymns describe a specific messianic figure or the community's leader (the "Teacher of Righteousness") is debated. But they demonstrate that the concept of a righteous sufferer whose suffering carried communal significance was present at Qumran.

**Eschatological Healing.** As 4Q521 shows, the Qumran community associated the messianic age with physical healing and restoration. This is important for our study because it demonstrates that the connection between the Messiah and healing was not a Christian invention. It was part of the broader Second Temple Jewish messianic expectation.

## Intertestamental Literature

### Wisdom of Solomon 2--5

The Wisdom of Solomon is a Jewish text written in Greek, probably in Alexandria, sometime in the first century BC or first century AD. Chapters 2 through 5 contain a remarkable portrait of a "righteous one" who suffers unjustly, is killed by the wicked, and is vindicated by God after death. The parallels to Isaiah 53 are striking, though the Wisdom of Solomon does not quote Isaiah directly:

> Let us lie in wait for the righteous one, because he is inconvenient to us and opposes our actions; he reproaches us for sins against the law... He professes to have knowledge of God, and calls himself a child of the Lord... Let us see if his words are true, and let us test what will happen at the end of his life; for if the righteous one is God's child, He will help him, and will deliver him from the hand of his adversaries. Let us test him with insult and torture, so that we may find out how gentle he is, and make trial of his forbearance. Let us condemn him to a shameful death, for, according to what he says, he will be protected.
>
> --- Wisdom of Solomon 2:12--20 (NRSV)

The vindication follows in chapter 5, where the wicked realize their error after the righteous one has been exalted:

> Then the righteous one will stand with great confidence in the presence of those who have oppressed him... Seeing him, they will be shaken with dreadful fear, and they will be amazed at the unexpected salvation. They will speak to one another in repentance, and in anguish of spirit they will groan, and say, "This is the one whom we once held in derision... We thought that his life was madness and that his end was without honor. Why has he been numbered among the children of God? And why is his lot among the saints?"
>
> --- Wisdom of Solomon 5:1--5 (NRSV)

The parallels to Isaiah 53 are unmistakable: the righteous one is despised, rejected, tested with suffering, killed --- and then vindicated, while those who rejected him are confounded. However, the suffering of the righteous one in the Wisdom of Solomon is not explicitly *vicarious* --- he does not suffer *for* others in the substitutionary sense of Isaiah 53. His vindication shames the wicked, but there is no language of bearing their sins or healing their wounds.

The significance is this: the Wisdom of Solomon demonstrates that the *pattern* of Isaiah 53 --- righteous suffering followed by divine vindication --- was a recognized theological category in pre-Christian Judaism. The idea that a righteous figure could suffer unjustly and be vindicated by God was "in the air" in the centuries before Jesus.

### 1 Enoch and the Son of Man

1 Enoch is a composite Jewish text, portions of which date to the third century BC or earlier. The section known as the Parables of Enoch (1 Enoch 37--71) describes a heavenly figure called the "Son of Man" or the "Elect One" who executes eschatological judgment, vindicates the righteous, and establishes God's kingdom. The dating of the Parables is debated --- some scholars place them before Jesus, others after --- but the broader Enochic tradition undeniably shaped Jewish messianic expectations.

The Son of Man in 1 Enoch is a figure of power and judgment, not of suffering. He does not bear the sins of others. He does not die. In this respect, the Enochic tradition differs sharply from Isaiah 53. But the Enochic Son of Man traditions are relevant because they contribute to the broader picture of messianic expectation in Second Temple Judaism: the Messiah was expected to bring restoration, justice, and the defeat of evil. The question was *how* --- through power, through suffering, or through both.

### 4 Maccabees and Vicarious Suffering

4 Maccabees is a Jewish philosophical treatise, probably written in the first century AD, that retells the story of the Maccabean martyrs. Its treatment of the martyrs' deaths is directly relevant to our study because it develops the concept of *vicarious atonement through suffering* --- the idea that the deaths of the righteous can have atoning value for others.

The mother of the seven martyred brothers says:

> These, then, who have been consecrated for the sake of God, are honored not only with this honor, but also because through them our enemies did not prevail against our nation, and the tyrant was punished, and the homeland purified, since they became, as it were, a ransom for the sin of our nation. And through the blood of those devout ones and their death as an atoning sacrifice, divine Providence preserved Israel that previously had been mistreated.
>
> --- 4 Maccabees 17:20--22 (NRSV)

This is remarkable language. The martyrs' deaths are described as a "ransom" (antipsychon) and an "atoning sacrifice" (hilasterion) for the nation. Their suffering is *vicarious* --- it benefits others. Their blood *purifies* the homeland. These are the same theological categories found in Isaiah 53: vicarious suffering, atonement, cleansing.

4 Maccabees demonstrates that the concept of vicarious, atoning suffering was not unique to Isaiah 53 or to Christianity. It was a recognized theological category in Second Temple Judaism. The martyrs' deaths were understood to have redemptive value --- though the specifics (how the atonement works, what it accomplishes) differ from the Christian understanding of Christ's death.

The relevance for the healing question is indirect but important: if pre-Christian Judaism already recognized that the suffering of the righteous could atone for others, then the theological framework for reading Isaiah 53 as vicarious atonement was well established before Jesus. The question of whether that atonement extends to physical healing remains open, but the broader theological context makes vicarious atonement a natural reading of the Servant Song.

## Was There a "Suffering Messiah" Concept Before Jesus?

This is one of the most debated questions in Second Temple studies, and it bears directly on the interpretation of Isaiah 53.

### The Messiah ben Joseph Tradition

In later rabbinic literature (particularly the Babylonian Talmud, Sukkah 52a), there is a tradition of two Messiahs: Messiah ben David (the triumphant, Davidic king) and Messiah ben Joseph (a messianic figure who *dies* in battle before the final redemption). The Messiah ben Joseph tradition is significant because it preserves a Jewish concept of a suffering and dying messianic figure --- one who dies for the benefit of the nation.

The critical question is: *when did this tradition originate?* If it predates Christianity, it demonstrates that the idea of a suffering Messiah was part of pre-Christian Jewish thought. If it postdates Christianity, it may be a Jewish development independent of Christian influence, or it may be an indirect response to Christian claims about a crucified Messiah.

Scholars are divided. Some (such as Joseph Klausner) argue that the Messiah ben Joseph tradition is ancient, rooted in Old Testament passages about Joseph's suffering and Zechariah 12:10 ("they shall look upon me whom they have pierced"). Others (such as Sigmund Mowinckel) argue that it is a post-Christian development with no clear pre-Christian attestation.

The honest assessment is that the evidence is ambiguous. The Messiah ben Joseph tradition is attested in documents that postdate Christianity, but some of the biblical roots it draws on (Zechariah 12:10; Genesis 37--50) were available to pre-Christian interpreters. Whether they drew the same conclusions from those texts before Christianity raised the question of a suffering Messiah is uncertain.

### What the Qumran Evidence Suggests

The Dead Sea Scrolls do not contain an explicit "suffering Messiah" doctrine. They do not identify Isaiah 53's Suffering Servant as the Messiah and then describe that Messiah as suffering for others' sins. What they *do* contain is a constellation of related ideas:

1. **A suffering righteous figure** whose suffering has communal significance (the Hodayot)
2. **Messianic expectations** that include liberation, restoration, and healing (4Q521, 11Q13)
3. **The text of Isaiah 53** itself, carefully preserved and presumably read and studied
4. **Vicarious atonement** as a recognized theological category (present also in 4 Maccabees, which shares the broader Second Temple milieu)

These elements do not add up to a fully developed "suffering Messiah" doctrine. But they demonstrate that the individual pieces --- suffering, messiahship, vicarious atonement, eschatological healing --- were all present in pre-Christian Judaism. Whether any pre-Christian Jewish community assembled these pieces into a coherent picture of a Messiah who suffers vicariously and whose suffering brings healing is unclear.

The Christian claim is that Jesus assembled these pieces --- or rather, that God assembled them in Him. The question is whether that assembly was novel or whether it drew on an existing interpretive tradition.

## Healing in Second Temple Messianic Expectations

### Isaiah 35:5--6 and the Messianic Age

One of the clearest connections between the Messiah and healing in pre-Christian Jewish thought comes not from Isaiah 53 but from Isaiah 35:

> "Then the eyes of the blind shall be opened, and the ears of the deaf shall be unstopped. Then shall the lame man leap as an hart, and the tongue of the dumb sing: for in the wilderness shall waters break out, and streams in the desert."
>
> --- Isaiah 35:5--6 (KJV)

This passage describes the blessings of the messianic age in unmistakably physical terms: the blind see, the deaf hear, the lame walk, the mute speak. These are not spiritual metaphors (or at least, not only spiritual metaphors). They describe bodily restoration.

This is precisely why Jesus, when answering John the Baptist's question "Art thou he that should come, or do we look for another?" (Matthew 11:3 KJV), replies by pointing to His healings:

> "Go and shew John again those things which ye do hear and see: The blind receive their sight, and the lame walk, the lepers are cleansed, and the deaf hear, the dead are raised up, and the poor have the gospel preached to them."
>
> --- Matthew 11:4--5 (KJV)

Jesus's answer only makes sense if His audience --- first-century Jews --- expected the messianic age to be accompanied by physical healing. And the evidence confirms that they did. The Qumran text 4Q521, quoted above, describes the Messiah's work in similar terms: "giving sight to the blind, straightening the bent... He shall heal the wounded, and revive the dead, and bring good news to the poor."

### The Connection and the Gap

The evidence establishes that pre-Christian Judaism connected the Messiah with healing. The messianic age was expected to bring physical restoration. This connection was drawn primarily from Isaiah 35 and Isaiah 61, not from Isaiah 53.

This is an important distinction. The modern healing-in-the-atonement debate centers on Isaiah 53:5 --- "with his stripes we are healed" --- and asks whether this verse promises physical healing through the Messiah's *suffering*. The pre-Christian Jewish evidence connects healing to the messianic *age* (Isaiah 35) and to the Messiah's *anointing* (Isaiah 61), but it does not clearly connect healing to the Messiah's *suffering* (Isaiah 53).

In other words: pre-Christian Judaism expected the Messiah to bring healing, but it did not (as far as our evidence shows) expect Him to bring healing *through His wounds*. The connection between messianic suffering and healing is precisely the claim that Isaiah 53:4--5 makes --- and precisely the claim that is debated.

### What This Means for the Debate

The pre-Christian evidence is valuable for both sides:

**For those who connect healing to the atonement:** The expectation that the Messiah would bring physical healing is well established in pre-Christian Judaism. Isaiah 53 describes the Messiah's suffering. Isaiah 35 and 61 describe the Messiah's healing work. Together, they suggest a Messiah whose redemptive work addresses the whole person --- body and soul. The pre-Christian expectation of messianic healing creates the context in which "with his stripes we are healed" could include physical healing.

**For those who read Isaiah 53:5 as spiritual healing:** The pre-Christian sources that connect the Messiah to healing draw on Isaiah 35 and 61, not Isaiah 53. The healing in Isaiah 53:5 sits in a context dominated by sin language (transgressions, iniquities), and pre-Christian Jewish readers do not appear to have read it as a physical-healing promise. The messianic healing expectations and the Servant Song's suffering-for-sin theme are related but distinct.

## Summary

The pre-Christian Jewish evidence paints a complex picture:

1. **The text of Isaiah 53 was preserved accurately.** The Dead Sea Scrolls confirm that the passage Second Temple Jews read was essentially the same text we study today, including the physical-sickness language of verse 4 and the healing language of verse 5.

2. **Messianic expectations included healing.** The Qumran community and broader Second Temple Judaism expected the messianic age to bring physical restoration --- the blind seeing, the deaf hearing, the lame walking. This expectation was grounded in Isaiah 35 and 61.

3. **Vicarious, atoning suffering was a recognized category.** 4 Maccabees and the Qumran Hodayot demonstrate that the concept of suffering that benefits others was part of the pre-Christian Jewish theological landscape.

4. **A fully developed "suffering Messiah who heals through his wounds" is not clearly attested before Christianity.** The individual elements are present --- messianic expectation, healing, vicarious suffering --- but whether they were combined into a single coherent doctrine before Jesus is uncertain.

5. **The gap between "Messiah brings healing" and "Messiah heals through his suffering" is the precise gap that the healing-in-the-atonement debate occupies.** Pre-Christian Judaism bridges part of that gap but not all of it. The reader must decide whether Isaiah 53:4--5 completes the bridge.
