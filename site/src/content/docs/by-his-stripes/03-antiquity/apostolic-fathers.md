The Apostolic Fathers are the earliest Christian writers outside the New Testament. They wrote within living memory of the apostles --- some of them knew apostles personally. Their testimony matters not because it is infallible but because it is *close*. If the apostles taught that physical healing was a provision of the atonement grounded in Isaiah 53:5, we would expect to find echoes of that teaching in the very next generation. If the apostles understood "by his stripes we are healed" as referring to spiritual restoration from sin, we would expect that reading to dominate the earliest post-apostolic literature.

What we find is instructive --- but also more limited than we might wish. These writers were not addressing our question. They had their own pastoral concerns: division, persecution, false teaching, martyrdom. Isaiah 53 entered their writings in service of those concerns, not in service of a systematic theology of healing. We must be honest about what their silence means and what it does not mean.

---

## Clement of Rome (c. 96 AD)

### The Man and His Context

Clement of Rome is traditionally identified as the third or fourth bishop of Rome after Peter. His letter to the Corinthian church --- known as 1 Clement --- is typically dated to around 96 AD, making it one of the earliest Christian writings outside the New Testament canon. The church at Corinth was again experiencing internal division (as it had in Paul's day), and Clement writes to urge unity, humility, and submission to proper church order.

### 1 Clement 16: The Most Extensive Early Quotation of Isaiah 53

Clement's letter contains the longest sustained quotation of Isaiah 53 in any post-apostolic document. In chapter 16, he quotes nearly the entire Servant Song --- and his purpose in doing so is unmistakable. He presents Christ's suffering not as a provision for physical healing but as a model of humility for a divided church.

Clement introduces the quotation with these words:

> For Christ is with those who are humble-minded, not with those who exalt themselves over His flock. The sceptre of the majesty of God, our Lord Christ Jesus, did not come in the pomp of pride or arrogance, although He might have done so, but in a lowly condition, as the Holy Spirit had declared regarding Him. For He says:
>
> "Lord, who hath believed our report, and to whom is the arm of the Lord revealed? We have declared in His presence: He is, as it were, a child, and like a root in thirsty ground; He has no form nor glory, yea, we saw Him, and He had no form nor comeliness; but His form was without honour, failing beyond the form of men; a man in stripes and toil, and acquainted with the bearing of sickness: for His face was turned away, and He was dishonoured, and not esteemed.
>
> He bears our sins and is pained for us; yet we supposed that He was in toil, and in stripes, and in affliction. But He was wounded for our transgressions, and bruised for our iniquities. The chastisement of our peace was upon Him, and by His stripes we were healed. All we like sheep have gone astray; each one went astray in his own way; and the Lord gave Him up for our sins, while He in the midst of His sufferings openeth not His mouth. He was brought as a sheep to the slaughter, and as a lamb before its shearer is dumb, so He openeth not His mouth. In His humiliation His judgment was taken away; who shall declare His generation? For His life is taken from the earth. For the transgressions of my people was He brought to death..."
>
> --- 1 Clement 16:2--10 (translation following Lightfoot/Holmes)

The quotation continues through the rest of the Servant Song. What matters for our study is Clement's *application*. He does not connect any of this language --- including "by His stripes we were healed" --- to physical healing. His point is christological and ethical: Christ, though possessing divine majesty, came in humility. The Corinthians, who are exalting themselves against one another, should follow His example.

### What Clement Does and Does Not Say

**What Clement does say:** Christ's suffering fulfills Isaiah 53. The Servant Song describes Christ's humility and vicarious suffering. Christians should imitate this humility.

**What Clement does not say:** He does not connect Isaiah 53 to physical healing. He does not develop a theology of healing in the atonement. He does not mention healing at all in his application of the passage. The phrase "by His stripes we were healed" is included in his quotation but receives no independent commentary --- it is part of the whole Servant Song, which Clement reads as a narrative of humility and substitutionary suffering.

**What this silence means:** Clement's silence on physical healing is not an argument against the doctrine. Clement was writing about church unity, not about healing. He used Isaiah 53 for the purpose at hand. An author's silence on a topic does not constitute denial of that topic. However, the fact that the earliest extensive quotation of Isaiah 53 in post-apostolic literature applies it to humility and sin-bearing --- with no hint of physical healing --- is a data point worth noting.

## Epistle of Barnabas (c. 70--130 AD)

### The Document and Its Date

The Epistle of Barnabas is an anonymous early Christian treatise, traditionally (though almost certainly incorrectly) attributed to the Barnabas who accompanied Paul. Its date is debated: most scholars place it somewhere between 70 and 130 AD, with the period after the destruction of the Temple in 70 AD being most likely. The author writes to demonstrate that the Old Testament, properly understood, points to Christ through typology and allegory.

### References to Isaiah 53

The Epistle of Barnabas does not quote Isaiah 53 at the same length as Clement, but it engages with the passage at several points. The author's interest is in the typological and sacrificial meaning of Christ's suffering. In his discussion of the suffering of Christ, the author writes:

> For to this end the Lord endured to deliver up His flesh to corruption, that we might be sanctified through the remission of sins, which is effected by His blood of sprinkling.
>
> --- Epistle of Barnabas 5:1 (translation following Lightfoot/Holmes)

And further:

> He Himself was to offer the vessel of the Spirit as a sacrifice for our sins, in order that the type established in Isaac when he was offered upon the altar might be fulfilled.
>
> --- Epistle of Barnabas 7:3

The author also draws on Isaiah's broader imagery of the Servant:

> Since then He was about to be manifested in the flesh and to suffer, His suffering was foreshown. For the prophet says concerning Israel: "Woe unto their soul, for they have devised an evil counsel against themselves, saying, Let us bind the righteous one, for he is unprofitable to us."
>
> --- Epistle of Barnabas 6:7 (drawing on Isaiah 3:9--10, LXX)

### The Author's Focus

The Epistle of Barnabas reads the Old Testament through a strongly typological lens. The author sees Christ's suffering prefigured in the sacrificial system, in the scapegoat ritual, in Isaac, and in the bronze serpent. His concern is to demonstrate that Christ's death was the reality to which all Old Testament types pointed. The categories are sacrificial and soteriological --- sin, atonement, sanctification, remission.

Physical healing does not appear in the author's application of Isaiah 53 or related passages. This is consistent with the broader pattern of the Apostolic Fathers: they read the Servant Song as a prophecy of Christ's sacrificial death for sin, not as a promise of physical healing.

## Ignatius of Antioch (c. 107 AD)

### The Man and His Context

Ignatius was the bishop of Antioch in Syria --- one of the most important early Christian centers. He was arrested during the persecution under Emperor Trajan and transported to Rome for execution. During the journey, he wrote seven letters to churches along the route, which survive as some of the most vivid early Christian documents. Ignatius was martyred in the Roman arena, probably around 107 AD.

### Isaiah 53 in Ignatius

Ignatius does not quote Isaiah 53 directly in his extant letters. This is not surprising: his letters are short, urgent, pastoral documents written under the pressure of impending death. They are not systematic theological treatises.

However, Ignatius's theology is deeply relevant to the healing question, because his letters reveal an early Christian posture toward physical suffering that is strikingly different from any "healing in the atonement" framework.

### Ignatius and Suffering

Ignatius did not merely accept his coming martyrdom --- he actively *desired* it. He saw physical suffering and death as the path to union with Christ. His letter to the Romans is perhaps the most startling:

> I am writing to all the churches and I enjoin all, that I am dying willingly for God's sake, if only you do not prevent it. I plead with you, do not do me an untimely kindness. Allow me to be eaten by the beasts, through whom I can attain to God. I am God's wheat, and I am being ground by the teeth of the beasts so that I may be found pure bread of Christ.
>
> --- Ignatius, To the Romans 4:1 (translation following Holmes)

And further:

> Let fire and cross and battles with beasts, mutilation, mangling, wrenching of bones, hacking of limbs, crushing of my whole body, cruel tortures of the devil --- let all these come upon me, if only I may attain to Jesus Christ.
>
> --- Ignatius, To the Romans 5:3

### What Ignatius's Theology Implies

Ignatius's letters reveal a first-generation post-apostolic bishop who:

1. Did not expect physical deliverance from suffering as a mark of genuine faith
2. Viewed bodily suffering as a means of spiritual union with Christ
3. Actively resisted attempts by other Christians to secure his release or physical safety
4. Understood the Christian life as fundamentally oriented toward conformity to Christ's suffering, not deliverance from it

This does not disprove the healing-in-the-atonement doctrine. Ignatius was facing martyrdom for the faith --- a distinct category from illness. One could argue that persecution and sickness are different matters entirely. But Ignatius's posture is significant: the earliest bishop of one of Christianity's most important churches did not operate with a theology in which physical well-being was a guaranteed provision of the cross. His theology ran in precisely the opposite direction: the cross meant *entering into* suffering, not *escaping* it.

## The Didache (c. 50--120 AD)

### The Document and Its Date

The Didache (pronounced "DID-ah-kay," from the Greek for "teaching") is one of the earliest Christian documents outside the New Testament. Its date is debated --- estimates range from as early as 50 AD to as late as 120 AD --- but many scholars place it in the late first century. It serves as a manual of church order, covering ethics, liturgy, and community practices.

### Isaiah 53 in the Didache

The Didache does not engage with Isaiah 53 at all. The document is primarily practical rather than exegetical: it instructs communities on how to conduct baptisms, celebrate the Eucharist, receive traveling prophets, and live the "two ways" (the way of life and the way of death).

### Healing in the Didache

While the Didache does not address Isaiah 53, it is worth noting what the document reveals about early Christian practice regarding healing and sickness. The Didache's eucharistic prayers include language that may be relevant:

> And concerning the broken bread: "We give Thee thanks, our Father, for the life and knowledge which Thou didst make known to us through Jesus Thy servant; to Thee be the glory forever. Even as this broken bread was scattered over the mountains and was gathered together and became one, so let Thy Church be gathered together from the ends of the earth into Thy kingdom; for Thine is the glory and the power through Jesus Christ forever."
>
> --- Didache 9:3--4 (translation following Holmes)

The document also instructs believers to confess their sins before gathering for worship:

> But every Lord's day do ye gather yourselves together, and break bread, and give thanksgiving after having confessed your transgressions, that your sacrifice may be pure.
>
> --- Didache 14:1

The Didache testifies to a community that practiced prayer, confession, and worship --- but it does not develop any theology connecting these practices to physical healing through the atonement. The document's silence is unsurprising given its practical focus, but it contributes to the broader picture: the earliest Christian communities, as reflected in their instructional literature, did not articulate a connection between Isaiah 53 and physical healing.

## Polycarp of Smyrna (c. 69--155 AD)

### The Man and His Context

Polycarp was bishop of Smyrna (modern Izmir, Turkey) and is traditionally regarded as a disciple of the Apostle John. If this tradition is accurate, Polycarp represents a direct link between the apostolic generation and the post-apostolic church. He was martyred at the age of eighty-six, probably around 155 AD, making him one of the last surviving connections to the apostolic era.

### Polycarp's Letter to the Philippians

Polycarp's surviving letter to the church at Philippi references Christ's suffering in terms that echo Isaiah 53:

> "Who his own self bare our sins in his own body on the tree, who did no sin, neither was guile found in his mouth," but endured all things for our sakes, that we might live in Him. Let us therefore become imitators of His patience, and if we suffer for His name's sake, let us glorify Him. For He gave us this example in Himself, and we have believed that this is so.
>
> --- Polycarp, To the Philippians 8:1--2 (translation following Holmes)

Notice that Polycarp is drawing on 1 Peter 2:22--24, which itself draws on Isaiah 53. His application follows Peter's: Christ bore our sins and demonstrated patient endurance. Believers are to imitate this pattern. The categories are moral and spiritual --- sin-bearing, patient suffering, ethical imitation --- not physical healing.

### The Martyrdom of Polycarp

The account of Polycarp's martyrdom (written by the church at Smyrna shortly after the event) is one of the earliest and most detailed martyrdom narratives. It describes Polycarp's arrest, trial, and execution by burning and stabbing. Several features are significant for our study:

1. **Polycarp did not seek physical deliverance.** When given the opportunity to recant and save his life, he refused: "Eighty and six years have I served Him, and He never did me any injury: how then can I blaspheme my King and my Saviour?"

2. **The account includes a supernatural element** --- the fire is said to have formed an arch around Polycarp's body without consuming him, and a fragrant scent arose from his body --- but this is presented as God's glory displayed in martyrdom, not as physical deliverance. Polycarp still dies. He is stabbed after the fire fails to kill him.

3. **The theological framework is conformity to Christ's suffering.** The narrator explicitly compares Polycarp's death to Christ's passion. The point is not that God delivers the faithful from physical harm but that God glorifies Himself through the faithful endurance of suffering.

### What Polycarp's Testimony Shows

Polycarp --- a man who may have known the Apostle John personally --- read Isaiah 53 (via 1 Peter 2:24) as being about sin-bearing, and he applied it to the pattern of patient endurance under suffering. His martyrdom account reveals a theology in which faithfulness leads *through* suffering, not *around* it. Physical deliverance is not the expectation; conformity to Christ's suffering is the goal.

## Summary Assessment

### What the Apostolic Fathers Consistently Show

Across the entire body of Apostolic Fathers literature, a clear pattern emerges:

1. **Isaiah 53 is read christologically.** The Servant is identified with Christ. This is unanimous.

2. **Isaiah 53 is read in terms of sin-bearing and sacrifice.** The Servant's suffering addresses human sin --- transgressions, iniquities, the straying of sheep. Clement quotes the passage for humility and substitution. The Epistle of Barnabas connects it to sacrificial typology. Polycarp echoes Peter's application to sin-bearing.

3. **No Apostolic Father develops a theology of physical healing from Isaiah 53.** The phrase "by his stripes we are healed" appears in Clement's extended quotation, but it receives no independent commentary connecting it to physical healing. No other Apostolic Father engages with the healing language of Isaiah 53:4--5 in a way that connects it to bodily healing as an atonement provision.

4. **The dominant posture toward physical suffering is acceptance, endurance, and even embrace.** Ignatius desires martyrdom. Polycarp refuses deliverance. The Didache instructs communities in confession and worship without reference to healing as an atonement benefit. The early church's posture was shaped by persecution and the expectation of suffering, not by an expectation of physical deliverance through the cross.

### What This Evidence Proves and What It Does Not

**It does not prove that "healing in the atonement" is false.** The Apostolic Fathers were not addressing the question we are asking. They were dealing with church division (Clement), typological interpretation (Barnabas), martyrdom (Ignatius, Polycarp), and church order (Didache). The absence of a teaching is not the same as the denial of a teaching. It is entirely possible that the earliest Christians held views about healing and the atonement that they never had occasion to articulate in the documents that survive.

**It does not prove that the early church denied God heals.** The Apostolic Fathers clearly believed in a God who acts supernaturally. They recorded miracles in martyrdom accounts. They practiced prayer. The question is not whether God heals but whether they grounded healing in a systematic theology of the atonement connected to Isaiah 53:5.

**What it does show is this:** The earliest post-apostolic Christians, writing within one or two generations of the apostles, consistently read Isaiah 53 as a prophecy of Christ's substitutionary suffering for sin. They applied it to humility, ethical imitation, and spiritual restoration. They did not apply it to physical healing. The reader must decide what weight to give this evidence.

A reader who holds that "healing is in the atonement" will need to explain why the earliest post-apostolic witnesses show no awareness of this doctrine. The explanation may be satisfying --- they were asking different questions, facing different pressures, writing in different genres. But the question must be faced honestly.

A reader who holds that Isaiah 53:5 refers to spiritual healing only will find significant support in these earliest voices. But that reader must also reckon with the fact that these writers were not exegeting Isaiah 53:4--5 with the question of physical healing in view. Their testimony is consistent with the spiritual-only reading, but it was not produced in answer to the physical-healing question.

The evidence from the Apostolic Fathers is a piece of the puzzle --- an important piece, but not the whole picture. The next section examines the Ante-Nicene Fathers (c. 150--325 AD), where the theological reflection becomes more developed and the evidence more nuanced.
