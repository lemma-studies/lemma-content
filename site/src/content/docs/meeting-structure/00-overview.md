*"When ye come together, every one of you hath a psalm, hath a doctrine, hath a tongue, hath a revelation, hath an interpretation. Let all things be done unto edifying."* --- 1 Corinthians 14:26 (KJV)

## The Question

Most Christians agree on two things: the Lord's Supper matters, and believers should build one another up when they gather. The difficulty comes when you try to do both in the same meeting.

The Lord's Supper calls for reverence, focus, and remembrance of Christ's death. Participatory worship calls for openness, teaching, spiritual gifts, and the "each one has" pattern of 1 Corinthians 14:26. Can both thrive in a single gathering? Should they be separated? And how do you decide without splitting the church over a question Scripture does not explicitly answer?

This study examines the question through the SCAR Quadrilateral: Scripture, Christocentrism, Antiquity, and Reality. The goal is not to declare one structure correct, but to present the biblical evidence, the historical record, and the practical experience of churches who have wrestled with this --- and to let the reader weigh the options.

## What Scripture Says

Scripture is remarkably clear on *principles* and remarkably silent on *structure*.

The Lord's Supper is commanded: "Do this in remembrance of me" (Luke 22:19). It proclaims the Lord's death until he comes (1 Cor 11:26). It requires self-examination, mutual care, and reverence (1 Cor 11:27-33). The early church broke bread on the first day of the week (Acts 20:7).

Participatory worship is also commanded: "When ye come together, every one of you hath a psalm, hath a doctrine" (1 Cor 14:26). Believers are to teach and admonish one another (Col 3:16), stir one another to love and good works (Heb 10:24-25), and maintain order --- "decently and in order" (1 Cor 14:40).

The question is whether 1 Corinthians 11 (Lord's Supper) and 1 Corinthians 14 (participatory worship) describe the same meeting or different meetings. Both use the phrase "when ye come together." Paul addresses both in the same letter to the same church. But he does not say explicitly whether they happen in one gathering or two.

This is a genuine ambiguity. Both integration and separation can be supported from Scripture. What cannot be ambiguous are the principles: love, unity, remembrance, mutual edification, and order.

## What Jesus Taught

Jesus instituted the Lord's Supper at the Last Supper with "Do this in remembrance of me" (Luke 22:19). But he gave something even higher than a command about structure --- he gave a command about love: "A new commandment I give unto you, That ye love one another; as I have loved you" (John 13:34). And he prayed for unity: "That they all may be one; as thou, Father, art in me, and I in thee" (John 17:21).

The Last Supper itself was led by Jesus as the primary teacher. It was not a fully open "each one has" gathering. Yet Jesus also said, "Where two or three are gathered together in my name, there am I in the midst of them" (Matt 18:20), and the apostolic church clearly practiced mutual edification.

What Jesus's example teaches us is a priority order: love and unity *first*, then remembrance, then mutual edification, then structural questions. If a meeting structure promotes love and unity, it is good. If it damages love and unity --- even if it is "biblically correct" in form --- something has gone wrong.

## What the Earliest Christians Practiced

The historical evidence shows that the early church used multiple structures --- and none of them caused a crisis.

Pliny the Younger (c. 112 AD) reports to Emperor Trajan that Christians in Bithynia met twice: before dawn for hymns and an oath, then later for a communal meal. This looks like separation.

Justin Martyr (c. 150 AD) describes an integrated Sunday meeting in Rome: Scripture reading, a discourse by the president, standing prayer, then the Eucharist with bread and wine distributed to all, including portions carried to the absent. This looks like integration.

Tertullian (c. 197 AD) distinguishes a morning Eucharist from an evening agape feast --- the Lord's Supper and the communal meal had separated. By the time of Hippolytus (c. 215 AD), the separation was formalized.

Throughout all these structures, participatory elements persisted: responsive singing (Pliny), the congregation saying "Amen" (Justin), prophets contributing after communion (Didache 10:7), and individual prayer and confession.

The pattern is flexibility guided by principles. The early church did not have one universal structure. What they had in common was reverence for the Lord's Supper, care for one another, and a deep concern for unity.

## The Major Views Today

### View 1: Two-Part Meeting

The Lord's Supper comes first (30-45 minutes), focused on remembrance of Christ's death --- hymns about the cross, prayers of thanksgiving, breaking bread and sharing the cup. Then a clear transition leads to open participatory worship (45-60 minutes) --- teaching, doctrine, spiritual gifts, mutual edification. This model honors both purposes with clear boundaries. It has biblical support (Acts 20:7 can be read as breaking bread followed by extended teaching) and practical success (SFMI and some Brethren assemblies use this pattern).

### View 2: Separate Meetings

A dedicated Lord's Supper meeting (weekly, Sunday morning) and a separate teaching/participatory meeting (weekly or bi-weekly, different time). This provides complete separation with no risk of blurring. It has historical precedent in Tertullian and Hippolytus. The challenge is that it requires two gatherings and may reduce attendance at one.

### View 3: Integrated with Guidelines

A single meeting that includes both Lord's Supper and participatory elements, governed by clear guidelines: time limits, designated phases, and maintained focus. This matches Justin Martyr's description and is the simplest structure. The challenge is that it requires strong discipline to prevent the participatory elements from overwhelming the reverent remembrance.

### View 4: Sequential Flow

Lord's Supper first, flowing naturally into participatory worship --- remembrance of Christ's death leads to gratitude, which leads to teaching and mutual edification. No hard boundary, just a natural progression. This is close to the current practice of many Brethren assemblies.

## What All Views Agree On

Every serious position agrees on the fundamentals:

- **Love is the foundation.** "The greatest of these is love" (1 Cor 13:13). Any structure that does not promote love is wrong, regardless of its biblical arguments.
- **Unity is essential.** "That they all may be one" (John 17:21). Structure must preserve unity, not cause division.
- **Remembrance is primary.** "Do this in remembrance of me" (Luke 22:19). The Lord's Supper should be reverent and focused on Christ's death.
- **Mutual edification is important.** "Each one has... Let all things be done for building up" (1 Cor 14:26). Participation should build up the body.
- **Order is required.** "Let all things be done decently and in order" (1 Cor 14:40). Not chaos, but structured participation.
- **Both integration and separation are valid.** Choose what serves love and unity best in your context.

## For the Reader

This study was written for believers who take both the Lord's Supper and mutual edification seriously --- and who want to honor both without splitting over questions that Scripture leaves open.

The evidence from all four lenses --- Scripture, Christocentrism, Antiquity, and Reality --- points to the same conclusion: **structure serves love and unity, not the other way around.** Multiple models work. No single model is "the biblical one." What matters is whether your gathering promotes love, preserves unity, maintains reverent remembrance, and enables mutual edification.

The ultimate test for any meeting structure is not structural correctness but Christlike fruit. As Paul wrote to the church struggling with exactly these questions:

> "Though I speak with the tongues of men and of angels, and have not charity, I am become as sounding brass, or a tinkling cymbal."
> --- 1 Corinthians 13:1 (KJV)

If you have perfect structure but lack love, you have nothing. If you have imperfect structure but have love and unity, you have everything.
