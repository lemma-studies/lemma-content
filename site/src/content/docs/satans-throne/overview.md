*"I know thy works, and where thou dwellest, even where Satan's seat is: and thou holdest fast my name, and hast not denied my faith, even in those days wherein Antipas was my faithful martyr, who was slain among you, where Satan dwelleth."* --- Revelation 2:13 (KJV)

## The Question

When Jesus dictated His letter to the church in Pergamum through the apostle John, He made a startling identification: this city was the location of "Satan's throne." Of all the cities in the Roman Empire --- including Rome itself --- Jesus singled out Pergamum. Why? What was it about this city that earned such a designation? And who was Antipas, the only individual named as a martyr in the entire Book of Revelation, who gave his life there?

This study investigates the historical, archaeological, and textual evidence to understand what Jesus meant. We examine the Greek text of Revelation 2:12-17, the known history of Pergamum from both Roman and early Christian sources, the archaeological record of the city's religious installations, and the traditions surrounding the martyrdom of Antipas.

## What Scripture Says

The letter to Pergamum (Revelation 2:12-17) contains language found nowhere else in the seven letters. Jesus identifies Himself as the one "which hath the sharp sword with two edges" --- a direct challenge to the Roman *ius gladii* (the right of the sword), the power of life and death held by the Roman proconsul whose seat was in Pergamum. He then uses *thronos* (throne) --- a word of established authority --- to describe Satan's presence there, and repeats the emphasis with "where Satan dwelleth."

The martyrdom of Antipas is set within this context. His death was not incidental --- it was the direct consequence of Satan's enthroned power in that specific place.

## What Jesus Taught

Jesus's identification of Pergamum as Satan's throne carries profound theological weight. He commends the church for holding fast despite living in the very seat of the enemy's power. The letter reveals Jesus's awareness of specific local conditions --- the political, religious, and cultural pressures unique to Pergamum --- and His expectation that faithfulness continues even in the most hostile environment.

## What the Earliest Christians Practiced

The early church fathers commented on the Pergamum letter, and several traditions preserve information about Antipas. Roman sources document Pergamum's role as the provincial capital of Asia and the center of the imperial cult. Archaeological excavations --- particularly the German excavations of the 1870s-1880s that uncovered the Great Altar of Zeus --- have provided physical evidence for what the first-century city looked like.

This study gives special attention to:
- **Roman provincial records** regarding Pergamum's administrative role
- **Pliny the Elder** and other Roman writers on Pergamum
- **Tacitus** and **Suetonius** on emperor worship in Asia Minor
- **Strabo's Geography** on Pergamum's temples and institutions
- **Pausanias** on the religious sites of Pergamum
- **Early church traditions** about Antipas and the Pergamum church
- **Archaeological evidence** from the Pergamon acropolis, the Asklepion, and the imperial cult temple

## The Major Views Today

### View 1: The Great Altar of Zeus
The massive altar complex on the Pergamon acropolis, one of the wonders of the ancient world, dominated the city skyline. Its throne-like shape and its dedication to Zeus --- the chief pagan deity --- make it a compelling candidate.

### View 2: The Imperial Cult Center
Pergamum was the first city in Asia to build a temple to a living emperor (Augustus, 29 BC). The provincial cult of Rome and Augustus made Pergamum the seat of emperor worship, where Christians would face the ultimate test: offer incense to Caesar or die.

### View 3: The Asklepion
The healing sanctuary of Asklepios, the serpent-god of healing, drew thousands of pilgrims. The serpent was Asklepios's primary symbol --- a direct association with "that old serpent, called the Devil, and Satan" (Revelation 12:9).

### View 4: Convergence
Satan's throne was not one building but the entire spiritual atmosphere of Pergamum --- the convergence of Zeus worship, emperor worship, serpent veneration, and Athena's cult, all concentrated in one city.
