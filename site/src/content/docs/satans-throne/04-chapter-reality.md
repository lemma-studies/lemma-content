## The Views in Practice

### View 1: The Great Altar of Zeus

**Held by:** Many popular commentators, some academic scholars (e.g., William Ramsay, Colin Hemer). Widely held in pastoral and devotional literature.

**Core claim:** The massive altar of Zeus on the Pergamon acropolis, with its throne-like shape and its dedication to the chief pagan deity, is the specific physical structure that Jesus identifies as "Satan's throne."

**The case at its strongest:**

[3-5 paragraphs presenting this view as its best advocates would present it]

- The visual argument: the altar's U-shaped platform with monumental staircase looks like a giant throne when viewed from below. A first-century observer approaching Pergamum would see it dominating the acropolis.
- Zeus was the chief deity of the Greek pantheon, the "father of gods and men." If any pagan deity's altar could be called Satan's throne, it would be the altar of Zeus.
- The Gigantomachy frieze depicted the triumph of the gods over chaotic forces --- a pagan "theology of power" carved in stone, asserting divine authority over the world.
- Sir William Ramsay, one of the greatest scholars of Asia Minor, favored this identification.
- The altar is now in the Pergamon Museum, Berlin --- having been literally removed from Pergamum.

**Pastoral fruit:** This view provides a vivid, tangible image of what Christians in Pergamum faced. It makes the text concrete and visual.

**Honest challenges:**
- The altar was dedicated c. 180-160 BC, over two centuries before Revelation was written. Was it still a significant cult site in the late first century?
- *Thronos* means "throne" --- a seat of ruling authority. An altar is not typically a throne. The Greek word for altar is *bomos* or *thusiasterion*, not *thronos*.
- The altar may have been less prominent by the first century compared to the imperial cult temple.
- Jesus says "where Satan *dwelleth*" --- implying ongoing residence, not just a historical monument.

### View 2: The Imperial Cult Center

**Held by:** Many academic scholars (e.g., Steven Friesen, S.R.F. Price, Craig Koester, David Aune). Strong in recent scholarship.

**Core claim:** "Satan's throne" refers to the provincial imperial cult headquartered at Pergamum, which was the first city in Asia Minor to build a temple to a living Roman emperor (Augustus, 29 BC). The throne is the seat of emperor worship.

**The case at its strongest:**

[3-5 paragraphs presenting this view as its best advocates would present it]

- Pergamum was the first city in the province of Asia to receive the *neokoria* --- the right to house and maintain a provincial temple to the Roman emperor. This was a unique distinction at the time of Augustus.
- Tacitus (*Annals* 4.37) records that when Asian cities competed for a second imperial temple under Tiberius in 26 AD, Pergamum was excluded because it already had one. This confirms Pergamum's status as the *original* seat of emperor worship in Asia.
- The imperial cult was the specific mechanism by which Christians were persecuted. Refusal to offer incense and wine before the emperor's image was treated as treason (*maiestas*), punishable by death.
- The word *thronos* fits perfectly: the imperial cult was a seat of ruling political-religious authority.
- Jesus's self-identification as the one with "the sharp sword" directly echoes the Roman proconsul's *ius gladii*, and the proconsul's judicial seat was in Pergamum.
- This view best explains why Antipas was killed: he refused emperor worship and was executed by the authority of the Roman state.

**Pastoral fruit:** This view connects the passage directly to the real political pressure on Christians and provides a model for faithfulness under governmental persecution.

**Honest challenges:**
- Emperor worship was practiced throughout the province, not just in Pergamum. Other cities (Smyrna, Ephesus) also had imperial cult temples. What made Pergamum unique?
- The provincial cult was a shared arrangement --- the *koinon* of Asia administered it. Was Pergamum truly "the seat" or just one location among several?
- Some scholars argue the imperial cult did not become compulsory until later (Trajan's rescript to Pliny, c. 112 AD), which may postdate the writing of Revelation.

### View 3: The Asklepion

**Held by:** A minority of scholars, but with a distinctive argument (e.g., some in the tradition of Albrecht Dieterich, elements in W.M. Ramsay).

**Core claim:** The Asklepion (healing sanctuary of Asklepios) is the most likely candidate because its serpent symbol directly identifies it with Satan ("that old serpent").

**The case at its strongest:**

[3-5 paragraphs presenting this view as its best advocates would present it]

- Asklepios was identified with and represented by the serpent. Living serpents were kept in the sanctuary and used in healing rituals. Revelation explicitly identifies Satan as "that old serpent, called the Devil, and Satan" (Revelation 12:9, 20:2).
- The Pergamum Asklepion was one of the most famous healing centers in the ancient world, drawing pilgrims from across the Roman Empire. It was not a minor cult but a major institution.
- The title "Asklepios Soter" (Asklepios the Savior) directly competed with Christian claims about Jesus as Savior.
- Coins from Pergamum prominently feature the serpent-staff of Asklepios, showing the cult's centrality to the city's identity.
- Aelius Aristides' *Sacred Tales* describe intensive serpent encounters and dream incubation at the Pergamum Asklepion.

**Pastoral fruit:** This view highlights the spiritual warfare dimension --- the serpent of Asklepios as a direct manifestation of "that old serpent."

**Honest challenges:**
- The Asklepion was located *outside* the city walls, below the acropolis. Would a cult center outside the city proper be called the place "where Satan dwelleth" (i.e., where Christians also lived)?
- *Thronos* (throne) implies ruling authority. The Asklepion was a healing center, not a seat of government.
- The serpent connection, while striking, is more implicit than explicit in the Revelation text. John does not mention serpents in the Pergamum letter.
- Other cities (Epidauros, Kos, Athens) also had major Asklepia. Why single out Pergamum?

### View 4: Convergence (Multiple Factors)

**Held by:** Many recent commentators (e.g., G.K. Beale, Robert Mounce, Grant Osborne, Ben Witherington III). Increasingly the majority position.

**Core claim:** "Satan's throne" does not refer to one specific structure but to the unique convergence of pagan cult, imperial worship, and serpent veneration that made Pergamum the most spiritually hostile environment for Christians in the province of Asia.

**The case at its strongest:**

[3-5 paragraphs presenting this view as its best advocates would present it]

- No other city in Asia Minor had all of these simultaneously: the Great Altar of Zeus, the first provincial imperial cult temple, one of the world's great Asklepia with its serpent worship, temples to Athena, Dionysos, and Demeter, and the massive Serapis/Isis temple (the "Red Basilica").
- The word *katoikei* ("dwelleth") suggests permanent residence --- Satan *lives* in Pergamum, not merely visits. This fits a city-wide saturation of pagan cult better than a single building.
- The double reference ("where Satan's seat is" / "where Satan dwelleth") may intentionally refuse to narrow it to one location, because the whole city is under Satan's dominion.
- This view accounts for all the textual evidence: the throne (political authority via imperial cult), the idolatry (pagan temples), and the martyrdom (judicial execution).
- Pergamum was not the largest city in Asia (that was Ephesus) or the richest (possibly Smyrna or Sardis), but it was arguably the most *religiously diverse and intense.*

**Pastoral fruit:** This view recognizes that spiritual opposition rarely comes from a single source. It models how to identify and navigate complex environments of spiritual pressure.

**Honest challenges:**
- If "Satan's throne" is everything, it is arguably nothing specific. Does this view dilute the text's precision?
- Jesus uses *thronos*, a specific word for a specific seat of authority. Can a general atmosphere have a "throne"?
- If the convergence view is correct, why did Jesus not similarly identify other religiously diverse cities (e.g., Corinth, Athens, Rome itself)?

## Pastoral Considerations

Regardless of which specific identification one holds, the text makes clear that Christians in Pergamum faced:

1. **Political pressure** --- the imperial cult required participation as proof of loyalty
2. **Social pressure** --- civic life (guilds, festivals, markets) was intertwined with pagan worship and temple feasts
3. **Spiritual pressure** --- the city was saturated with competing claims to divine authority
4. **Mortal danger** --- Antipas died for his refusal to compromise

The church did not flee. They "held fast" (v.13). This is the model for every church that finds itself in hostile territory.
