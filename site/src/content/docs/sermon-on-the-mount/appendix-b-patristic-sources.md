This appendix collects all patristic quotations cited in Chapter 3 (Antiquity) into a single chronological reference. Each entry provides the full quotation, translator attribution, source information, historical context, and significance for reading the Sermon on the Mount.

The pre-Nicene cutoff (325 AD) marks the Council of Nicaea and the beginning of the church-state alliance under Constantine. Entries from after that date are included under "Post-Nicene Witnesses" for reference, as they contain the most extensive surviving commentaries on the Sermon.

All quotations are given in full. Where a father's argument is summarized rather than quoted directly, this is noted explicitly.

### How to Use This Appendix

Each entry follows a consistent format:

- **Quotation** --- the complete text in block-quote format, with translator and source line.
- **Context** --- who the author was, when and where they wrote, and the purpose of the work.
- **Sermon passages echoed/addressed/cited** --- the specific Matthew 5--7 texts that the quotation reflects.
- **Significance** --- what the quotation tells us about how the early church read the Sermon on the Mount.

Entries are arranged chronologically within three periods: the Apostolic Era (c. 70--150 AD), the Ante-Nicene Era (c. 150--325 AD), and the Post-Nicene period (after 325 AD). A summary table at the end provides a quick-reference overview of all sources.

### Sources and Translations

## Apostolic Era (c. 70--150 AD)

### Didache, 1:2--4 (c. 70--100 AD, Syria)

> "The way of life is this. First of all, thou shalt love the God that made thee; secondly, thy neighbour as thyself. And all things whatsoever thou wouldest not have befal thyself neither do thou unto another. Now of these words the doctrine is this. Bless them that curse you, and pray for your enemies and fast for them that persecute you; for what thank is it, if ye love them that love you? Do not even the Gentiles the same? But do ye love them that hate you, and ye shall not have an enemy. Abstain thou from fleshly and bodily lusts. If any man give thee a blow on thy right cheek, turn to him the other also, and thou shalt be perfect; if a man impress thee to go with him one mile, go with him twain; if a man take away thy cloak, give him thy coat also; if a man take away from thee that which is thine own, ask it not back, for neither art thou able."
> --- *Didache* 1:2--4 (Lightfoot translation)

**Context:** The *Didache* ("Teaching of the Twelve Apostles") is one of the earliest surviving Christian documents outside the New Testament. It functions as a church manual covering moral instruction, baptism, fasting, prayer, and the Eucharist. Its opening "Two Ways" section (chapters 1--6) draws so extensively from the Sermon on the Mount that many scholars consider it the earliest surviving catechism built on Jesus's teaching in Matthew 5--7.

<!-- verify-track: skip --> **Sermon passages echoed:** "Bless them that curse you, and pray for your enemies" (Matt 5:44). "If any man give thee a blow on thy right cheek, turn to him the other also" (Matt 5:39). "If a man impress thee to go with him one mile, go with him twain" (Matt 5:41). "And thou shalt be perfect" echoes "Be ye therefore perfect" (Matt 5:48). The negative Golden Rule ("all things whatsoever thou wouldest not have befal thyself neither do thou unto another") inverts Matthew 7:12.

**Significance:** The Didache uses Sermon material as baptismal instruction --- teaching given to new converts before baptism. Non-retaliation, love of enemies, and generosity under compulsion are presented as the standard expected of every baptized Christian. The commands are imperative, not advisory; present obligation, not future ideal. The Greek phrase *kai esē teleios* ("and thou shalt be perfect") ties the catechetical instruction directly to Matthew 5:48.

### Clement of Rome, 1 Clement 13:1--2 (c. 96 AD, Rome)

> "Let us therefore, brethren, be of humble mind, laying aside all haughtiness, and pride, and foolishness, and angry feelings; and let us act according to that which is written (for the Holy Spirit saith, 'Let not the wise man glory in his wisdom, neither let the mighty man glory in his might, neither let the rich man glory in his riches; but let him that glorieth glory in the Lord, in diligently seeking Him, and doing judgment and righteousness'), being especially mindful of the words of the Lord Jesus which He spake, teaching us meekness and long-suffering. For thus He spoke: 'Be ye merciful, that ye may obtain mercy; forgive, that it may be forgiven to you; as ye do, so shall it be done unto you; as ye judge, so shall ye be judged; as ye are kind, so shall kindness be shown to you; with what measure ye mete, with the same it shall be measured to you.'"
> --- 1 Clement 13:1--2 (Roberts-Donaldson translation, ANF Vol. 1)

**Context:** Written by Clement of Rome to the Corinthian church around 96 AD, this is one of the earliest post-apostolic documents we possess. Clement addresses division in the Corinthian congregation and appeals to the teaching of Jesus as the standard for resolving it.

<!-- verify-track: skip --> **Sermon passages echoed:** "Be ye merciful, that ye may obtain mercy" parallels Matthew 5:7. "Forgive, that it may be forgiven to you" echoes Matthew 6:14. "As ye judge, so shall ye be judged; with what measure ye mete, with the same it shall be measured to you" closely paraphrases Matthew 7:1--2.

**Significance:** Clement introduces the Sermon material as "the words of the Lord Jesus which He spake, teaching *us* meekness and long-suffering." The pronoun *us* is crucial. He does not present the Sermon as teaching addressed to a past audience, a future dispensation, or a spiritual elite. He applies it to a pastoral crisis in his own day as a present, binding standard of behavior.

### Polycarp, To the Philippians 2:3 (c. 110--130 AD, Smyrna)

> "Being mindful of what the Lord said in His teaching: 'Judge not, that ye be not judged; forgive, and it shall be forgiven unto you; be merciful, that ye may obtain mercy; with what measure ye mete, it shall be measured to you again'; and once more, 'Blessed are the poor, and those that are persecuted for righteousness' sake, for theirs is the kingdom of God.'"
> --- Polycarp, *To the Philippians* 2:3 (Roberts-Donaldson translation, ANF Vol. 1)

**Context:** Polycarp was bishop of Smyrna and, according to Irenaeus (*Against Heresies* 3.3.4), a disciple of the Apostle John. His letter to the Philippians is one of the few surviving documents from a writer with direct apostolic connection. He quotes the Beatitudes and the Sermon's teaching on judgment and mercy as "what the Lord said in His teaching."

<!-- verify-track: skip --> **Sermon passages echoed:** "Judge not, that ye be not judged" (Matt 7:1). "Be merciful, that ye may obtain mercy" (Matt 5:7). "With what measure ye mete, it shall be measured to you again" (Matt 7:2). "Blessed are the poor" (Matt 5:3). "Blessed are ... those that are persecuted for righteousness' sake, for theirs is the kingdom of God" (Matt 5:10).

**Significance:** Within one generation of the apostles, Polycarp treats the Sermon as normative present instruction for Christians. He quotes both the Beatitudes and the teaching on judgment as binding. Like Clement, he applies the Sermon to believers in his own time without qualification.

### Justin Martyr, First Apology 14--16 (c. 155 AD, Rome)

**On chastity, oaths, and love of enemies (*First Apology* 15--16):**

> "Concerning chastity, He uttered such sentiments as these: 'Whosoever looketh upon a woman to lust after her, hath committed adultery with her already in his heart before God.' And, 'If thy right eye offend thee, cut it out; for it is better for thee to enter into the kingdom of heaven with one eye, than, having two eyes, to be cast into everlasting fire.' ... And, 'Whosoever shall marry her that is divorced from another husband, committeth adultery.' ... And, 'Swear not at all; but let your yea be yea, and your nay, nay; for whatsoever is more than these cometh of evil.' And, 'If ye love them that love you, what new thing do ye? for even fornicators do this. But I say unto you, Pray for your enemies, and love them that hate you, and bless them that curse you, and pray for them that despitefully use you.'"
> --- Justin Martyr, *First Apology* 15--16 (Marcus Dods and George Reith translation, ANF Vol. 1)

**On the transformation of believers (*First Apology* 14):**

> "...we who formerly delighted in fornication, but now embrace chastity alone; we who formerly used magical arts, dedicate ourselves to the good and unbegotten God; we who valued above all things the acquisition of wealth and possessions, now bring what we have into a common stock, and communicate to every one in need; we who hated and destroyed one another, and on account of their different manners would not live with men of a different tribe, now, since the coming of Christ, live familiarly with them, and pray for our enemies, and endeavour to persuade those who hate us unjustly to live conformably to the good precepts of Christ, to the end that they may become partakers with us of the same joyful hope of a reward from God the ruler of all."
> --- Justin Martyr, *First Apology* 14 (Marcus Dods and George Reith translation, ANF Vol. 1)

**Context:** Justin's *First Apology* is addressed to the Roman Emperor Antoninus Pius and his court. It is an apologetic work --- a defense of Christian faith and practice before the highest civil authority. In chapters 15--17, Justin quotes extensively from the Sermon on the Mount, presenting it as what Christians actually do. Justin is not preaching to Christians but explaining Christianity to outsiders, and he stakes his case for Christianity's legitimacy on the Sermon.

<!-- verify-track: skip --> **Sermon passages cited:** "Whoever looketh upon a woman to lust after her" (Matt 5:28). "If thy right eye offend thee, cut it out" (Matt 5:29). "Whoever shall marry her that is divorced" (Matt 5:32). "Swear not at all" (Matt 5:34). "Love your enemies, and pray for them that curse you" (Matt 5:44). The second quotation reflects the transformed life described throughout the Sermon, especially the teaching on wealth (Matt 6:19--21) and love of enemies (Matt 5:44).

**Significance:** Justin's testimony is extraordinary for two reasons. First, he presents the Sermon to an emperor as actual Christian behavior --- "we who formerly hated ... now pray for our enemies." This is not aspirational language; it is apologetic. Second, he does not qualify the Sermon's commands as intended for a different audience or a future age. Writing only about a century after Jesus spoke the Sermon, Justin treats every command as binding on all believers in his own time.

## Ante-Nicene Era (c. 150--325 AD)

### Irenaeus of Lyon, Against Heresies 4.13.1 (c. 180 AD, Lyon)

**Author's summary of the argument** (ANF Vol. 1, pp. 477--478; Irenaeus's reasoning is paraphrased here rather than directly quoted, as the passage is argumentative rather than epigrammatic):

Christ's commands in the Sermon do not relax the law's demands but intensify them. Where the law prohibited murder, Christ prohibited anger. Where the law prohibited adultery, Christ prohibited lust. Where the law required oaths be kept, Christ prohibited oaths entirely. This "extension" proves that the Sermon belongs to the new covenant and is binding on all who receive it. The law was a preparation; the Sermon is the fulfillment. To claim these commands are not for present believers would, in Irenaeus's framework, be to claim that Christ's covenant is less demanding than Moses's --- an absurdity.

**Source:** Alexander Roberts and William Rambaut translation, ANF Vol. 1, pp. 477--478.

**Context:** Irenaeus was bishop of Lyon and a disciple of Polycarp (who was a disciple of John), giving him a theological genealogy as direct as any post-apostolic writer's. In *Against Heresies* 4.13, he addresses the relationship between the Mosaic law and Christ's teaching.

**Sermon passages addressed:** The antitheses of Matthew 5:21--48 are central to Irenaeus's argument: anger vs. murder (Matt 5:21--22), lust vs. adultery (Matt 5:27--28), oaths abolished (Matt 5:33--37). He reads these not as examples but as the structure of the new-covenant ethic.

**Significance:** Irenaeus reads the Sermon as the definitive expression of Christ's new-covenant ethic --- not an impossible standard, not a future kingdom program, but the law of life for Christians now. His argument that the Sermon "extends" the law rules out any reading that would set it aside as impractical or inapplicable.

### Tertullian of Carthage, De Corona 11, De Idololatria 19, De Patientia 8 (c. 200--220 AD, Carthage)

**On military service (*De Corona* 11):**

> "Shall it be held lawful to make an occupation of the sword, when the Lord proclaims that he who uses the sword shall perish by the sword? And shall the son of peace take part in the battle when it does not become him even to sue at law? And shall he apply the chain, and the prison, and the torture, and the punishment, who is not the avenger even of his own wrongs?"
> --- Tertullian, *De Corona* 11 (S. Thelwall translation, ANF Vol. 3)

**On faith and the military (*De Idololatria* 19):**

> "But now inquiry is made about this point, whether a believer may turn himself unto military service, and whether the military may be admitted unto the faith. ... the Lord afterward, in disarming Peter, unbelted every soldier."
> --- Tertullian, *De Idololatria* 19 (S. Thelwall translation, ANF Vol. 3)

**On non-retaliation (*De Patientia* 8):**

> "If one attempt to provoke you by manual violence, the monition of the Lord is at hand: 'To him,' He saith, 'who smiteth thee on the face, turn the other cheek likewise.' Let outrageousness be wearied out by your patience. Whatever that blow may be, conjoined with pain and contumely, it shall receive a heavier one from the Lord."
> --- Tertullian, *De Patientia* 8 (S. Thelwall translation, ANF Vol. 3)

**Context:** Tertullian was a North African trained lawyer turned theologian. He is the most vigorous ante-Nicene voice for the literal obedience reading of the Sermon, applying its commands to specific practical questions --- military service, oaths, non-retaliation --- with legal precision.

**Sermon passages applied:** "Resist not evil" and "turn the other cheek" (Matt 5:39) ground the teaching on non-retaliation in *De Patientia*. "Swear not at all" (Matt 5:34) underlies Tertullian's refusal of oaths. "Love your enemies" (Matt 5:44) and the implied prohibition of violence ground his rejection of military service. His phrase "the son of peace" draws on Christ's description of the peacemakers (Matt 5:9).

**Significance:** Tertullian reads the Sermon as literal, binding law for every baptized Christian. No military service, because the Sermon forbids violence and retaliation. No oaths, because Christ said "swear not at all." No reprisal, even when physically assaulted. He does not treat these as counsel for the unusually devout but as basic obligations. His legal training gives these readings deliberate precision --- he understood the social cost and accepted it.

### Clement of Alexandria, Paedagogus 3.12, Stromata 4.6 (c. 200 AD, Alexandria)

**Author's summary** (the ANF Vol. 2 translation of Clement is often paraphrase rather than strict rendering; readers should consult ANF Vol. 2 or PG 8 for Clement's exact wording):

On wealth and simplicity (*Paedagogus* 3.12): Christ's instructions about not storing up treasure on earth, about the lilies of the field, and about simplicity in dress and food are binding moral guidance for Christians. Wealth is not evil in itself, but the Christian must hold it loosely and use it generously --- this is what the Sermon requires.

On the Beatitudes (*Stromata* 4.6): "They also are peacemakers, who teach those who war against the stratagems of sin to have recourse to faith and peace" (Wilson translation, ANF Vol. 2). Clement reads the Beatitudes as a progression of Christian virtue.

**Source:** Wilson translation, ANF Vol. 2.

**Context:** Clement was head of the Catechetical School of Alexandria. His approach is more philosophical and pedagogical than Tertullian's --- less legal, more suited to his educated Alexandrian audience. He does not reject literal application but tends to interpret the Sermon's commands at a moral and spiritual level.

**Sermon passages addressed:** "Lay not up for yourselves treasures upon earth" (Matt 6:19). "Consider the lilies of the field" (Matt 6:28). "Blessed are the peacemakers" (Matt 5:9). Clement also applies the Sermon's teaching on simplicity in dress and food to the daily lives of Alexandrian Christians.

**Significance:** Despite his gentler tone, Clement reaches the same fundamental conclusion as the other ante-Nicene writers: the Sermon is addressed to Christians now, and its commands are to be followed. He does not suggest the Sermon belongs to a future dispensation or a different audience.

### Origen of Alexandria, Contra Celsum 8.73 (c. 245 AD, Caesarea)

> "To those enemies of our faith who require us to bear arms for the commonwealth, and to slay men, we can reply: 'Do not those who are priests at certain shrines ... keep their hands free from blood, that they may with hands unstained and free from human blood offer the appointed sacrifices to your gods?' ... And we do take our part in public affairs, when along with righteous prayers we join self-denying exercises and meditations, which teach us to despise pleasures, and not to be led away by them."
> --- Origen, *Contra Celsum* 8.73 (Crombie translation, ANF Vol. 4)

**Context:** Origen was the most prolific and philosophically sophisticated of the ante-Nicene fathers. In *Contra Celsum*, he defends Christian refusal of military service against the pagan philosopher Celsus. Though this passage comes from Book 8 rather than his Sermon commentary directly, it reveals Origen's hermeneutical posture: the Sermon's commands on non-violence are not a future ideal but the basis for present Christian practice.

**Sermon passages applied:** "Love your enemies" (Matt 5:44) and "resist not evil" (Matt 5:39) underlie Origen's defense of Christian pacifism. His analogy comparing Christians to pagan priests who keep their hands free from blood draws on the Sermon's call to a higher righteousness (Matt 5:20). In his commentary fragments on Matthew 5:39, Origen reads "turn the other cheek" as both literal non-violence and spiritual warfare against demonic powers --- holding both senses without canceling either.

**Significance:** Origen consistently holds together the literal and spiritual senses of the Sermon. He allows for a deeper, allegorical layer of meaning --- the struggle against spiritual enemies --- but he does not use this reading to cancel the literal one. In practice, his conclusions align with Tertullian's: Christians do not serve in the military, do not retaliate, and do not take oaths. The Sermon is for Christians now, in both its literal and spiritual senses.

## Post-Nicene Witnesses (for reference)

The SCAR framework places primary weight on pre-Nicene evidence. The following post-Nicene fathers are included because they produced the most extensive surviving commentaries on the Sermon on the Mount.

---

### John Chrysostom, Homilies on Matthew 18.1--18.4 (c. 390 AD, Antioch/Constantinople)

**On Matthew 5:39 ("turn the other cheek"), *Homilies on Matthew* 18.1:**

> "'What then?' it is said, 'ought we not to resist the evil one?' Indeed we ought, but not in this way, but as He hath commanded, by giving one's self up to suffer wrongfully; for thus shalt thou prevail over him. For one fire is not quenched by another, but fire by water."
> --- Chrysostom, *Homilies on Matthew* 18.1 (George Prevost translation, revised by M.B. Riddle; Philip Schaff, series editor. NPNF Series 1, Vol. 10)

**On Matthew 5:44 ("Love your enemies"), *Homilies on Matthew* 18.4:**

> "Seest thou how many steps He hath ascended, and how He hath set us on the very summit of virtue? Nay, mark it, numbering from the beginning. A first step is, not to begin with injustice: a second, after he hath begun, to vindicate one's self by equal retaliation; a third, not to do unto him that is vexing us the same that one hath suffered, but to be quiet; a fourth, even to give one's self up to suffer wrongfully; a fifth, to give up yet more than the other, who did the wrong, wishes; a sixth, not to hate him who hath done so; a seventh, even to love him; an eighth, to do him good also; a ninth, to entreat God Himself on his behalf."
> --- Chrysostom, *Homilies on Matthew* 18.4 (George Prevost translation, revised by M.B. Riddle; Philip Schaff, series editor. NPNF Series 1, Vol. 10)

**On whether the commands are possible, *Homilies on Matthew* 18.2:**

> "Let us not therefore suppose His injunctions impossible. Nay, for besides their expediency, they are very easy, if we are sober-minded; and the profit of them is so great as to be an exceeding help, not to ourselves only, but to those also who are using us despitefully."
> --- Chrysostom, *Homilies on Matthew* 18.2 (George Prevost translation, revised by M.B. Riddle; Philip Schaff, series editor. NPNF Series 1, Vol. 10)

**Context:** Chrysostom's *Homilies on Matthew* contain the most detailed verse-by-verse patristic commentary on the Sermon on the Mount (Homilies 15--24 cover Matthew 5--7 comprehensively). He was a pastor-preacher, and his homilies apply every command directly to the lives of his congregation.

**Sermon passages addressed:** "Whosoever shall smite thee on thy right cheek, turn to him the other also" (Matt 5:39). "Love your enemies" (Matt 5:44). "All things whatsoever ye would that men should do to you, do ye even so to them" (Matt 7:12). Chrysostom's Homilies 15--24 cover the full Sermon verse by verse; the passages above receive the most extended practical treatment.

**Significance:** Chrysostom insists Christ does not command the impossible. The Sermon's commands are difficult, but their difficulty is the point: they produce the character Christ intends. He addresses the possibility objection directly: "Let us not therefore suppose His injunctions impossible... they are very easy, if we are sober-minded" (18.2). He preaches this to ordinary congregants in Antioch and Constantinople, not to monks or ascetics.

### Augustine of Hippo, De Sermone Domini in Monte 1.3.10 and 1.2.4 (c. 394 AD, Hippo)

> "For this reason the number of sentences before us is to be carefully considered. For the beatitudes begin with humility: 'Blessed are the poor in spirit,' i.e. those not puffed up, while the soul submits itself to divine authority, fearing lest after this life it go away to punishment, although perhaps in this life it might seem to itself to be happy."
> --- Augustine, *De Sermone Domini in Monte* 1.3.10 (William Findlay translation, NPNF Series 1, Vol. 6)

> "Then, the meek are those who yield to acts of wickedness, and do not resist evil, but overcome evil with good."
> --- Augustine, *De Sermone Domini in Monte* 1.2.4 (William Findlay translation, NPNF Series 1, Vol. 6)

**Context:** Augustine's *De Sermone Domini in Monte* ("On the Lord's Sermon on the Mount," c. 394 AD) is the first full-length treatise devoted exclusively to the Sermon on the Mount. It covers every verse of Matthew 5--7 in two books. Augustine wrote it relatively early in his career; in his later *Retractationes* (2.21), he noted he would have treated some points differently with greater maturity.

**Sermon passages addressed:** "Blessed are the poor in spirit" (Matt 5:3). "Blessed are the meek" (Matt 5:5). Augustine works through all eight Beatitudes (Matt 5:3--12) as a sequence, linking them to the sevenfold gifts of the Spirit in Isaiah 11:2--3 and to the seven petitions of the Lord's Prayer (Matt 6:9--13). The treatise covers the full Sermon --- every verse of Matthew 5--7.

**Significance:** Augustine reads the Beatitudes as ascending stages of the spiritual life. He treats every verse of the Sermon as addressed to Christians and as requiring obedience. His key nuance: the Sermon's commands require grace. The Christian does not obey the Sermon by willpower alone but by the power of the Spirit. This becomes the standard Augustinian (and later Protestant) framework --- the Sermon is binding, but its demands drive the believer to dependence on Christ. Even in his later revisions, Augustine did not retract the basic claim that the Sermon addresses all believers.

## Observations

Several patterns emerge from this collection of sources spanning c. 70--400 AD.

**1. Unanimous application to present believers.** Not a single writer --- from the Didache to Augustine --- suggests that the Sermon is intended for a future dispensation, a past audience, or a different covenant community. Every writer treats it as addressed to Christians in the present age and as requiring obedience.

**2. Catechetical use from the earliest period.** The Didache embeds the Sermon's commands into baptismal instruction. Within a generation of the apostles, the church was teaching new converts to follow the Sermon's most difficult commands as the basic content of Christian formation, not as an advanced curriculum for spiritual athletes.

**3. Literal reading dominant before Nicaea.** Tertullian, Origen, and the Didache all apply the Sermon's commands on non-violence and oaths literally. The question of Christian military service was a live issue in the pre-Nicene church, and the Sermon was the primary text cited by those who argued Christians could not serve. The literal reading was not a minority position before Constantine; it was the mainstream.

**4. Allegorical readings supplemented, not replaced, literal ones.** Even Origen --- often cited as the great allegorizer --- held together the literal and spiritual senses. He read "turn the other cheek" as both spiritual struggle and instruction for actual non-violence. The allegorical and the literal were complementary layers in the pre-Nicene period, not competing alternatives.

**5. The Constantinian shift changed the conversation.** After the Edict of Milan (313 AD) and the rise of Christianity as the imperial religion, the practical implications of the literal reading became vastly more complicated. The post-Nicene move toward more nuanced and spiritualized readings is difficult to separate from this political transformation.

**6. No writer treats the Sermon as an impossible ideal.** The later Protestant reading of the Sermon as "law's mirror" --- showing us how far we fall short to drive us to grace --- is absent from the ante-Nicene period. The early fathers believed the Sermon could be obeyed, even if imperfectly and only with divine help. Augustine comes closest to this nuance by emphasizing grace as the condition of obedience, but even he does not suggest the commands exist merely to expose failure.

**7. The early church accepted the cost.** Early Christians were expelled from the military, refused to testify under oath, accepted physical assault without retaliation, and shared goods with the poor --- all based on their reading of the Sermon. The testimony from Tertullian, Justin, and the Didache reflects a community that lived the Sermon's commands and bore the social consequences.

## A Note on Completeness

This appendix includes every patristic source cited in Chapter 3. The Sermon on the Mount generated a larger body of patristic commentary than almost any other New Testament text. Beyond the sources collected here, significant additional discussions of the Sermon appear in:

- **Cyprian of Carthage** (c. 250 AD), *De Bono Patientiae* and *Ad Quirinum* --- extends Tertullian's literal reading
- **Lactantius** (c. 310 AD), *Divine Institutes* 6 --- applies Sermon ethics to Roman political philosophy
- **Athanasius** (c. 356 AD), *Life of Antony* --- the Sermon as the spark for Antony's renunciation of possessions
- **Jerome** (c. 398 AD), *Commentary on Matthew* --- verse-by-verse commentary with philological notes
- **Gregory of Nyssa** (c. 380 AD), *The Beatitudes* --- extended spiritual and mystical reading of Matt 5:3--12

These sources are noted here for readers who wish to pursue the patristic tradition beyond the scope of this study. Chapter 3 provides the analysis; this appendix provides the primary texts.
