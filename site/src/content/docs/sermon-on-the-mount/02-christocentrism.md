## Connecting to Chapter 1

Chapter 1's Scripture analysis established the contours of the question: Is the Sermon on the Mount (Matthew 5--7) for believers today, or was it addressed to a specific audience under specific conditions that no longer obtain? The textual evidence surfaced several open questions that grammar and context alone cannot fully resolve:

- Does the Sermon describe the ethics of a *future* millennial kingdom, or the ethics of a kingdom that is *already present* in the life of every believer?
- Does the audience --- "his disciples" (Matt 5:1) --- limit the Sermon's scope to those who heard it on that hillside, to all disciples in every age, or to a Jewish remnant awaiting the kingdom?
- How do the Sermon's radical demands (turn the other cheek, do not resist evil, be perfect as your Father in heaven is perfect) relate to the rest of Jesus's teaching and to the apostolic letters?

The Christocentric lens brings a dimension that no amount of grammatical analysis can supply on its own: the character and priorities of the one who spoke these words. The Sermon on the Mount is not anonymous legislation. It was delivered by a specific person who also *lived* its demands, commissioned others to teach them, and staked the credibility of his kingdom on their being practiced. If we want to know whether the Sermon is for today, we must ask: What did the Speaker himself indicate? And did he live as though his own words were meant to be taken seriously?

## Jesus as the Speaker --- The Authority Question

The Sermon on the Mount is distinguished from every other ethical discourse in Scripture by the formula Jesus repeats six times in Matthew 5: "Ye have heard that it was said... But I say unto you."

This is not commentary. It is not rabbinic midrash in which a teacher draws out the meaning of an earlier authority. It is a claim of personal authority *over* the Torah itself --- a claim so audacious that it would be blasphemous from any merely human speaker.

The six antitheses in full:

**On murder (Matt 5:21--22):**

> "Ye have heard that it was said by them of old time, Thou shalt not kill; and whosoever shall kill shall be in danger of the judgment: But I say unto you, That whosoever is angry with his brother without a cause shall be in danger of the judgment: and whosoever shall say to his brother, Raca, shall be in danger of the council: but whosoever shall say, Thou fool, shall be in danger of hell fire."
> --- Matthew 5:21--22 (KJV)

**On adultery (Matt 5:27--28):**

> "Ye have heard that it was said by them of old time, Thou shalt not commit adultery: But I say unto you, That whosoever looketh on a woman to lust after her hath committed adultery with her already in his heart."
> --- Matthew 5:27--28 (KJV)

**On divorce (Matt 5:31--32):**

> "It hath been said, Whosoever shall put away his wife, let him give her a writing of divorcement: But I say unto you, That whosoever shall put away his wife, saving for the cause of fornication, causeth her to commit adultery: and whosoever shall marry her that is divorced committeth adultery."
> --- Matthew 5:31--32 (KJV)

**On oaths (Matt 5:33--34):**

> "Again, ye have heard that it hath been said by them of old time, Thou shalt not forswear thyself, but shalt perform unto the Lord thine oaths: But I say unto you, Swear not at all; neither by heaven; for it is God's throne."
> --- Matthew 5:33--34 (KJV)

**On retaliation (Matt 5:38--39):**

> "Ye have heard that it hath been said, An eye for an eye, and a tooth for a tooth: But I say unto you, That ye resist not evil: but whosoever shall smite thee on thy right cheek, turn to him the other also."
> --- Matthew 5:38--39 (KJV)

**On love for enemies (Matt 5:43--44):**

> "Ye have heard that it hath been said, Thou shalt love thy neighbour, and hate thine enemy. But I say unto you, Love your enemies, bless them that curse you, do good to them that hate you, and pray for them which despitefully use you, and persecute you."
> --- Matthew 5:43--44 (KJV)

In each case, Jesus sets his own word alongside --- and above --- the Mosaic code. He is not abolishing the law; he says as much explicitly (Matt 5:17). He is *deepening* it, pressing past external compliance to the interior condition that the law was always meant to address. Murder becomes anger. Adultery becomes lust. The legal floor becomes a moral ceiling.

And the crowd knew exactly what was happening. Matthew records their response:

> "And it came to pass, when Jesus had ended these sayings, the people were astonished at his doctrine: For he taught them as one having authority, and not as the scribes."
> --- Matthew 7:28--29 (KJV)

"As one having authority" --- *hōs exousian echōn*. The scribes taught by citation: "Rabbi Hillel said... Rabbi Shammai said..." They derived authority from the chain of transmission. Jesus derived authority from himself. He did not cite a higher human source because there was none. The implication is unavoidable: this is not a prophet bringing God's word from outside himself; this is the Lawgiver, the one who stood behind Moses on Sinai, now speaking in his own name.

If the Speaker has this authority --- an authority the text itself affirms --- then the question of whether his teaching is "for today" takes on a different weight. One does not casually relegate the Lawgiver's own teaching to a future dispensation without very strong exegetical grounds for doing so.

## Jesus as the Exemplar --- Did He Live the Sermon?

The most powerful testimony to whether the Sermon on the Mount is meant to be lived is the fact that Jesus himself lived it. He did not give commands he was unwilling to keep. Every major demand of the Sermon finds its embodiment in the life of the one who preached it.

### Turn the Other Cheek

Jesus commanded: "Whosoever shall smite thee on thy right cheek, turn to him the other also" (Matt 5:39).

Before the high priest, Jesus was struck by an officer:

> "And when he had thus spoken, one of the officers which stood by struck Jesus with the palm of his hand, saying, Answerest thou the high priest so? Jesus answered him, If I have spoken evil, bear witness of the evil: but if well, why smitest thou me?"
> --- John 18:22--23 (KJV)

Jesus did not strike back. He responded with a question --- calm, measured, appealing to justice without retaliation. And at the cross, he endured not merely a slap but scourging, mockery, and crucifixion without resistance.

> "Who, when he was reviled, reviled not again; when he suffered, he threatened not; but committed himself to him that judgeth righteously."
> --- 1 Peter 2:23 (KJV)

Peter, who witnessed the Passion, recognized in it the pattern of the Sermon's own ethic.

### Love Your Enemies

Jesus commanded: "Love your enemies, bless them that curse you, do good to them that hate you, and pray for them which despitefully use you, and persecute you" (Matt 5:44).

From the cross, while his enemies were executing him:

> "Then said Jesus, Father, forgive them; for they know not what they do."
> --- Luke 23:34 (KJV)

This is not a general wish for humanity. It is a specific prayer for specific enemies --- the soldiers driving the nails, the religious leaders who engineered his death, the crowd that chose Barabbas. Jesus loved his enemies in the extremity of their enmity. The Sermon's command was not theoretical for him.

### Do Not Resist Evil

Jesus commanded: "Resist not evil" (Matt 5:39).

Throughout the Passion narratives, Jesus's non-resistance is deliberate and repeated. When Peter drew a sword in Gethsemane, Jesus stopped him:

> "Then said Jesus unto him, Put up again thy sword into his place: for all they that take the sword shall perish with the sword. Thinkest thou that I cannot now pray to my Father, and he shall presently give me more than twelve legions of angels?"
> --- Matthew 26:52--53 (KJV)

The point is not that Jesus lacked the capacity to resist. He had twelve legions of angels at his disposal. His non-resistance was not weakness; it was chosen obedience --- the very thing the Sermon demands of his followers.

### Go the Extra Mile

Jesus commanded: "Whosoever shall compel thee to go a mile, go with him twain" (Matt 5:41).

The entire incarnation can be understood as "going beyond." Paul captures this in language that echoes the Sermon's logic of excess:

> "For ye know the grace of our Lord Jesus Christ, that, though he was rich, yet for your sakes he became poor, that ye through his poverty might be rich."
> --- 2 Corinthians 8:9 (KJV)

He was not compelled to come. He chose to come. And having come, he did not stop at teaching or healing; he went to the cross. The one who told others to go the second mile walked all the way to Golgotha.

### Give to Him That Asketh

Jesus commanded: "Give to him that asketh thee, and from him that would borrow of thee turn not thou away" (Matt 5:42).

Jesus's ministry was a sustained pattern of giving without condition. He healed lepers, restored sight, fed thousands, and cast out demons --- often for people who never asked and never thanked him. The ten lepers are the sharpest example: ten cleansed, one returned to give thanks (Luke 17:12--19). Jesus gave without demanding reciprocity.

### Be Reconciled to Your Brother

Jesus commanded: "First be reconciled to thy brother, and then come and offer thy gift" (Matt 5:24).

After the resurrection, it was Jesus who took the initiative toward the disciples who had abandoned him. He did not wait for Peter to come to him. He sought Peter out, cooked breakfast for him on the shore, and restored him with a threefold question that undid the threefold denial:

> "He saith unto him the third time, Simon, son of Jonas, lovest thou me? Peter was grieved because he said unto him the third time, Lovest thou me? And he said unto him, Lord, thou knowest all things; thou knowest that I love thee. Jesus saith unto him, Feed my sheep."
> --- John 21:17 (KJV)

The Reconciler sought reconciliation. The one who commanded his followers to take the initiative toward a broken relationship did exactly that.

### Pray in Secret

Jesus commanded: "But thou, when thou prayest, enter into thy closet, and when thou hast shut thy door, pray to thy Father which is in secret" (Matt 6:6).

Mark records a pattern that runs throughout Jesus's ministry:

> "And in the morning, rising up a great while before day, he went out, and departed into a solitary place, and there prayed."
> --- Mark 1:35 (KJV)

> "And he withdrew himself into the wilderness, and prayed."
> --- Luke 5:16 (KJV)

Jesus's prayer life was not performed for an audience. He withdrew --- repeatedly, deliberately, sometimes at great cost to his schedule. The command to pray in secret was his own practice before it was anyone else's.

### Do Not Lay Up Treasures on Earth

Jesus commanded: "Lay not up for yourselves treasures upon earth" (Matt 6:19).

He lived this with startling literalness:

> "And Jesus saith unto him, The foxes have holes, and the birds of the air have nests; but the Son of man hath not where to lay his head."
> --- Matthew 8:20 (KJV)

The one who told others not to store up earthly treasure owned nothing. He was born in a borrowed manger, preached from a borrowed boat, rode a borrowed donkey, ate a borrowed Passover meal, was buried in a borrowed tomb. The pattern is not accidental. It is the Sermon on the Mount made flesh.

## The Great Commission: The Decisive Passage

If there is a single text that bears most directly on whether the Sermon on the Mount is for believers today, it is the Great Commission in Matthew 28. The question is not subtle, and the text is not ambiguous:

> "And Jesus came and spake unto them, saying, All power is given unto me in heaven and in earth. Go ye therefore, and teach all nations, baptizing them in the name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Ghost: Teaching them to observe all things whatsoever I have commanded you: and, lo, I am with you alway, even unto the end of the world. Amen."
> --- Matthew 28:18--20 (KJV)

Three elements of this commission bear directly on the Sermon's applicability.

### "All Things Whatsoever I Have Commanded You"

The phrase is *panta hosa eneteilamēn humin* --- "all things, as many as I commanded you." The scope is comprehensive. Jesus does not say "some things" or "the things that apply to your dispensation" or "the things that are feasible." He says *all things*. And the Sermon on the Mount is the largest single block of Jesus's commands in the Gospel of Matthew. It is difficult to imagine how the apostles could have heard "all things whatsoever I have commanded you" and excluded chapters 5 through 7.

### "All Nations"

The Greek is *panta ta ethnē* --- "all the nations," "all the Gentiles." This is not a commission limited to Israel. It is not a commission for the Jewish disciples to teach a Jewish audience a Jewish ethic. It is explicitly universal. Whatever Jesus commanded is to be taught to every nation. If the Sermon on the Mount is kingdom legislation intended only for ethnic Israel in a future dispensation, then its inclusion in the "all things" of the Great Commission is puzzling at best and misleading at worst.

### "Unto the End of the World"

The phrase *heōs tēs synteleias tou aiōnos* --- "until the consummation of the age" --- establishes the temporal scope. Jesus's commands are to be taught and observed not merely in the apostolic generation, not merely until the canon is complete, but until the very end of the present age. The Sermon is not time-limited by the Great Commission; it is time-*extended*.

### The Dispensationalist Response

Those who hold that the Sermon on the Mount is kingdom legislation for a future age must account for the Great Commission, and the most thoughtful among them do. The argument runs as follows: the Great Commission itself may be a "kingdom commission," part of the same eschatological program that includes the Sermon. On this reading, Matthew 28:18--20 is addressed to the Jewish disciples as representatives of the future kingdom, and the church age --- the "mystery parenthesis" between Israel's rejection of the kingdom and its future restoration --- operates under a different set of instructions, primarily the Pauline epistles.

This is a coherent reading, but it requires a very specific hermeneutical framework (the sharp distinction between Israel and the church) and it raises its own difficulties. If the Great Commission is not for the church today, then the church's basis for baptizing and making disciples is undermined. Most dispensationalists do not press the point this far; they acknowledge that the Great Commission applies to the church while maintaining that the Sermon's ethics are aspirational rather than normative. But the tension remains: how can the commission to teach "all things whatsoever I have commanded you" apply to the church while the largest body of those commands does not?

## Jesus's Own Hermeneutic of the Law

Before evaluating whether the Sermon applies today, it is worth asking how Jesus himself handled the question of which commandments are binding and how. His hermeneutical method is visible in several key passages.

### The Summary of the Law (Matt 22:36--40)

When a lawyer asked which commandment was greatest, Jesus answered:

> "Jesus said unto him, Thou shalt love the Lord thy God with all thy heart, and with all thy soul, and with all thy mind. This is the first and great commandment. And the second is like unto it, Thou shalt love thy neighbour as thyself. On these two commandments hang all the law and the prophets."
> --- Matthew 22:37--40 (KJV)

"On these two commandments *hang* all the law and the prophets." The verb is *krematai* --- the entire Torah and the entire prophetic corpus are *suspended from* these two commands, as a door hangs from its hinges. Love of God and love of neighbor are not two more commands among six hundred thirteen; they are the structural principle from which all the others derive their meaning and authority.

This hermeneutic illuminates the Sermon on the Mount directly. The Sermon is organized around love: the antitheses move from external compliance to interior transformation, from legal obligation to sacrificial generosity, and the climax of the entire series is love for enemies (Matt 5:43--48). The Sermon is, in structure and substance, an exposition of what it means to love God and neighbor. If the summary of the law is binding on all people in all ages --- and no major Christian tradition denies this --- then the Sermon, as its exposition, participates in the same permanence.

### The Weightier Matters (Matt 23:23)

Jesus rebuked the scribes and Pharisees not for keeping the law but for keeping the wrong parts of it at the expense of the right ones:

> "Woe unto you, scribes and Pharisees, hypocrites! for ye pay tithe of mint and anise and cummin, and have omitted the weightier matters of the law, judgment, mercy, and faith: these ought ye to have done, and not to leave the other undone."
> --- Matthew 23:23 (KJV)

"The weightier matters of the law: judgment, mercy, and faith." Jesus does not treat all commandments as equal. Some are heavier, more central, more urgent. And the heavy ones are not the ceremonial or the ritual; they are the ethical --- justice, mercy, faithfulness. The Sermon on the Mount is almost entirely about these "weightier matters." It says nothing about tithing herbs or ritual purity. It is relentlessly ethical, relentlessly concerned with interior transformation and neighbor-love. By Jesus's own hermeneutical standard, the Sermon contains the weightiest material in the Torah tradition.

### The Sabbath Principle (Mark 2:27)

When criticized for his disciples' behavior on the Sabbath, Jesus responded:

> "And he said unto them, The sabbath was made for man, and not man for the sabbath."
> --- Mark 2:27 (KJV)

This is a hermeneutical principle, not merely a ruling about Sabbath. The law exists *for the benefit of the people it governs*, not as an end in itself. Commands are not arbitrary tests of obedience; they are designed to produce human flourishing. Applied to the Sermon on the Mount, this principle suggests that the Sermon's demands --- turn the other cheek, love your enemies, do not worry, judge not --- are given for human benefit. They are not impossible burdens imposed to expose failure; they are the path of life that Jesus both taught and walked.

## The Already/Not-Yet Kingdom

One of the most important Christocentric contributions to the question is Jesus's teaching on the nature of his kingdom. The Sermon on the Mount is explicitly kingdom discourse --- it opens with "the kingdom of heaven" (Matt 5:3, 10) and is saturated with kingdom language throughout. If we can understand when and how Jesus understood his kingdom to operate, we can better understand whether the Sermon's ethics are for now, for later, or for both.

### The Kingdom Is at Hand

Jesus's public ministry opened with a declaration:

> "From that time Jesus began to preach, and to say, Repent: for the kingdom of heaven is at hand."
> --- Matthew 4:17 (KJV)

"At hand" --- *ēngiken* --- means "has drawn near," "has arrived," "is upon you." The kingdom is not a distant future reality in Jesus's announcement. It is pressing into the present. It demands response *now* --- repentance *now* --- because it is *here* now.

### The Kingdom Is Still Coming

And yet the Sermon itself contains a prayer that the kingdom is not yet fully realized:

> "Thy kingdom come. Thy will be done in earth, as it is in heaven."
> --- Matthew 6:10 (KJV)

If the kingdom had fully arrived, there would be no need to pray for its coming. The prayer assumes a gap between what is and what will be --- between the kingdom's inauguration and its consummation. God's will is done in heaven; it is not yet fully done on earth. The Sermon's ethics describe life in that gap.

### Kingdom Ethics for the Already/Not-Yet

This has direct implications for the question at hand. If the kingdom is entirely future --- belonging to a millennial age after Christ's return --- then the Sermon's ethics are future legislation, a code of conduct for a world that does not yet exist. But if the kingdom is already present, even partially, then the Sermon's ethics are *present* obligations, even if they cannot be perfectly realized until the kingdom is fully consummated.

Jesus's own language points to the second option. The Beatitudes are in the present tense: "Blessed *are* the poor in spirit, for theirs *is* the kingdom of heaven" (Matt 5:3). Not "blessed *will be*" or "theirs *will be*." The kingdom belongs to the poor in spirit *now*. The peacemakers are called sons of God *now*. The meek will inherit the earth --- that is future --- but they are blessed *now*, in their meekness, because they already belong to the kingdom that is already at hand.

The Sermon describes what it looks like to live as a kingdom citizen in a world where the kingdom is real but not yet universal --- where God's will is being done but is not yet done on earth as it is in heaven. This is precisely the condition of every believer between Pentecost and the return of Christ.

### The Spirit as the Enabler

If the Sermon's demands seem impossible --- and they do, to anyone who takes them seriously --- Jesus himself provided the means of obedience. In his farewell discourse, he promised:

> "If ye love me, keep my commandments. And I will pray the Father, and he shall give you another Comforter, that he may abide with you for ever; Even the Spirit of truth; whom the world cannot receive, because it seeth him not, neither knoweth him: but ye know him; for he dwelleth with you, and shall be in you."
> --- John 14:15--17 (KJV)

> "But the Comforter, which is the Holy Ghost, whom the Father will send in my name, he shall teach you all things, and bring all things to your remembrance, whatsoever I have said unto you."
> --- John 14:26 (KJV)

> "Howbeit when he, the Spirit of truth, is come, he will guide you into all truth."
> --- John 16:13 (KJV)

The sequence is significant. "Keep my commandments" (John 14:15) --- which includes, at minimum, everything in the Sermon on the Mount. And then: "I will give you the Comforter" --- the Spirit who enables what the flesh cannot achieve on its own. The Sermon's demands are not given in a vacuum. They are given by one who also gives the Spirit to those who believe. The impossibility of the Sermon is real; the provision for its obedience is equally real.

Paul makes this connection explicit:

> "For the law of the Spirit of life in Christ Jesus hath made me free from the law of sin and death. For what the law could not do, in that it was weak through the flesh, God sending his own Son in the likeness of sinful flesh, and for sin, condemned sin in the flesh: That the righteousness of the law might be fulfilled in us, who walk not after the flesh, but after the Spirit."
> --- Romans 8:2--4 (KJV)

"The righteousness of the law" --- including the deepened law of the Sermon --- is "fulfilled in us" who walk by the Spirit. The Sermon is not set aside because it is too hard. It is made possible because the Spirit has come.

## Love and Unity as Jesus's Priorities

The question of the Sermon's applicability cannot be separated from Jesus's overarching priorities, which he stated with unmistakable clarity.

### The New Commandment

On the night of his betrayal, Jesus gave what he himself called a "new commandment":

> "A new commandment I give unto you, That ye love one another; as I have loved you, that ye also love one another. By this shall all men know that ye are my disciples, if ye have love one to another."
> --- John 13:34--35 (KJV)

What is "new" about this commandment is not the obligation to love --- that is as old as Leviticus 19:18. What is new is the standard: "as I have loved you." Jesus's own self-giving love becomes the measure. And the Sermon on the Mount is the most sustained description in all four Gospels of what that self-giving love looks like in daily life: turning the other cheek, going the extra mile, loving enemies, giving without reciprocity, praying for persecutors.

If the Sermon is not for today, then the new commandment loses its primary illustration. The two cannot be separated without doing violence to both.

### The Prayer for Unity

In his high-priestly prayer, Jesus prayed for every future believer:

> "Neither pray I for these alone, but for them also which shall believe on me through their word; That they all may be one; as thou, Father, art in me, and I in thee, that they also may be one in us: that the world may believe that thou hast sent me."
> --- John 17:20--23 (KJV)

Unity is not an optional feature of Christian community; it is the means by which "the world may believe." And the Sermon on the Mount is a sustained instruction in the practices that make unity possible: reconciliation before worship (Matt 5:23--24), generous interpretation of others (Matt 7:1--5), treating others as you wish to be treated (Matt 7:12). If the Sermon is set aside as future legislation, the very practices that Jesus prayed would characterize his people are deferred to an age when they will no longer be needed in the same way.

### The Sermon's Own Architecture of Love

The Sermon on the Mount is not a random collection of ethical sayings. It has a structure, and that structure is organized around love. The antitheses begin with anger and end with the command to love enemies (Matt 5:21--48). The climax of the antitheses --- "Love your enemies... that ye may be the children of your Father which is in heaven" (Matt 5:44--45) --- is immediately followed by the Sermon's most theologically loaded command:

> "Be ye therefore perfect, even as your Father which is in heaven is perfect."
> --- Matthew 5:48 (KJV)

The "perfection" (*teleios*) in view is not sinless performance. In context, it is the wholeness of love that does not discriminate between friend and enemy, because the Father's love does not discriminate between the just and the unjust (Matt 5:45). This is the same *teleios* root that appears in 1 Corinthians 13:10, and its presence here reinforces what the entire Sermon is about: the maturation of love into something that resembles the love of God himself.

The Golden Rule, placed near the Sermon's conclusion, functions as a summary statement:

> "Therefore all things whatsoever ye would that men should do to you, do ye even so to them: for this is the law and the prophets."
> --- Matthew 7:12 (KJV)

"This is the law and the prophets" --- echoing Jesus's summary of the law in Matthew 22:40. The Golden Rule and the love commands are, in Jesus's own teaching, the hermeneutical key to the entire Old Testament. The Sermon does not add new legislation alongside the old. It *fulfills* the old by revealing what the law always meant.

If the Sermon's love ethic is not for today, then what is left? The new commandment (John 13:34) presupposes the Sermon's teaching on love. The Great Commission (Matt 28:20) commands the teaching of everything Jesus commanded. The summary of the law (Matt 22:37--40) identifies love as the hinge on which everything else hangs. Remove the Sermon, and the supporting architecture of Jesus's entire ethical teaching is weakened.

## Summary: What the Christocentric Lens Reveals

The Christocentric examination of the Sermon on the Mount yields the following:

**On authority:** Jesus speaks the Sermon not as a rabbi interpreting Torah but as the Lawgiver himself, with authority that surpasses Moses. The "But I say unto you" formula claims personal legislative authority over the law. If this Speaker intends his words for a specific audience and a specific time only, the text gives no indication of it.

**On exemplification:** Jesus lived the Sermon. He turned the other cheek, loved his enemies, prayed for his persecutors, gave without condition, reconciled with his disciples, prayed in secret, and owned nothing. If the Sermon is not for today, then Jesus lived by a standard he did not intend for his followers --- a conclusion that sits uneasily with the rest of his teaching.

**On the Great Commission:** Jesus commanded his apostles to teach *all nations* to observe *all things* he commanded, *until the end of the age*. The Sermon on the Mount is the largest block of commands in Matthew's Gospel. The most natural reading of the Great Commission includes it. The dispensationalist response --- that the Great Commission itself is kingdom legislation --- is coherent but carries significant costs.

**On Jesus's hermeneutic:** Jesus identified love of God and love of neighbor as the foundation of the entire law. He named justice, mercy, and faithfulness as "the weightier matters." He taught that the law exists for human benefit. The Sermon on the Mount is organized around precisely these concerns. By Jesus's own hermeneutical standard, the Sermon contains the most essential, not the most dispensable, material.

**On the already/not-yet kingdom:** Jesus inaugurated his kingdom as a present reality while teaching his disciples to pray for its future consummation. The Sermon describes kingdom ethics for people living in the overlap --- citizens of a kingdom that is real but not yet fully realized. This is the condition of every believer between Pentecost and the return of Christ.

**On the Spirit:** Jesus promised the Holy Spirit to enable obedience to his commands. The impossibility of the Sermon is real; the provision for its practice is equally real. Paul confirms that "the righteousness of the law" is fulfilled in those who walk by the Spirit (Rom 8:4).

**On love and unity:** Jesus's overarching priorities --- love (John 13:34--35) and unity (John 17:20--23) --- are precisely the priorities of the Sermon on the Mount. Removing the Sermon from present Christian obligation removes the primary illustration of what Jesus's love looks like in daily practice.

The Christocentric lens does not render a verdict; the evidence must be weighed alongside the other SCAR components. But it does create a strong presumption: the one who spoke the Sermon lived it, commissioned its teaching to all nations until the end of the age, and provided the Spirit to enable its practice. Any reading that sets the Sermon aside as not for today must account for all of this evidence --- and must explain why the Preacher lived by a code he did not intend for his people.
