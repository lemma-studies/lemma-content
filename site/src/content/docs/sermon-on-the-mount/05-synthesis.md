## What Has Come Before

This study has examined the question "Is the Sermon on the Mount for believers today to follow?" through four distinct lenses:

- **Scripture** (Chapter 1) examined the Sermon passage by passage, word by word, in Greek and in context --- from the audience question (Matt 5:1--2) through the hinge passage on fulfillment (5:17--20), the six antitheses, the two builders parable (7:24--27), the Great Commission (28:18--20), and the extensive NT echoes in James, Paul, and Peter.
- **Christocentrism** (Chapter 2) asked whether the Speaker himself lived the Sermon, what his own hermeneutic of the law reveals, and how the Great Commission, the promise of the Holy Spirit, and Jesus's priorities of love and unity bear on the question.
- **Antiquity** (Chapter 3) surveyed the earliest Christian witness --- from the Didache (c. 70--100 AD) through Augustine (c. 394 AD) --- documenting how the pre-Nicene church read, taught, and practiced the Sermon's commands.
- **Reality** (Chapter 4) presented four major interpretive traditions at their strongest: Literal Obedience (Anabaptist), Kingdom Ethics for All Believers (Mainstream Evangelical/Reformed), the Law's Mirror (Lutheran), and Future Kingdom/Primarily for Israel (Classical Dispensational).

This chapter draws those four threads together. It names the common ground that all views share (more than most expect), identifies the genuine tensions that remain (without resolving them), notes where the cumulative evidence points, and offers questions to help the reader weigh the evidence personally.

## Areas of Agreement

The debate over the Sermon on the Mount is often framed as a sharp divide. In reality, the areas of agreement across all four views are substantial --- and more important for Christian unity than the areas of disagreement. A reader who begins here will find the common ground surprisingly wide.

### 1. Jesus speaks with supreme authority

Every view confesses that the Sermon on the Mount is the word of the Son of God. The "But I say unto you" formula (Matt 5:22, 28, 32, 34, 39, 44) claims authority over the Torah itself, and the crowds recognized it:

> "For he taught them as one having authority, and not as the scribes."
> --- Matthew 7:28--29 (KJV)

No Anabaptist, evangelical, Lutheran, or dispensationalist denies this. Jesus is not a rabbi offering an opinion. He is the Lawgiver.

### 2. The Christian life requires transformation

Even the strongest dispensationalist who reserves the Sermon for a future kingdom applies Romans 12:14--21 --- which says substantially the same things --- to the church today. Even the most rigorous Lutheran who reads the Sermon as law's mirror expects that faith in Christ will produce the fruit of the Spirit (Gal 5:22--23). Every view insists that following Jesus means a changed life. No one reads the Sermon and concludes that Christians may remain as they were.

### 3. The Sermon addresses the heart, not merely external behavior

The six antitheses move from external act to interior disposition: murder to anger, adultery to lust, oath-keeping to transparent honesty. All four views recognize that the Sermon's deepest demand is internal transformation, not behavioral compliance alone. The righteousness that exceeds the scribes and Pharisees (Matt 5:20) is a righteousness of the heart.

### 4. Love is the governing principle

Jesus himself identified love of God and love of neighbor as the two commandments on which "all the law and the prophets" hang (Matt 22:37--40). The Sermon on the Mount is organized around love --- from reconciliation before worship (5:23--24) through enemy-love (5:44--48) to the Golden Rule (7:12). Every tradition agrees that love is the Sermon's center of gravity.

### 5. The Beatitudes describe genuine blessedness

No major tradition disputes that poverty of spirit, mourning, meekness, hunger for righteousness, mercy, purity of heart, peacemaking, and endurance under persecution are genuinely blessed conditions. The debate is about *how* these qualities are cultivated and *when* they are fully realized --- not about whether they are real.

### 6. Jesus lived the Sermon

As Chapter 2 documented in detail, Jesus turned the other cheek, loved his enemies, prayed for his persecutors, gave without condition, prayed in secret, and owned nothing. Every view acknowledges that the Speaker practiced what he preached. The one who said "Love your enemies" prayed from the cross, "Father, forgive them" (Luke 23:34). The one who said "Lay not up for yourselves treasures upon earth" had nowhere to lay his head (Matt 8:20).

### 7. Grace and obedience are not enemies

This may be the most important point of agreement, because it is the one most often obscured by the debate. No major Christian tradition teaches that obedience to the Sermon earns salvation. And no major Christian tradition teaches that grace eliminates the call to obey. Paul, who wrote "by grace are ye saved through faith" (Eph 2:8), also wrote "Bless them which persecute you" (Rom 12:14). James, who wrote "be ye doers of the word" (James 1:22), also wrote of "the royal law according to the scripture" --- love of neighbor (James 2:8). Grace saves. The saved are called to obey. These are not in tension; they are in sequence.

### 8. The Sermon's NT echoes are real

As the cross-reference table in Chapter 1 documented, the Sermon's themes permeate the entire New Testament --- James, Paul, Peter, and John all echo the Sermon's teaching. Every view acknowledges this. The disagreement is about what the echoes mean (direct application or analogical overlap), not about whether they exist.

### 9. Hearing alone is not enough

The Sermon's own conclusion is the one thing no view disputes:

> "Therefore whosoever heareth these sayings of mine, and doeth them, I will liken him unto a wise man, which built his house upon a rock."
> --- Matthew 7:24 (KJV)

The wise builder hears *and* does. Even the Lutheran who reads the Sermon as an impossible ideal that drives to grace expects that encounter with grace to produce a changed life. Even the dispensationalist who reserves the Sermon for a future kingdom does not suggest that admiring the Sermon from a distance is what Jesus intended.

### 10. The Sermon does not contradict Paul --- it complements him

A persistent worry in the dispensational tradition is that applying the Sermon to the church means pitting Jesus against Paul, or law against grace. But as Chapter 1 demonstrated, Paul's ethical instruction in Romans 12--14, Galatians 5--6, and throughout the epistles aligns closely with the Sermon's teaching. Paul did not see himself as replacing Jesus's instruction; he saw himself as applying it. The Sermon and the epistles are not rival programs. They are two witnesses to the same ethic of love, spoken by the same Spirit.

### 11. The Sermon remains central to Christian identity

No church tradition treats the Sermon on the Mount as peripheral. It is the most-quoted, most-preached, and most-studied block of Jesus's teaching in the entire Christian tradition. The Beatitudes are recited in liturgy across liturgical traditions. The Lord's Prayer (Matt 6:9--13) is prayed by every denomination. The Golden Rule (Matt 7:12) is recognized by believers and non-believers alike as the distilled ethical wisdom of the Christian faith. Whatever their hermeneutical differences, all four views treat the Sermon as indispensable.

## Areas of Tension

The common ground is real, but so are the disagreements. What follows are the genuine tensions that remain --- named honestly, with the strongest argument from each side. These are not resolved here. They are presented so the reader can weigh them.

### Tension 1: What does "fulfil" mean?

The word *pleroo* ("fulfil") in Matthew 5:17 is the hinge on which the entire debate turns, as Appendix A documented in detail.

> "Think not that I am come to destroy the law, or the prophets: I am not come to destroy, but to fulfil."
> --- Matthew 5:17 (KJV)

- **On one side:** "Fulfil" means bring to full expression. Jesus deepens the law, extending it from external compliance to the heart's interior. The Sermon *is* the law in its completed, Christ-interpreted form, and believers follow it as such. This reading finds strong support in the six antitheses (5:21--48), which intensify rather than abolish the Mosaic commands, and in Paul's use of the same word: "Love is the fulfilling (*pleroma*) of the law" (Rom 13:10, KJV).

- **On the other side:** "Fulfil" means prophetic completion. Jesus is the reality to which the law pointed, and his coming brings the law's purpose to its intended terminus. On this reading, Christians are under "the law of Christ" (Gal 6:2), not the Mosaic code, and the Sermon describes the old-covenant law in its final expression before the cross inaugurated a new economy. Matthew's own dominant use of *pleroo* throughout the Gospel is prophetic fulfillment (1:22; 2:15, 17, 23; 4:14; etc.).

- **What makes this genuinely difficult:** The word itself sustains both readings. Matthew uses *pleroo* prophetically elsewhere, but the immediate context of 5:17--48 points to ethical deepening. The interpreter must decide which context controls --- the broader Matthean pattern or the immediate Sermon passage.

### Tension 2: Is the Sermon achievable?

- **Views 1 and 2 say yes.** The Sermon is genuinely livable --- costly, imperfect in this age, empowered by the Spirit, but real. Paul confirms this: "the righteousness of the law might be fulfilled in us, who walk not after the flesh, but after the Spirit" (Rom 8:4, KJV). Anabaptist communities have practiced Sermon ethics for five centuries. The Amish forgiveness at Nickel Mines (2006) is not an anomaly; it is the fruit of a tradition that takes the Sermon at its word.

- **View 3 says the difficulty is the point.** The demands are intentionally impossible precisely because they are meant to humble: "Be ye therefore perfect, even as your Father which is in heaven is perfect" (Matt 5:48, KJV). If anger is murder (5:22) and lust is adultery (5:28), then every human being is condemned. The Sermon shows us our need for grace.

- **What makes this genuinely difficult:** The Lutheran insight is not wrong. The Sermon's demands *are* staggering, and the honest believer *does* feel their weight. But Jesus used imperative verbs --- commands to be obeyed, not merely admired. The question is whether "these commands humble us" and "these commands are to be obeyed" are mutually exclusive, or whether they describe two dimensions of the same reality. Can a command both expose our inability *and* call us to genuine obedience through the Spirit?

### Tension 3: Who is the audience?

- **Views 1, 2, and 3 say all believers.** The grammatical evidence (Matt 5:1--2: "his disciples came unto him"), the Great Commission (28:20: "all nations... all things whatsoever I have commanded you"), and the extensive NT echoes in James, Paul, and Peter all point to a universal audience. The Didache used the Sermon for catechesis of all converts. Justin Martyr presented it to the Roman emperor as what Christians actually do.

- **View 4 says primarily Israel.** The Sermon was addressed to Jewish disciples in a Jewish context, with Jewish social referents (the temple altar, the Sanhedrin, oaths by Jerusalem). The "kingdom of heaven" is a Jewish phrase pointing to Israel's promised messianic kingdom. The church, which did not yet exist (Matt 16:18 --- future tense), operates under a different dispensational arrangement, primarily the Pauline epistles.

- **What makes this genuinely difficult:** The Jewish context of the Sermon is real. The references to the altar (5:23--24), Jewish courts (5:25), and the Sanhedrin (5:22) do presuppose a Jewish social world. But the Great Commission explicitly extends Jesus's commands to "all nations," and Luke's Sermon on the Plain delivers the same teaching to a Gentile-accessible audience (Luke 6:17--49). The question is whether Jewish context limits application or merely colors illustration.

### Tension 4: Does the "already/not yet" kingdom resolve or defer the question?

- **For Views 1 and 2:** The kingdom is already inaugurated ("theirs *is* the kingdom of heaven," Matt 5:3 --- present tense). The Sermon describes how citizens of the present kingdom live now, in the tension between inauguration and consummation. The ethics are operative because the kingdom is operative.

- **For View 4:** The kingdom of heaven is the millennial kingdom promised to Israel. Its full establishment is future. The Sermon describes the ethics of that future kingdom. The church age is a "parenthesis" operating under the dispensation of grace.

- **What makes this genuinely difficult:** Both sides have scriptural support. The present tense of "theirs *is*" (5:3, 10) and "the kingdom of God is come unto you" (Matt 12:28) favor the already-present reading. But "Thy kingdom come" (Matt 6:10) acknowledges the kingdom has not yet fully arrived. The interpreter must weigh whether "not yet fully" means "not yet operative" or "operative but incomplete."

### Tension 5: The role of the Holy Spirit

- **Views 1, 2, and 4 acknowledge the Spirit's role, though differently.** Views 1 and 2 hold that the Spirit enables obedience to the Sermon in the present. View 4 holds that the Spirit's indwelling ministry is the distinctive mark of the church age, and the Sermon --- delivered before Pentecost --- does not presuppose this reality.

- **View 3 holds that the Spirit's role is primarily to convict:** The Spirit uses the Sermon to show us our sin, driving us to depend on Christ's righteousness rather than our own.

- **What makes this genuinely difficult:** The Sermon itself does not mention the Holy Spirit. Jesus promises the Spirit in the Upper Room Discourse (John 14:15--17, 26; 16:13), and Paul confirms that the Spirit enables the law's fulfillment (Rom 8:2--4). But the Sermon's silence about the Spirit is a genuine textual feature that each view must account for.

### Tension 6: Literal application and hyperbole

- **View 1 takes the Sermon's commands literally:** Turn the other cheek. Do not swear at all. Give to everyone who asks. Love your enemies --- actually, concretely, in practice.

- **All views acknowledge some hyperbole:** Jesus says "if thy right eye offend thee, pluck it out" (Matt 5:29). No tradition practices this literally. If this command is hyperbole, how does one determine which commands are hyperbolic and which are literal? The principle of distinction must come from somewhere, and views disagree about where.

- **What makes this genuinely difficult:** The Anabaptist response --- that context, genre, and common sense distinguish hyperbole from literal command --- is reasonable. But once the principle of non-literal reading is admitted for 5:29, critics ask whether it can be extended to 5:39 (turn the other cheek) or 5:34 (swear not at all). The line must be drawn somewhere, and where one draws it substantially shapes how one lives.

### Tension 7: The Constantinian question

- **Before Constantine (pre-313 AD):** The church read the Sermon's commands on non-violence, non-retaliation, and refusal of oaths literally. Christians were expelled from the military. This was not a minority position; it was the dominant reading, as Chapter 3 documented.

- **After Constantine:** The church-state alliance made literal application vastly more complicated. A Christianity responsible for governing an empire could not straightforwardly refuse military service or civic oaths. The development of "just war" theory, two-kingdoms theology, and the counsels-of-perfection tradition all emerged in this post-Constantinian context.

- **What makes this genuinely difficult:** Was the Constantinian shift a necessary maturation of Christian political thought, or was it a compromise that softened the Sermon's demands under pressure from political power? Both answers have been held by serious Christians. The reader must weigh whether the pre-Nicene reading reflects the apostolic ideal, or whether it reflects the luxury of political irrelevance.

## The Weight of Evidence

This study does not render a verdict. But it would be dishonest to pretend the evidence is evenly distributed. Several observations about the cumulative weight are appropriate.

**The patristic evidence is unusually one-sided.** Chapter 3 documented that from the Didache (c. 70--100 AD) through Augustine (c. 394 AD), not a single writer treats the Sermon as intended for a future dispensation, a past audience, or a merely theoretical ideal. Every pre-Nicene writer who addresses the Sermon applies it to believers in his own time. The Didache uses it for baptismal catechesis. Justin Martyr presents it to the Roman emperor as what Christians actually practice. Tertullian refuses military service on its basis. This unanimity across three centuries, multiple continents, and diverse theological temperaments is remarkable.

**The Great Commission creates a significant burden of proof for those who would exclude the Sermon.** "Teaching them to observe all things whatsoever I have commanded you" (Matt 28:20) is comprehensive. The Sermon on the Mount is the largest block of Jesus's commands in Matthew. To exclude it from "all things" requires an argument that the text itself does not provide. The burden falls on those who would limit the scope, not on those who read it at face value.

**The NT echoes are too extensive to be coincidental.** James, Paul, Peter, and John all independently apply Sermon-style ethics to church life --- without any suggestion that these teachings belong to a different dispensation. Romans 12 reads like a systematic application of the Sermon to Gentile believers. James 1--5 reads like pastoral commentary on the Sermon in a church setting. These are not faint parallels; they are sustained, detailed, and purposeful.

**The Lutheran insight about the Sermon's impossible demands is genuine, even if the Sermon's primary function is ethical instruction.** "Be ye therefore perfect" (Matt 5:48) is staggering. The honest reader does feel the gap between the Sermon's demands and lived experience. This feeling is not an error; it is the appropriate response to encountering the holiness of God in the teaching of his Son. But the question is whether that feeling is the *purpose* of the Sermon or a *side effect* of its real purpose, which is to call God's people to a radical love made possible by the Spirit.

**Progressive dispensationalists have moved significantly toward Views 1 and 2.** Within the dispensational tradition itself, the most rigorous contemporary scholars (Bock, Blaising, Saucy) have largely abandoned the "Sermon is not for the church" reading. This internal evolution is significant. When a tradition's own best scholars revise a position, the revision deserves weight.

**The classical dispensational reading of the Sermon (View 4) has no early church support.** The distinction between Israel's kingdom program and the church's grace program, as applied to the Sermon on the Mount, originates with John Nelson Darby (c. 1830) and was popularized by the Scofield Reference Bible (1909). No writer before the nineteenth century reads the Sermon this way --- and as late as the 1890s, *Bibliotheca Sacra*'s own treatments of the Sermon show no awareness of such a reading (Hall [1891] reads it as gospel-in-germ for the church; Berle [1897] as law-schoolmaster leading to Christ); the Jewish-audience restriction first appears in that journal's pages with Harris (1918), a decade after Scofield's Bible. Even the mainstream critique names the origin the same way: "It is this... fear that the promises of the Sermon on the Mount depend for their fulfilment on human merit that led J. N. Darby to relegate them to the future 'kingdom age'" (Stott, quoted in Wiley, *JODT* 23 [2019]: 8). This does not automatically make the reading wrong --- the Reformation recovered truths obscured for centuries --- but it does mean the reading lacks historical corroboration, and the reader should weigh this accordingly.

## A Note on Grace and Obedience

The dispensationalist's concern about law versus grace is not frivolous. It arises from a genuine love for the gospel and a desire to protect the sufficiency of Christ's finished work. The fear is real: if the Sermon on the Mount is "for today," does the church return to a law-keeping religion?

The answer, given clearly throughout the New Testament, is no. Obedience to the Sermon is not the means of justification. It is the fruit of justification. The sequence is grace first, then obedience --- never the reverse.

Paul makes this explicit:

> "For by grace are ye saved through faith; and that not of yourselves: it is the gift of God: Not of works, lest any man should boast. For we are his workmanship, created in Christ Jesus unto good works, which God hath before ordained that we should walk in them."
> --- Ephesians 2:8--10 (KJV)

Saved by grace. Created for good works. The works do not earn the grace; the grace produces the works. And the Sermon on the Mount describes what those works look like --- love, mercy, reconciliation, generosity, honesty, and trust in the Father's provision.

Jesus himself placed obedience within the framework of love, not law:

> "If ye love me, keep my commandments."
> --- John 14:15 (KJV)

The sequence matters. Love first; then obedience. Not: obey, and God will love you. But: because God has loved you, keep his commands. The Sermon is not a ladder to climb toward God's approval. It is the shape of life for those who have already been approved by grace.

Paul makes the same point in Romans, just chapters after his most sustained exposition of justification by faith alone:

> "I beseech you therefore, brethren, by the mercies of God, that ye present your bodies a living sacrifice, holy, acceptable unto God, which is your reasonable service."
> --- Romans 12:1 (KJV)

"By the mercies of God" --- that is, because of the grace already received in chapters 1--11 --- "present your bodies." The rest of Romans 12 is Sermon on the Mount ethics applied to the church: bless persecutors, overcome evil with good, live peaceably with all. Paul does not see this as a return to law. He sees it as the natural outworking of grace.

And the law of Christ is not the Mosaic law reimposed. It is the law of love, fulfilled by the Spirit:

> "For all the law is fulfilled in one word, even in this; Thou shalt love thy neighbour as thyself."
> --- Galatians 5:14 (KJV)

> "Bear ye one another's burdens, and so fulfil the law of Christ."
> --- Galatians 6:2 (KJV)

John confirms the same principle:

> "For this is the love of God, that we keep his commandments: and his commandments are not grievous."
> --- 1 John 5:3 (KJV)

"His commandments are not grievous" --- not because they are easy, but because the one who gives them also gives the love and the Spirit that make obedience possible. The Sermon on the Mount is the most sustained illustration of what this love looks like in daily life. To follow the Sermon is not to place oneself back under the Mosaic code. It is to love as Christ loved --- which is exactly what the gospel frees us to do.

## Questions for the Reader

These questions are not leading. They are designed to help you weigh the evidence and seek the Spirit's guidance. Take them slowly. Let the text speak for itself.

### From Scripture

1. When Jesus says "all things whatsoever I have commanded you" in Matthew 28:20, is there any textual basis for excluding the Sermon on the Mount from "all things"? If so, what is it?

2. Paul wrote "Bless them which persecute you: bless, and curse not" (Rom 12:14) to a Gentile church. James wrote "above all things, my brethren, swear not" (James 5:12) to scattered believers. Peter wrote "not rendering evil for evil" (1 Pet 3:9) to the churches of Asia Minor. If the Sermon is for Israel and a future kingdom, why do the apostles apply its ethics to the church without qualification?

3. What do the two temporal clauses in Matthew 5:18 mean for you? "Till heaven and earth pass... till all be fulfilled" --- does "all" refer to the cross, to the end of the world, or to something else? How does your answer shape your reading of the Sermon?

4. The word *teleios* in Matthew 5:48, read alongside Luke 6:36 ("Be ye therefore merciful"), appears to mean "completeness of love" rather than "sinless perfection." Does this change how you hear the command "Be ye therefore perfect"?

### From Christocentrism

5. Jesus lived every major demand of the Sermon --- non-retaliation, enemy-love, generosity, secret prayer, voluntary poverty. If the Sermon is not intended to be followed in the present age, why did Jesus model it as a way of life?

6. In John 14:15, Jesus says "If ye love me, keep my commandments." Does this verse place the Sermon's commands within the framework of love and relationship rather than law and obligation? If so, does that change how you approach the Sermon's demands?

7. The Great Commission extends to "all nations" and "unto the end of the world" (Matt 28:19--20). If the Sermon is part of what Jesus "commanded," does the Great Commission settle the applicability question? If not, what limits would you place on "all things"?

8. Jesus promised the Holy Spirit as the enabler of obedience (John 14:15--17, 26; 16:13). Paul confirmed that "the righteousness of the law might be fulfilled in us, who walk not after the flesh, but after the Spirit" (Rom 8:4). Does the Spirit's indwelling presence change what is achievable for the believer --- and does that bear on whether the Sermon can be followed today?

### From Antiquity

9. The Didache (c. 70--100 AD), likely written within a generation of the apostles, uses the Sermon's commands as baptismal instruction for all new converts. Does this tell us anything about how the earliest church understood the Sermon's audience?

10. No pre-Nicene writer --- not one --- treats the Sermon as intended for a future dispensation or as an impossible ideal designed to expose sin. The reading is unanimous from c. 70 to c. 325 AD. How much weight should this historical consensus carry in your own reading?

11. The pre-Nicene church practiced the Sermon's commands on non-violence and refusal of oaths at real cost --- expulsion from the military, refusal to swear in court, acceptance of persecution. Does the fact that they *practiced* these commands (not merely affirmed them in writing) affect how you assess their reading?

12. The Constantinian shift (c. 313 AD) changed the church's social position from persecuted minority to imperial religion. Did this shift illuminate aspects of the Sermon that the pre-Nicene church had missed, or did it introduce compromises that softened the Sermon's demands?

### From Reality

13. Anabaptist communities have practiced Sermon ethics --- non-resistance, refusal of oaths, mutual aid, enemy-love --- for five hundred years. The Amish forgiveness at Nickel Mines (2006) demonstrated Sermon obedience in a moment of devastating grief. Does the existence of communities that actually live the Sermon affect how you assess its feasibility?

14. If the Sermon is primarily an impossible ideal that drives to grace (View 3), how do you account for the communities that have, in fact, practiced it? Does their experience validate or challenge the "impossible" reading?

15. Every view agrees that the Christian life demands transformation. In practical terms, what does that transformation look like for you? Do the Sermon's specific commands --- turn the other cheek, love your enemies, do not worry, give to him that asketh --- shape your daily life? If not, is the obstacle theological or practical?

16. The classical dispensational reading of the Sermon (View 4) was first articulated by John Nelson Darby around 1830 and has no known precedent in the first eighteen centuries of church history. Does the novelty of a reading matter in your assessment? If the church read the Sermon one way for 1,800 years, what burden of proof does a new reading carry?

## Closing: The House on the Rock

The reader who has followed this study through five chapters, an appendix on Greek terms, and a collection of patristic quotations may feel overwhelmed by the volume of evidence. The temptation is to retreat into a comfortable theological position without engaging the full weight of the material. Resist that temptation. The evidence deserves to be weighed, not merely catalogued.

This study has tried to honor every position. The Anabaptist's radical obedience, the evangelical's Spirit-empowered kingdom ethic, the Lutheran's profound sense of human inability, and the dispensationalist's love for grace --- each reflects a genuine encounter with the Sermon's demands. The church is richer for the conversation between them. But a conversation that never reaches a conclusion is not a conversation; it is avoidance. The evidence points somewhere, and the reader must follow it.

But after all the exegesis, all the word studies, all the patristic citations, and all the careful analysis of competing views, the Sermon's own conclusion remains the simplest and most searching thing in the entire study:

> "Therefore whosoever heareth these sayings of mine, and doeth them, I will liken him unto a wise man, which built his house upon a rock: And the rain descended, and the floods came, and the winds blew, and beat upon that house; and it fell not: for it was founded upon a rock. And every one that heareth these sayings of mine, and doeth them not, shall be likened unto a foolish man, which built his house upon the sand: And the rain descended, and the floods came, and the winds blew, and beat upon that house; and it fell: and great was the fall of it."
> --- Matthew 7:24--27 (KJV)

Jesus did not say "whosoever understands these sayings" or "whosoever correctly classifies these sayings in a dispensational framework." He said "whosoever *heareth* these sayings of mine, and *doeth* them." The verb is *poieo* --- to do, to make, to perform. The Sermon ends where it began: with a call to action.

The reader is now equipped with the textual evidence, the historical witness, the major views at their strongest, and the key Greek terms that shape the debate. The Spirit of God --- the same Spirit Jesus promised would guide his people into all truth (John 16:13) --- is present to illumine the reading of Scripture. The evidence has been laid out as honestly as this study can manage, with every effort to present each view at its strongest and no effort to force a conclusion.

What remains is for each reader to do what the Sermon itself demands: hear, weigh, pray --- and then build. The question is not merely academic. It is the question of what kind of house you will construct with the years God has given you: a house on rock, or a house on sand.

Whatever you conclude, let it be a conclusion you can live by. The Sermon on the Mount was never meant to be an intellectual exercise. It was given by a Teacher who expected his students to do the homework. It was lived by a Savior who walked the path before asking anyone else to walk it. And it was sealed by a promise: "Lo, I am with you alway, even unto the end of the world" (Matt 28:20). You are not asked to obey the Sermon alone. You are asked to obey it in the company of the one who spoke it and the Spirit he sent.

May the one who taught the Sermon on the Mount --- who lived its every demand, who died with forgiveness on his lips, who rose to commission its teaching to all nations, and who promised his Spirit to every believer until the end of the age --- guide you as you answer.
