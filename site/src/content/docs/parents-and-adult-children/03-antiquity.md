## Overview

The pre-Nicene church (roughly AD 30 to 325) inherited a strong
household-piety culture from both Judaism and the Greco-Roman world,
but reshaped it around Christ. The apostolic and ante-Nicene writers
teach parents to raise children in the fear of God, teach children to
honor and obey their parents, and simultaneously honor consecrated
unmarried adults (both men and women) as a distinct Christian calling.
There is no single early-church template for the unmarried adult
child's household arrangement.

A note on how Antiquity functions in SCAR. Antiquity is not a
magisterium and does not overrule Scripture. It functions as a check
against novelty — a witness to what the church, reading the same
Scriptures in continuity with the apostles, has understood the
Scriptures to permit and forbid. This is the historic Protestant
posture stated by the Westminster Confession of Faith 1:6: "The whole
counsel of God, concerning all things necessary for his own glory,
man's salvation, faith and life, is either expressly set down in
Scripture, or by good and necessary consequence may be deduced from
Scripture." Antiquity's role in SCAR is to help the reader see what
the church has held to be "expressly set down" and what has been
faithfully "deduced by good and necessary consequence" — and to flag
when a modern claim departs from both.

## 3.1 The Context — Greco-Roman *Patria Potestas* and Jewish Household

Roman law recognized *patria potestas* — the father's legal power over
his descendants, including adult sons, potentially for life. Sons under
this power could not own property in their own name; the father's
death dissolved the power. Roman practice was more flexible than Roman
law: adult sons commonly conducted independent lives with paternal
consent. Daughters passed from *patria potestas* into the *manus* of
the husband at marriage (in older forms) or remained in the father's
household legally while living with the husband (in later forms).

First-century Judaism did not have a legal equivalent of *patria
potestas*. Sons ordinarily learned their father's trade and married
young (mid-to-late teens). Daughters ordinarily married very young by
modern standards. Betrothal was consequential; the father's role in
arranging a daughter's marriage was substantial.

The early Christian writers navigate both settings. They do not adopt
*patria potestas* as their model. Nor do they retreat from strong
household ethics.

### 3.1.1 The Qumran Community and the Dead Sea Scrolls

Independent Second Temple Jewish testimony survives in the Dead Sea
Scrolls. The Qumran sectarians provide a first-century witness that
neither the New Testament nor the Roman law codes provide directly:
how one strict Jewish community actually structured the transition
from paternal household to adult community life.

Three texts bear on the present question.

**The Rule of the Congregation (1QSa 1:6-11).** The scroll prescribes
the community's staged formation of male members:

> "From his youth they shall educate him in the Book of Meditation,
> and according to his age instruct him in the statutes of the
> covenant ... at twenty years of age he shall pass among those who
> are numbered, to enter the lot in the midst of his family and be
> joined to the holy congregation. He shall not approach a woman to
> know her by lying with her before he is fully twenty years old,
> when he knows good and evil." (1QSa 1:6-11; the paper's composite
> rendering from the Hebrew — no public-domain English exists; cf.
> Vermes, *The Complete Dead Sea Scrolls in English*, and García
> Martínez)

*Observations.* Independent Second Temple Jewish evidence for age
twenty as the threshold of adult status — precisely the biblical
military age (Num. 1:3). Marriage, likewise, is barred until that
threshold — on the construal of 1QSa 1:9-11 reflected in the
standard translations; what exactly attaches at twenty has been
discussed in the scrolls literature since Yadin, "A Crucial
Passage in the Dead Sea Scrolls," *JBL* 78 (1959): 238-41. The passage combines paternal formation ("from his youth
they shall educate him") with a community-mediated adult transition
("he shall pass among those who are numbered"). The son is passed
*from* paternal instruction *into* the congregation's structured
life — a Jewish sectarian analogue of the vocational household
transfer pattern.

**The Damascus Document (CD 7:6-7 and parallels).** The Damascus
Document treats those who "walk in the perfection of holiness ...
according to the covenant of God" — some celibate, some marrying
and begetting children in the camps. Both are recognized as
faithful.

**Josephus on the Essenes (*Jewish War* 2.8.2–2.8.13; *Antiquities*
18.1.5).** Josephus reports two orders: one wholly celibate, one
marrying "for the propagation of the race." The celibate order does
not beget children but *adopts other men's children* while still
young and teaches them:

> "They neglect wedlock, but choose out other persons' children,
> while they are pliable, and fit for learning; and esteem them to be
> of their kindred, and form them according to their own manners."
> (*War* 2.8.2)

*Observations.* Josephus's testimony independently corroborates the
scroll evidence: Second Temple Judaism outside the Pharisaic
mainstream already recognized (a) a legitimate celibate calling for
both men and women, and (b) transfer of children out of the paternal
household into another formative community for teaching. Neither is
a Christian innovation.

*Bearing on the present question.* The Qumran and Josephus material
provide first-century Jewish evidence — *before* the pre-Nicene
Christian writers — that:

- **Age twenty** functioned as the operative threshold for adult male
  responsibility, including marriage (1QSa 1:9-11). This aligns with
  the biblical military threshold (Num. 1:3).
- **The unmarried celibate life** was a publicly recognized honorable
  calling in Second Temple Judaism, both for men and women (Josephus
  on the Essenes). Christian celibacy did not arise in a Jewish
  vacuum.
- **Community-structured adult transitions** — formal enrollment
  and a community-set marriage threshold — have a
  Second Temple Jewish precedent. Ignatius's later requirement of
  episcopal approval for marriage (*To Polycarp* 5:2) is not
  without Jewish precursors in community-structured transition.
- **Formation of the young by a community other than the paternal
  household** is not a Christian invention; Josephus attests the
  Essene practice explicitly.

The Dead Sea Scrolls thus support both the *marriage-arrangement*
theme (community-mediated marriage) and the *vocational household
transfer* theme (formation in another sphere) — providing Second
Temple Jewish testimony to patterns that later appear in both
Christian antiquity and modern practice.

### 3.1.2 Rabbinic Judaism (Mishnah, Talmud, Traditional Halakhah)

Rabbinic Judaism is the tradition that has, over two millennia,
thought hardest about the parent-adult-child relationship on the basis
of the same Old Testament texts the present analysis engages. Though
the Mishnah (redacted c. 200 AD) and the Talmuds (Yerushalmi c. 400
AD; Bavli c. 500-600 AD) postdate the pre-Nicene sources of §3.2-3.10
below, they preserve halakhic reasoning whose seeds are demonstrably
Second Temple — visible already in Ben Sira, Philo, and Josephus, and
in the Qumran material of §3.1.1. Their inclusion here provides
independent, non-Christian corroboration of much of the pattern
Scripture establishes.

The rabbinic contribution to our question comes down to nine points.

**1. Honor (כִּבּוּד, *kibbud*) and Reverence (מוֹרָא, *mora*) as
distinguishable duties.** Kiddushin 31b splits the Fifth Commandment
into two lifelong obligations — precisely the move the New Testament
makes in Ephesians 6:1-2 with ὑπακούω / τιμάω. *Kibbud* is
positive material service ("to feed, to give drink, to clothe, to
cover, to bring in and out"). *Mora* is respectful posture ("not to
stand in his place, not to sit in his place, not to contradict him,
not to decide against him"). The two-duty framework is not a
Christian innovation — it is the rabbinic reading of the same Torah.

**2. Provision as concrete, non-optional duty.** Kiddushin 31b-32a
asks from whose funds the honor-costs are borne (מִשֶּׁל מִי,
*mishel mi*) — that is, when the
adult son must provide from his own funds versus the father's. The
ruling: provision from the father's own means where possible;
personal service from the son; and where the father cannot provide,
the son must (as later codified at *Shulchan Arukh Yoreh De'ah*
240:5) — the direct halakhic anchoring of the principle Jesus
presses against the *korban* loophole in Mark 7:9-13.

The tradition preserves a paradoxical pair of cases (the aphorism
at b. Kiddushin 31a-b, in the name of Avimi son of R. Abbahu; the
fuller wording and both stories in y. Peah 1:1, retold by Rashi ad
loc.): "There is one who feeds his father fatted
pheasants and inherits Gehenna, and there is one who puts his father
to grind at the mill and inherits the world to come." The stories
behind the paradox are the point. In the first, the son provided
luxury food but answered his father with contempt when the father
asked where it came from ("Old man, eat and be quiet, as dogs eat
and are quiet"). In the second, the king's officers had come to
conscript a member of the household for the royal mill-service —
dangerous, potentially fatal, corvée labor — and the son said to
the father, "You grind at the mill here in my place; I will go do
the king's work." He took the danger onto
himself and left the father the safer task. The rabbinic point is
*not* that a son should extract labor from his father, but that the
attitude and self-cost of provision matter as much as the substance:
a rich provision given with contempt dishonors, while a hard
provision given with respect and self-sacrifice honors.

**3. Honor at the top of the ranked commandments.** Mishnah Peah 1:1
— recited daily in the morning liturgy — lists the acts whose
"fruits a man enjoys in this world while the principal remains for
him in the world to come." Honor of father and mother heads the list,
alongside acts of loving-kindness and Torah study. Kiddushin 30b
compares the honor of parents to the honor of God, invoking the
parallel language of Exodus 20:12 and Proverbs 3:9.

**4. Age of majority does *not* trigger household exit.** Mishnah
Niddah 5:6-6:11 fixes religious majority at thirteen for boys and
twelve for girls. But majority in this sense is *religious
responsibility*, not household independence. Pirkei Avot 5:21 gives
the classical life-stages: five for Scripture, ten for Mishnah,
thirteen for the commandments (bar mitzvah), fifteen for Talmud,
**eighteen for marriage** (חוּפָּה, *chuppah*), **twenty for pursuit
of
livelihood**, thirty for strength, forty for understanding.[^analects]
The household transition is *marriage*, not majority — the rabbinic
embodiment of the Gen 2:24 pattern §1.3.1 sets out.

[^analects]: This schematic age-staged life-course has a striking
    generic parallel in the *Analects* of Confucius (2:4, c. 5th
    century BCE): "At fifteen I set my heart on learning; at thirty
    I stood firm; at forty I had no doubts; at fifty I knew the
    decrees of Heaven; at sixty my ear was attuned; at seventy I
    could follow what my heart desired without transgressing what
    was right." The genre — a named sage's decade-milestone
    progression, shared 15/30/40 markers, adulthood organized
    around fixed ritual and vocational transitions — is
    substantially the same. Direct literary dependence is
    implausible: no plausible channel runs from 5th-c. BCE China
    to 2nd-c. CE Palestine, and Yehuda ben Tema's saying draws on
    independent biblical and Second Temple antecedents. The far
    more probable explanation is independent development of a
    common genre in traditional agrarian societies that organized
    adult life around fixed stages. It is a striking
    cross-cultural parallel: two unrelated traditions
    independently framed the arc of adult maturity in decade
    milestones.

**5. Consent required — the daughter's marital autonomy from
majority.** Kiddushin 41a: "It is forbidden for a man to betroth his
daughter when she is a minor, until she grows up and says, 'I want
so-and-so.'" Rabbinic law positively forbids the father from
marrying off a maturing daughter without her consent. Ketubot 39a
and 46b-48a develop the transition through the *naarah* (12 to 12½,
a transitional six-month window) and *bogeret* (mature, from 12
years, 6 months, and 1 day onward — about 12½) stages: paternal
control of the daughter's earnings and marriage-arrangement ends at
*bogeret* (the threshold as at §1.4.1). **From maturity she is her
own agent for marriage** —
parental blessing is normal but not legally required. This is
distinctively mainstream rabbinic halakhah: Qumran (§3.1.1) by
contrast retained paternal authority over the unmarried daughter
indefinitely and had almost no framework for female legal
independence. The rabbinic picture is a much softer one than the
popular caricature of Jewish "arranged marriage" and closely
matches the Rebekah pattern of Genesis 24:57-58 (§1.4.5). It sits
in apparent tension with 1 Corinthians 7:36-38 (§1.4.2) — but only
under the "father-of-the-virgin" reading of that passage; under the
"betrothed-couple" reading the passage is not about paternal
authority at all. Even under the father-reading, Paul's language
concerns *permission* to marry an older unmarried daughter, not
compulsion against her will, and no specific age is in view. The
consent-of-the-daughter principle running from Genesis 24 through
mainstream rabbinic halakhah is not overturned by Paul on either
reading.

**6. The rebellious son as dead letter.** The rabbis piled restrictions
on Deuteronomy 21:18-21 until it became functionally inoperative
(Sanhedrin 68b-71a). The son must be between exactly thirteen and
thirteen-and-a-half years old (a three-month window); both parents
must be alive, agree, and (in some views) have similar voices and
appearances; specific quantities of meat and wine must have been
consumed in a specific setting.

Sanhedrin 71a issues the tradition's own verdict: **"It never was and
never will be" (לֹא הָיָה וְלֹא עָתִיד לִהְיוֹת, *lo hayah ve-lo
ʿatid lihyot*). Why then was it
written? "Interpret it and receive reward."** The rabbinic reading:
Deut 21 is *moral warning*, not legal procedure. Persistent
rebellion is grave, but no actual civil execution is contemplated.
The New Testament nowhere applies it. Neither did the rabbis.

Two further notes belong to this datum. *First*, the
dead-lettering itself is contested evidence: readers inclined
toward continuing paternal authority press that a tradition
declaring a Torah statute inoperative is not interpreting the
text but nullifying it — the "making the word of God of none
effect" objection, recorded with its rejoinders at §1.2.1 (the
fourth 1b reply) and §1.4.1. *Second*, the statute has a
canonical afterlife that neither the civil-procedure reading nor
the dead-letter reading exhausts. The prophets take up its exact
phrase — סוֹרֵר וּמוֹרֶה (*sorer u-moreh*, "stubborn and
rebellious") — and apply it to Israel nationally: "a stubborn
and rebellious generation" (Ps. 78:8), "this people hath a
revolting and a rebellious heart" (Jer. 5:23; cf. Isa. 30:1;
1:2). On the prophetic use, the rebellious son of Deut. 21
became the mirror in which the nation was to see itself, and the
judgment the statute prescribes fell at national scale in the
exile. The New Testament then completes the figure in a
direction commentators have long noted (e.g., Davies and
Allison, *Matthew*, ICC, vol. 2, on 11:19): the one figure in
the New Testament described by his opponents in the statute's
own charge-list vocabulary — "a man gluttonous, and a
winebibber" (Matt. 11:19; Luke 7:34; cf. "a glutton, and a
drunkard," Deut. 21:20; §2.4) — is
Jesus; and the verses immediately following the statute, the
curse on the man hanged on a tree (Deut. 21:22-23), are the
verses Paul applies to the crucified Christ (Gal. 3:13). Read
together — a typological reading available to every view in this
paper — the statute's canonical trajectory runs not to the city
gate but to the cross: the true Son, described by his accusers
as the rebellious son, bears the curse of the statute's own
context for the genuinely rebellious child, Israel and every
reader. Whether the rabbinic restriction of the civil procedure
was wisdom about the statute's real function or evasion of a
discipline whose absence the nation paid for — Eli's
unrestrained sons and the judgment that followed at Shiloh
(1 Sam. 2-4; §5.0) is the canonical case for the cost — the
paper records both assessments without adjudicating them.

**7. The father's obligations toward the son — including finding
him a wife.** Kiddushin 29a-b lists the father's duties: to
circumcise the son, redeem him if firstborn, teach him Torah, **find
him a wife**, **teach him a craft (*ummanut*)**, and (some add) teach
him to swim. Notice the marriage duty: *the father's obligation
includes actively facilitating the son's marriage*.

The rabbinic model does not eject the son at majority. It sets him
up in his own household around marriage-age, with the father
providing vocational grounding first and marital initiation second.
Sotah 44a, invoking Deuteronomy 20:5-7, gives the sequence: "Build a
house, plant a vineyard, then marry." This matches almost exactly the
sequence the NCFIC pastoral counsel (§4.1a) arrives at through
Reformed reasoning — two independent traditions reading the same
Torah toward the same practical order.

**8. Genesis 2:24 in rabbinic reading — always marriage-etiological,
never independence-authorizing.** Genesis Rabbah 17-18 treats the
verse as the definition of marital union. Sanhedrin 58a uses it to
define forbidden unions in Noahide law. The rabbis read the "leaving"
as making the marriage bond *primary over the birth family in
matters of the new couple's decisions and priorities* — a reading
that materially supports the conclusion of §1.3.1. **No rabbinic
source reads Genesis 2:24 as authorizing pre-marital independence.**
The individualist reading of the verse has no ancient warrant in the
tradition closest to the text.

**9. One real divergence — celibacy.** Rabbinic Judaism has essentially
no place for lifelong celibacy as an honorable calling. Yevamot 63b
records Ben Azzai defending his own unmarried state on the ground
that "my soul yearns for the Torah; the world can be maintained by
others" — but he is the exception, and even he acknowledges the
pressure of the norm. Yevamot 62b: "He who has no wife lives without
joy, without blessing, without goodness..." — the drift is uniformly
toward marriage.

This is a real divergence from the New Testament (1 Cor 7:7-8;
Matthew 19:12; Isaiah 56:3-7 as read in Acts 8:26-39 and by the
pre-Nicene church of §3.2-3.10). The Qumran material of §3.1.1 shows
that Second Temple Judaism was not uniform on this point; the
Essenes recognized celibate vocation. But post-70 rabbinic Judaism
consolidated around marriage-normativity in a way Christianity did
not. On celibacy the New Testament and early church open a space
(§1.6.2; §3.4-3.9) that post-70 rabbinic normativity closes.

*Bearing on the present question.* Rabbinic Judaism supplies an
independent Second-Temple-continuous witness to the meaning of shared
Old Testament texts. Independently, from the same Old Testament:

- **Honor is lifelong and substantive** — matching §1.2.2.
- **Provision is concrete duty** — matching the Mark 7 gloss.
- **Reverence is distinct from obedience** — matching the obey/honor
  distinction of §1.2.
- **Marriage is the household-transition, not majority-at-thirteen** —
  matching §1.3.1.
- **The daughter's consent is required from maturity** — matching the
  Rebekah pattern (§1.4.5).
- **Deut 21 is not a lever for authoritarian parenting** — matching
  §1.7.
- **The father sets the son up toward marriage rather than pushing
  him out** — matching the warrior-departure pattern (§1.4.8) and the
  vocational-then-marital sequence.

The single real divergence — celibacy — is precisely the point where
the New Testament, the pre-Nicene church, and the Qumran material of
§3.1.1 open a space the mainstream rabbinic tradition does not. On
the parent-adult-child dynamic itself, the several parallels noted
above hold across an independent Second-Temple-continuous tradition
reading the same Old Testament — evidence that the shared findings
above are not projections of modern categories back onto Scripture,
but readings that faithful hearers of the same text have arrived at
across radically different centuries and settings. Points where the
Chapter 4 views disagree are not adjudicated by rabbinic parallel;
the parallels bear only on points already common across the faithful
views.

## 3.2 The Didache (late 1st or early 2nd century)

The *Didache*, one of the earliest post-apostolic works, gives
household instructions in a section paralleling the "Two Ways":

> **Didache 4:9 (Lightfoot trans.)** — Thou shalt not withhold thy
> hand from thy son or from thy daughter, but from their youth thou
> shalt teach them the fear of God.

> **Didache 4:10-11 (Lightfoot trans.)** — Thou shalt not command
> thy bondservant or thine handmaid in thy bitterness, who trust in
> the same God as thyself, lest haply they should cease to fear the
> God who is over both of you; for He cometh, not to call men with
> respect of persons, but He cometh to those whom the Spirit hath
> prepared. But ye, servants, shall be subject unto your masters,
> as to a type of God, in shame and fear.

*Observations.* The Didache preserves what looks like a truncated
household code: parents-to-children (4:9) and masters-to-slaves
(4:10-11) are treated in the paired form familiar from Ephesians 5-6
and Colossians 3-4. But two elements of the New Testament
*Haustafeln* are conspicuously absent:

- No *child-to-parent* address is given. The Didache tells the
  parent to teach and not withdraw the hand, but does not repeat
  the Ephesians 6:1-3 / Colossians 3:20 command "children, obey
  your parents." A slave-to-master command is included at 4:11.
  The corresponding child-to-parent command is not.
- Nothing addresses adult unmarried offspring — no equivalent of
  1 Corinthians 7 or Numbers 30, no direction on marriage
  arrangement, no direction on adult children in the parental
  household.

*What "from their youth" means.* The Greek is ἀπὸ νεότητος (*apo
neotētos*) — the same construction Paul uses to Timothy in
1 Timothy 4:12 (where Timothy is likely in his 30s) and the rich
young ruler uses in Luke 18:21 ("from my youth up"). In patristic
Greek νεότης covers roughly the whole formative span from childhood
through young adulthood; it does not narrowly mean "small children."
Even so, the Didache's context is unambiguously *catechetical*
formation — the parallel construction in Barnabas 19:5 (§3.3), the
"Two Ways" instructional frame, and the phrase "teach them the fear
of God" all indicate training up the not-yet-formed, not governance
of the already-formed adult. Compare Proverbs 22:6.

Sons and daughters are addressed together and equally. Parents are
commanded not to *withdraw* their hand — implying that the
temptation the Didache guards against is under-discipline, not
over-control. The formation aim is the fear of God.

*What the Didache does not settle.* Because the text speaks only to
the formative period and drops the child-side of the code, it
supplies no direct evidence for either View 1 (continuing
household authority into adulthood) or View 2 (launched at
adulthood). The Didache's silence on adult unmarried offspring is
data, but it is data of the argument-from-silence kind: it cannot
carry weight either way on its own.

## 3.3 The Epistle of Barnabas (early 2nd century)

Barnabas repeats the Didache formula almost verbatim:

> **Barnabas 19:5 (Lightfoot trans.)** — Thou shalt love thy
> neighbour more than thine own soul. Thou shalt not murder a child
> by abortion, nor again shalt thou kill it when it is born. Thou
> shalt not withhold thy hand from thy son or daughter, but from
> their youth thou shalt teach them the fear of God.

*Observations.* The two texts together indicate a fixed early-church
catechetical pattern about *raising* children. Neither text addresses
what happens after the child becomes an adult.

## 3.4 Ignatius of Antioch and Polycarp of Smyrna (c. 107-155)

Ignatius, bishop of Antioch, and Polycarp, bishop of Smyrna, are
the two great post-apostolic witnesses — both of the generation
that stood within living memory of the apostles, both martyred.
Their assumption that marriage in the church proceeds by church
oversight (Ignatius, *To Polycarp* 5:2) carries the weight §4.5
places on it precisely because of who they were and how close they
stood to the apostolic generation.

> **Polycarp, To the Philippians 4:2 (Lightfoot trans.)** — ... our
> wives also, to walk in the faith that hath been given unto them
> and in love and purity, cherishing their own husbands in all
> truth and loving all men equally in all chastity, and to train
> their children in the training of the fear of God.

> **Ignatius, To Polycarp 5:1-2 (Lightfoot trans.)** — Tell my
> sisters to love the Lord and to be content with their husbands in
> flesh and in spirit. In like manner also charge my brothers in
> the name of Jesus Christ to love their wives, as the Lord loved
> the Church. If any one is able to abide in chastity to the honour
> of the flesh of the Lord, let him so abide without boasting. If
> he boast, he is lost.

*Observations.* Marriage and celibacy stand side by side as honorable
callings — with celibacy honored provided it is unboastful. The
household is the ordinary sphere; unmarried adults have a recognized
place.

Ignatius also treats marriage as an ecclesial event, not a private
one:

> **Ignatius, To Polycarp 5:2 (ANF trans.)** — But it becomes both
> men and women
> who marry, to form their union with the approval of the bishop,
> that their marriage may be according to God, and not after their
> own lust. Let all things be done to the honour of God.

This is early evidence that the marriage decision was not treated as
a private matter between the couple alone. Church oversight of the
marriage-arrangement moment is assumed by the beginning of the
second century.

## 3.4a Shepherd of Hermas (mid-2nd century)

> **Vision 1.3 (ANF trans.)** — But God is not angry with you on
> account of this, but that you may convert your house, which have
> committed iniquity against the Lord, and against you, their
> parents. And although you love your sons, yet did you not warn
> your house, but permitted them to be terribly corrupted. On this
> account is the Lord angry with you, but He will heal all the
> evils which have been done in your house... For as a smith
> hammers out his work, and accomplishes whatever he wishes, so
> shall righteous daily speech overcome all iniquity. Cease not
> therefore to admonish your sons; for I know that, if they will
> repent with all their heart, they will be enrolled in the Books
> of Life with the saints.

> **Vision 2.2-2.3 (ANF trans.)** — Your seed, O Hermas, has
> sinned against God, and they have blasphemed against the Lord,
> and in their great wickedness they have betrayed their parents.
> And they passed as traitors of their parents, and by their
> treachery did they not reap profit... But make known these words
> to all your children, and to your wife, who is to be your
> sister... But as for you, Hermas, remember not the wrongs done
> to you by your children... For they will be instructed with
> righteous instruction, if you remember not the wrongs they have
> done you. For the remembrance of wrongs works death. And you,
> Hermas, have endured great personal tribulations on account of
> the transgressions of your house, because you did not attend to
> them, but were careless and engaged in your wicked transactions.

*Observations.* The *Shepherd of Hermas* was the most widely-read
Christian text of the second century outside the New Testament and
was read publicly in some churches into the fourth (attested at
Athanasius, *Ep. Fest.* 39). The opening visions are directly on
the paper's territory: a father is held accountable by the Lord
for the sins of the grown members of his household. (That the
children are grown is an inference — the text gives no ages — but
their capacity to "betray their parents," which Osiek reads as
"renunciation, blasphemy, betrayal — all terms that could be
related to political persecution but need not be," possibly
apostasy under persecution driven by material interests (Carolyn
Osiek, *Shepherd of Hermas*, Hermeneia, Fortress, 1999, on Vis.
2.2, p. 80), presupposes agents acting in the public world.) What the
text states is a culpable omission and its remedy. The omission:
"you did not warn your house, but permitted them to be terribly
corrupted"; "you did not attend to them, but were careless" — the
verb of the failure in the Greek of Vis. 1.3 is νουθετέω
(*noutheteō*, admonish). The remedy: "convert your house," "make
known these words," "cease not to admonish your sons," daily
"righteous speech," and the refusal of grudge-keeping so that the
children "will be instructed with righteous instruction" — with
the children's restoration running through their own repentance
("if they will repent with all their heart"). Both wings of the
paper's question can claim this text, and each should be allowed
its reading. Readers of Views 1a/1b will hear a paterfamilias
answerable before God for the ongoing government of his house —
the Lord addresses the father, not the children; blames him for
permitting what he should have prevented; and expects him to
"convert" them — pre-Nicene evidence for the continuing-office
reading. Readers of Views 2a/2b will observe that every
instrument the text prescribes is a speech-instrument —
admonition, instruction, making-known, and the intercession and
repentance the visions enact — and that no directive command over
the grown children appears anywhere in the remedy; on this
hearing the office the text enforces operates in senses (2)-(4)
of §1.1.1. The text itself does not adjudicate between these
readings: on the 2a/2b hearing the directive-authority question
never arises in it; on the 1a/1b hearing the father's
answerability itself implies the office. What the text enforces on
either construal is the father's answerability and the word he
owes his house. A
minor companion witness in the same generation is *1 Clement*
21:6-8, addressed to the Corinthian church: "Let us reverence our
rulers; let us honour our elders; let us instruct our young men in
the lesson of the fear of God; let us guide our women toward that
which is good... Let your children be partakers of the discipline
that is in Christ."

## 3.5 Justin Martyr (mid-2nd century)

> **First Apology 15** — And many, both men and women, who have been
> Christ's disciples from childhood, remain pure at the age of sixty
> or seventy years; and I boast that I could produce such from every
> race of men.

*Observations.* Justin's public defense of the church cites the
existence of lifelong celibates — men and women — as evidence of
Christian virtue. He does not describe their household arrangements.
Some evidently lived at home; some in ecclesial community. The point
is that unmarried adulthood as a settled state was public and honored.

## 3.6 Athenagoras (late 2nd century)

> **Legatio 33** — You would find many among us, both men and women,
> growing old unmarried, in hope of living in closer communion with
> God.

*Observations.* Same pattern as Justin — the honored existence of
lifelong unmarried adults, both sexes, in the Christian communities.

## 3.6a Clement of Alexandria (c. 150-215)

Clement is the most household-focused pre-Nicene writer. His
*Paedagogus* (*The Instructor*) is a systematic treatment of the
Christian household under the tutelage of Christ; his *Stromata*
III is the most substantial pre-Nicene defense of marriage against
the encratite party that took bodily renunciation as constitutive
of the higher Christian life.

**Paedagogus III.11 (the paper's summary — not a quotation).** The
Christian household, from the master and mistress to the children
and servants, is drawn under a single Instructor; the household's
ordinary conduct — food, dress, speech, gesture, hospitality — is
the field on which Christ trains the whole family into virtue.
The chapter's own image is the Instructor as charioteer, "who,
pulling back the reins from far, leads and drives to salvation
the human horse — that is, the irrational part of the soul" (ANF
2, Wilson trans.); the whole of Books II-III applies that office
to the household's daily practice, item by item.

**Stromata III.12.79-90 (the paper's summary — not a quotation;
the ANF renders Book III only in Latin, a Victorian propriety
measure, so no public-domain English of the passage exists to
quote).** Marriage is a work of the Creator and is not for
Christians a lower state than celibacy. Those who reject marriage
as such reject the God who made it. Both marriage and celibacy
are honorable callings; celibacy is not superior in principle,
and marriage is not a concession to weakness. The apostles
married. The condemnation of the encratite refusal of marriage
rests on the goodness of creation and the apostolic pattern.
(English: Ferguson, FOC 85, or Chadwick's Library of Christian
Classics *Alexandrian Christianity*, 1954.)

*Observations.* Clement matters for the paper on two axes. First,
*Paedagogus* treats the Christian household as a single object of
formation under Christ — the language of *paideia* now
Christianized. This is straightforwardly the pre-Nicene picture of
the household-as-formative-community that Views 1a and 1b both
draw on, and Clement is unusual in the pre-Nicene corpus for
sustaining that picture at book length. Second, and more
consequentially for the paper's Non-Negotiable #4 (TL;DR, Non-Negotiables),
*Stromata* III supplies *pre-Nicene* support for the position that
marriage and celibacy are both honorable callings — the position
the paper elsewhere anchors in 1 Timothy 4:1-5 and in the later
canonical settlement. The paper's distinction from encratite
bodily-renunciation readings does not rest on 1 Timothy alone:
Clement shows that the pre-Nicene
church was already prepared to fight this fight and win it, in the
second and third centuries, on the ground of creation-goodness and
apostolic pattern. Non-Negotiable #4 is not a late-canon settlement
imposed on an earlier ambiguity; it is the pre-Nicene majority
position, articulated most fully by Clement, against a minority
encratite pressure that the church rejected. Editions: *Paedagogus*
and *Stromata* in ANF vol. 2 (Wilson, 1885); more recent critical
Greek text in Sources chrétiennes 70, 108, 158 (Mondésert, Marrou,
Matray, Van den Hoek); English of *Stromata* III with commentary in
John Ferguson, *Clement of Alexandria: Stromateis Books One to
Three*, FOC 85, CUA Press, 1991.

## 3.7 Tertullian (late 2nd to early 3rd century)

Tertullian addresses many household questions. Two are pertinent:

> **On the Veiling of Virgins 1 (ANF, Thelwall trans.)** — ... it
> behooves our virgins to be veiled from the time that they have
> passed the turning-point of their age: that this observance is
> exacted by truth, on which no one can impose prescription — no
> space of times, no influence of persons, no privilege of regions.

Tertullian's rule engages precisely at maturity: chapter 11 of the
same treatise denies that the veil is worn from infancy — it
begins "from the time when she begins to be self-conscious, and to
awake to the sense of her own nature." The treatise thus
presupposes adult virgins as a standing, publicly visible class in
the congregation, with the church's discipline for that class
engaging at the threshold of womanhood — not a fringe category.

**Ad Uxorem I.5-8 (the paper's summary — not a quotation).**
Discussion of the widow's freedom, including her freedom to
remarry or to remain single. Not directly on unmarried children
under paternal authority, but establishing that the state of
unmarried adulthood is publicly recognized.

*Observations.* Tertullian's writings are polemical and sometimes
extreme, but they consistently treat unmarried adult women as full
members of the church, not as permanent extensions of paternal
authority.

### 3.7.1 The Passio Perpetuae (c. 203)

Within a few years of Tertullian's writing, and in the same North
African church he served — some scholars have argued Tertullian himself
was the editor of the surviving text — the *Passio Sanctarum
Perpetuae et Felicitatis* records the martyrdom of Vibia Perpetua, a
twenty-two-year-old catechumen of noble birth, nursing an infant son,
whose pagan father made four documented attempts to force her
recantation. The *Passio* preserves what purports to be Perpetua's own
diary — one of the earliest first-person texts by a Christian woman —
and it turns on the exact question this paper puts to Antiquity: what
does honor of a father look like when the father commands the daughter
away from Christ?

The *Passio* records three distinct encounters between Perpetua and
her father, in ascending intensity across the arc of her imprisonment
and trial. In the first (ch. 3), her father comes to the prison
seeking to argue her out of her confession; it is here that Perpetua
offers the vessel-and-its-name dialogue, and here that her father —
enraged — physically threatens her:

> **Passio Perpetuae 3** — "Father," said I, "do you see this vessel
> lying, a pitcher or whatsoever it may be? ... Can it be called by
> any other name than what it is?" And he said, "No." "So neither can
> I call myself anything other than what I am, a Christian." Then my
> father, provoked by this word, threw himself upon me as though he
> would pluck out my eyes; but he only vexed me, and he departed
> vanquished, he and the arguments of the devil.
>
> (English rendering after W. H. Shewring, *The Passion of SS. Perpetua
> and Felicity, MM.,* Sheed & Ward, 1931; Latin/Greek critical text in
> Thomas J. Heffernan, *The Passion of Perpetua and Felicity,* Oxford,
> 2012.)

In the second encounter (ch. 5), the father returns, this time not
to argue but to plead. He kisses her hands, throws himself at her
feet, invokes his grey hairs, and cries out on behalf of her mother,
her aunt, her brothers, and her nursing son. In the third (ch. 6),
at the tribunal itself, the father appears carrying the infant and
begs her before the procurator to have pity on her child; when he
interrupts the proceedings, the procurator has him beaten with a rod
and driven off. Perpetua's grief is explicit — "I was grieved for
his grey hairs, that he alone of all my kindred would not rejoice at
my passion" — and she does not deny her father's fatherhood, curse
him, or renounce him. What she denies is that his directive authority
reaches to the confession of Christ. She then goes willingly to the
arena and dies.

Two features are decisive for the paper's purposes. The first is what
the pre-Nicene church chose to *do* with this account. The *Passio*
was read liturgically on Perpetua's feast day, was cited by Augustine
in three surviving sermons (Sermons 280, 281, and 282, with the
augmented recension of 282 sometimes counted as a fourth), and was
preserved and copied through the mediaeval period as one of the
church's canonical martyr narratives.
The church did not read Perpetua's refusal as a defect in filial piety
to be regretted or explained away; it read it as exemplary Christian
constancy under paternal pressure, precisely the case Chapter 1's
mixed-household treatment (§1.4.10) generalizes from the New Testament
witness.

The second feature is what the account does *not* claim. Perpetua does
not renounce her father, does not curse him, and does not deny that he
retains the offices of teaching, counsel, and intercession that
§1.1.1 names as senses (2), (3), and (4) of authority. She refuses him
on one point: the point on which his command runs directly against the
command of Christ. Sense (1) — directive authority in the specific act
of confession — is defeated by the higher command; the other senses
of authority are not addressed by the narrative one way or the other.
This is the same shape the paper's exegesis of Acts 4:19 / 5:29 and
Matthew 10:34-36 already found in the apostolic period. Perpetua is
the pre-Nicene church's most vivid narrative embodiment of it, and
Tertullian's contemporary or near-contemporary hand in transmitting it
places it inside the same North African stream that produced the
*Ad Uxorem* passages cited above.

It is worth being precise about how the mixed-household case maps to
the four senses of authority named in §1.1.1. Perpetua's father is a
pagan; by definition he cannot exercise sense (2) *formative
authority* in Christian catechesis (he is trying to *undo* it) and
he cannot exercise sense (4) *priestly authority* as intercession to
Christ (he does not confess Christ). Those two senses are not so
much *defeated* in his case as they are spiritually *moot* from his
side — they never had subject-matter to operate on. Sense (1)
*directive authority* is defeated at the exact point where his
command runs against Christ's command (the confession). What remains
active for Perpetua toward her father is the fifth-commandment duty
of *honor* — respect for his person, grief at his grief, and the
kind of deference that continues to recognize him as father even
while refusing his one demand — and the operation of sense (4) in
the reverse direction: the believing child interceding for the
unbelieving parent, which is the shape §1.4.10 draws from the
Timothy pattern and 1 Corinthians 7:12-16. Perpetua's grief at her
father's grey hairs is not sentiment against the confession; it is
the fifth commandment operating exactly where sense (1) has been
overruled.

*Observations.* The *Passio Perpetuae* does not settle the question
between the four views on the ordinary case of a believing daughter in
a believing household. It is silent on that question. What it settles
— and what the pre-Nicene church took it as settling — is the limit
case: when paternal command runs against Christ's command, the adult
daughter's refusal is not disobedience to the fifth commandment but
obedience to the higher command that Christ himself named in Matthew
10. All four views recognize this limit. The *Passio* is the
narrative on which the pre-Nicene consensus about that limit rests.

### 3.7.2 The Acts of Paul and Thecla — a Popular Witness the Church Itself Policed

One more second-century narrative belongs in the inventory
precisely because of how the church handled it. The apocryphal
*Acts of Paul and Thecla* (2nd c., Asia Minor) narrates a virgin
of Iconium, betrothed to Thamyris, who on hearing Paul's preaching
of continence refuses the marriage. Her own mother Theocleia
demands of the governor, "Burn the wicked wretch; burn in the
midst of the theatre her that will not marry, in order that all
the women ... may be afraid"; Thecla, delivered, resolves to
follow Paul — "I shall cut my hair, and follow you wherever you
may go" (*Acts of Paul and Thecla*, ANF vol. 8). The narrative
thus celebrates an adult daughter's refusal of both betrothal and
maternal authority for consecrated celibacy, and it circulated
widely enough that Tertullian had to address appeals to it within
a generation.

Its evidential standing is exactly what the paper's method
requires be stated. The work is apocryphal, colored by the
encratite tendency the church resisted (§3.6a), and the church's
own discipline policed appeals to it: Tertullian records that "the
presbyter who composed that writing ... after being convicted, and
confessing that he had done it from love of Paul, was removed from
his office" (*De baptismo* 17, rejecting appeals to Thecla's
example as warrant for women teaching and baptizing). What the
text and its reception attest is therefore not a normative answer
but the live pre-Nicene *negotiation* of the very tension this
chapter maps: a popular narrative celebrated the daughter's
refusal, and the church both honored the consecrated calling
(§3.5-3.8) and refused to let the popular narrative function as
doctrine. Readers of Views 1a/1b will note that the church's check
ran against the celebrated autonomy-narrative; readers of Views
2a/2b will note that the check concerned the work's authorship and
its use for women's church office, and that the church nowhere
reasserted the mother's or the betrothed's authority as the
counter-principle. The datum cuts both ways, which is why it
belongs in the inventory.

## 3.7a Origen (c. 184-253, via Eusebius *Historia Ecclesiastica* 6.2)

Origen's biography preserved by Eusebius supplies two pre-Nicene
data points that fall directly on the paper's seams. Both are in
*HE* 6.2, in Eusebius's narrative of Origen's youth.

**Eusebius, *HE* 6.2.3-6 (the paper's summary, with quotation from
the NPNF² trans.).** When Origen's father Leonides was imprisoned
under Septimius Severus, Origen — then not quite seventeen —
burned with such zeal for martyrdom that he attempted to leave
the house and follow his father. His mother, unable to
restrain him with words, hid all his clothing so that he could
not go out. Being thus prevented, and driven by his zeal, he
wrote to his father in prison, urging him not to flinch on his
family's account: "Take heed not to change your mind on our
account."

**Eusebius, *HE* 6.2.12-15 and 6.3.3 (the paper's summary).**
After his father's martyrdom and the confiscation of the estate
by the imperial treasury, Origen — not quite seventeen —
supported his widowed mother and six younger brothers through
teaching, first as a grammarian and then, in his eighteenth year,
as head of the
catechetical school of Alexandria.

*Observations.* Both incidents sit exactly on the paper's material.
*First:* the mother's clothes-hiding is a case of parental restraint
exercised at the very Christ-allegiance boundary the paper has
worked (§1.4.10, §3.7.1). The mother is not commanding him to deny
Christ — she is preventing an adolescent's zeal for martyrdom from
carrying him past what he was ready to bear, at a moment when his
family also needed his life. Eusebius reports the incident with
approval, and Origen's own subsequent life bears out the mother's
judgment. This qualifies the "no restraint at the confession seam"
picture with a specific pastoral exception: a parent may act to
*preserve life* even where the child is prepared to lose it,
without violating the confession seam itself (Leonides is the one
called to die; Origen's later confession comes in a different form,
as teacher and, eventually, under Decius, as confessor). *Second:*
the seventeen-year-old provider case is a pre-Nicene instance of
the reversed provision the paper treats at §1.4.4 — the adult child
requiting the parent. Origen's provision for his widowed mother and
six brothers, taken up when the paternal estate was confiscated, is
1 Tim. 5:4 lived as biography. Both incidents are settled parts of
the pre-Nicene record. Text: Eusebius, *Historia Ecclesiastica*
6.2, in Kirsopp Lake, LCL 265 (Harvard, 1932) or NPNF² vol. 1
(Schaff, 1890).

## 3.8 Cyprian (mid-3rd century)

Cyprian's *De Habitu Virginum* (c. 249) is a bishop's disciplinary
treatise regulating the dress and conduct of the consecrated
virgins of his church.

> **De Habitu Virginum 3 (ANF, Wallis trans.)** — My address is now
> to virgins, whose glory, as it is more eminent, excites the
> greater interest. This is the flower of the ecclesiastical seed,
> the grace and ornament of spiritual endowment ... the more
> illustrious portion of Christ's flock.

> **De Habitu Virginum 22 (ANF, Wallis trans.)** — That which we
> shall be, you have already begun to be. You possess already in
> this world the glory of the resurrection ... in that you continue
> chaste and virgins, you are equal to the angels of God.

*Observations.* What the treatise evidences is carried less by any
single sentence than by its form: a bishop addresses the
consecrated virgins of his flock directly, praises their calling,
and issues sustained regulation of their conduct — and Cyprian's
*Epistle 4* (the *virgines subintroductae* case, §1.9) shows the
same episcopal discipline operating with sanctions. By the
mid-3rd century, then, consecrated virgins were a distinct order
under the church's direct pastoral care. The treatise says nothing
about fathers, in either direction; the inference that such
virgins stood under episcopal care *rather than exclusively under
paternal authority* is the paper's inference from the jurisdiction
the treatise exercises, not a statement Cyprian makes.

## 3.9 Methodius (late 3rd to early 4th century)

Methodius's *Symposium* (a dialogue among ten virgins celebrating
virginity) portrays consecrated women living in community, discoursing
together, and pursuing a shared spiritual life. Whatever else it
teaches, it shows that by the end of the pre-Nicene period, at least
some unmarried adult women lived in ecclesial community rather than
in their fathers' households.

## 3.10 Patterns Across the Pre-Nicene Church

Assembling the sources:

- **Formation of children** — sons and daughters, in the fear of the
  Lord, from youth (Didache, Barnabas, Polycarp).
- **Marriage** — normal, honored, treated as the ordinary path.
- **Consecrated unmarried adulthood** — honored, public, both sexes
  (Ignatius, Justin, Athenagoras, Tertullian, Cyprian, Methodius).
- **Household arrangements for unmarried adults** — variable. Some
  lived at home; some in ecclesial community; the church exercised
  pastoral care in either case.
- **Continuing paternal authority over unmarried adult daughters** —
  not codified as a doctrine. The New Testament texts (Num. 30 by
  reflection; 1 Cor. 7 by direct citation) are used by later Fathers
  in exhortations to obedience and reverence, but no ante-Nicene
  writer builds a theological framework of continuing legal authority
  parallel to *patria potestas*.

*The View 1b answer to the absence-finding,* recorded here as the
other chapters record each view's answer to the data that press on
it: in the marriage-economy of the pre-Nicene world, where
daughters ordinarily passed to a husband's house at or near
maturity (§1.4.1), the window of unmarried adult daughterhood was
short for most households, so a doctrine governing that case had
little occasion to be codified. On this reply, the silence is weak
evidence about a question that rarely presented itself, and the
authority 1b finds already established in Scripture (Num. 30 and
the Haustafel, on its reading) did not need a patristic framework
to survive the period. Whether that reply carries the weight is
for the reader; the data of §3.2-3.9 stand either way.

## 3.11 What Antiquity Requires, Permits, and Forbids

The early church:

- **Requires** — parental teaching of children in the fear of God;
  filial honor at every age; provision for parents; marital purity;
  and, for those consecrated to virginity, sincerity without boasting.
- **Permits** — marriage as ordinary; unmarried adulthood as
  consecrated calling for both men and women; unmarried adults either
  in the paternal household or in ecclesial community.
- **Forbids** — abandonment of parents; boastful celibacy; and
  (increasingly, by the 3rd century) private marriages arranged
  without pastoral involvement.

## 3.12 What Antiquity Leaves Open

- Whether the unmarried adult daughter's continuing residence at home
  entails continuing paternal *authority* or continuing paternal
  *care*.
- Whether the unmarried adult son and daughter are treated symmetrically
  in the household. The evidence points to some asymmetry (daughters
  are addressed as veiled virgins in the assembly; sons more often
  as itinerant apprentices, catechumens, or clergy) but no systematic
  doctrine.
