## What Has Come Before

This study has examined the Angel of the LORD through four distinct lenses, each corresponding to a pillar of the SCAR Quadrilateral:

- **Scripture** (Chapter 1): The Hebrew Bible presents a figure who speaks as YHWH, receives worship, and exercises divine prerogatives --- yet is simultaneously sent by YHWH and distinguished from him, creating a paradox the text does not resolve.
- **Christocentrism** (Chapter 2): The NT identifies Christ with OT theophanic events (1 Cor 10:4, John 12:41, Acts 7:30--38), while Hebrews 1--2 argues Christ is categorically superior to all angels.
- **Antiquity** (Chapter 3): The pre-Nicene fathers unanimously identified the Angel with the pre-incarnate Son; Augustine reversed this after the Arian crisis; Jewish sources (Philo, Targums, Talmud) independently developed parallel "second power" theology.
- **Reality** (Chapter 4): Four major views --- Christophany, Created Angel, Theophany of the Father, Literary Device --- each presented at full strength with arguments, pastoral fruit, and honest challenges.

The work of synthesis is not to declare a winner. It is to identify what everyone agrees on, name the genuine tensions, apply the SCAR priority framework honestly, and equip the reader to weigh the evidence before God.

## Areas of Agreement

Theological debates often obscure how much common ground exists. On the question of the Angel of the LORD, the degree of agreement across all four views is striking --- and worth stating explicitly, because these shared convictions are more important than the disagreements that follow.

### 1. The Angel of the LORD Is Not an Ordinary Angel

No serious interpreter of any persuasion treats this figure as just another angelic messenger. The Created Angel view places him in a class entirely apart from the seraphim, cherubim, or any of the *mal'akhim* who populate the heavenly court. The Christophany and Theophany views hold the figure to be divine. Even the Literary Device view acknowledges that the texts portray encounters of extraordinary and unique character. Whatever the Angel of the LORD is, he is not ordinary.

### 2. The Passages Depict Genuine Encounters with God

All four views agree that when Hagar wept in the wilderness, when Moses stood before the bush, when Gideon trembled at the winepress --- these were real encounters with the living God. The Christophany view says they met the Son. The Theophany view says they met the Father. The Created Angel view says they met God through his supreme representative. The Literary Device view says the narratives communicate genuine divine revelation, even if the "angel" figure is a narrative convention. But none of them say this was imagination or myth. The God of Israel drew near, and the Angel of the LORD passages are the record of that nearness.

### 3. "My Name Is in Him" Signals Extraordinary Authority

The declaration of Exodus 23:21 --- that YHWH's *shem* (name) resides in the Angel --- is universally recognized as marking this figure with authority beyond anything granted to any other messenger in the Hebrew Bible. The name of God in biblical theology is not a label; it is the presence, character, and authority of God himself. The Christophany view reads this as ontological identity. The Created Angel view reads it as delegated authority at its absolute apex. The Theophany view reads it as the Father manifesting through a visible form. But all agree: no other messenger in Scripture is described this way.

### 4. The Angel Exercises Prerogatives Elsewhere Reserved for God

Forgiveness of sins. Redemption of the afflicted. Receiving worship without rebuke. Swearing oaths in the first person as YHWH. Promising descendants and land. Calling the ground holy. These are actions that, everywhere else in the Hebrew Bible, belong to God alone. The Angel performs them. The views disagree on *why* --- divine nature, divine manifestation, supreme agency, or narrative convention --- but the data itself is not disputed by anyone.

### 5. These Texts Reveal God's Intimate, Personal Care for His People

Hagar, abandoned and pregnant. Jacob, wrestling alone in the dark. Moses, tending sheep in obscurity. Gideon, threshing wheat in hiding. Manoah's wife, barren and without hope. In every case, the Angel comes to the forgotten, the overlooked, the afraid --- not with distant sovereign pronouncements but with personal attention, calling people by name, meeting them precisely where they are. All four views celebrate this pastoral reality.

### 6. The Identification-Distinction Paradox Is Real

*The problem is in the text itself.* The same passages that identify the Angel as YHWH also distinguish him from YHWH. He speaks as God in the first person; he also speaks of God in the third person. He is called YHWH; he is also sent by YHWH. No view resolves this cleanly. Trinitarian theology accounts for it --- but was not articulated until centuries later. The agency principle explains much --- but strains when the Angel is called *go'el* and placed in parallel with God Almighty. The literary convention reading defers the question rather than answering it. The paradox belongs to the text, not to any one reading of it.

### 7. The Old Testament Presents Something More Complex Than Simple Monotheism Allows

Strict unitary monotheism --- one person, full stop --- has difficulty accounting for the Angel of the LORD passages. Even the Theophany view must explain why God manifests in a form that is "sent" and textually distinguished from the sender. The rabbinic insistence that there are not "two powers in heaven" (b. Sanhedrin 38b) is itself evidence that the texts were generating exactly this inference. The Angel passages do not teach the Trinity. But they create a textual space in which something like the Trinity becomes thinkable.

### 8. The Christophany and Theophany Views Agree: The Figure Is Truly Divine

The two largest Christian traditions on this question --- the Christophany view (dominant in Protestantism) and the Theophany view (significant in Eastern Orthodoxy and Augustinian Catholicism) --- agree that the Angel of the LORD is not a creature. He is God. They disagree on *which* divine person appears, or whether the question of persons is even the right framework. But on the fundamental question --- created being or uncreated God? --- the two most historically significant Christian readings give the same answer.

### 9. Even the Created Angel View Acknowledges the Figure's Unique Status

The strongest versions of the Created Angel view do not minimize the Angel. They argue that God appointed a supreme representative who operates at a level no other created being approaches --- bearing the divine name, exercising delegated divine authority, serving as the visible face of the invisible God. The distance between "the highest possible created agent of God" and "God himself in visible form" is real and important --- but it is not the distance between reverence and dismissal.

## Areas of Tension

If the areas of agreement are surprisingly broad, the areas of tension are genuinely unresolved. These are not problems for one side that the other side has neatly solved. They are live questions on which serious, careful, godly interpreters reach different conclusions --- and on which the evidence, honestly weighed, does not compel a single answer.

### 1. The Hebrews Question

Does Hebrews 1--2 rule out identifying Christ as "the Angel"?

The argument *for* is straightforward and powerful. The author of Hebrews opens his letter with a sustained demonstration that the Son is superior to angels:

> "Being made so much better than the angels, as he hath by inheritance obtained a more excellent name than they. For unto which of the angels said he at any time, Thou art my Son, this day have I begotten thee? And again, I will be to him a Father, and he shall be to me a Son?"
> --- Hebrews 1:4--5 (KJV)

> "And again, when he bringeth in the firstbegotten into the world, he saith, And let all the angels of God worship him. And of the angels he saith, Who maketh his angels spirits, and his ministers a flame of fire. But unto the Son he saith, Thy throne, O God, is for ever and ever: a sceptre of righteousness is the sceptre of thy kingdom."
> --- Hebrews 1:6--8 (KJV)

The logic seems clear: Christ is categorically above angels. To call him "the Angel" is to place him in a category the author of Hebrews has just labored to distinguish him from. If Christ is not an angel, then the Angel of the LORD is not Christ.

The argument *against* is equally serious. The word *mal'akh* means "messenger" --- it denotes a function, not an ontological category. Hebrews is arguing that the Son is not a *created* angel --- not that he cannot serve in the role of divine messenger. The Son is called "Apostle" (Hebrews 3:1), which means "one who is sent" --- a category that includes human beings. If the Son can be an apostle (a sent one) without being a mere human, he can be a *mal'akh* (a messenger) without being a mere angel. The pre-Nicene fathers held both truths simultaneously: the Son is fully divine *and* he served as the Father's visible messenger in the Old Testament. They did not see Hebrews as contradicting this.

The tension cannot be dissolved by either side. Those who invoke Hebrews must explain why *mal'akh* is categorically different from "Apostle" or "Servant" --- both of which Hebrews applies to Christ. Those who identify Christ as the Angel must explain why the inspired author drew such a sharp line between the Son and angels if the Son had functioned under that title for centuries.

### 2. The Pre-Nicene Consensus vs. the Post-Nicene Correction

Was the early church right, or were they corrected?

The historical facts are not in dispute. From Justin Martyr through Eusebius, the identification of the Angel with the pre-incarnate Son was the unanimous Christian reading. Justin called the Angel a "second God" --- distinct from the Father "in number, not in will." No pre-Nicene father is known to have dissented.

Augustine broke with this consensus in *De Trinitate*, arguing that any person of the Trinity could have appeared in the OT theophanies. His position became the dominant Western reading and remains influential today.

The question is *why*. Arius had argued that the Son was a creature --- and used the Angel identification as evidence. If the Son appeared as a *mal'akh*, Arius reasoned, he must be a created messenger. The pre-Nicene fathers would have rejected this inference (Justin held the Angel to be fully divine), but Augustine apparently concluded that the safest course was to sever the identification altogether.

Was Augustine correcting a genuine exegetical error that three centuries of fathers had missed? Or was the Arian crisis forcing a strategic retreat that abandoned a sound reading because heresy had made it dangerous? If the pre-Nicene fathers --- some within living memory of the apostles --- unanimously reached the same conclusion, that consensus carries serious evidential weight. If, on the other hand, the early fathers were working with less developed trinitarian categories, then Augustine's correction is the mature reading built on the foundation they laid. Both positions are defensible. Neither is obviously wrong.

### 3. Reading Forward vs. Reading Backward

Does the New Testament tell us who the Angel *is*, or does it reinterpret the Old Testament in ways the original authors did not intend?

The New Testament clearly connects Christ to Old Testament events. Paul writes:

> "And did all drink the same spiritual drink: for they drank of that spiritual Rock that followed them: and that Rock was Christ."
> --- 1 Corinthians 10:4 (KJV)

John, citing Isaiah's vision of the LORD in the temple, declares:

> "These things said Esaias, when he saw his glory, and spake of him."
> --- John 12:41 (KJV)

Stephen, recounting the exodus, describes the figure at the burning bush:

> "And when forty years were expired, there appeared to him in the wilderness of mount Sina an angel of the Lord in a flame of fire in a bush."
> --- Acts 7:30 (KJV)

And Jude, in a textual variant with serious manuscript support, writes:

> "I will therefore put you in remembrance, though ye once knew this, how that the Lord, having saved the people out of the land of Egypt, afterward destroyed them that believed not."
> --- Jude 5 (KJV)

Some manuscripts read "Jesus" where the KJV reads "the Lord" --- a variant attested in major codices and considered by many textual critics to be the harder (and therefore possibly original) reading.

These passages constitute a strong case that the apostolic church understood Christ to have been personally present in Old Testament events. But the hermeneutical question remains: when Paul says "that Rock *was* Christ," does he mean Christ was literally present, or is he making a typological identification --- the Rock *pointed to* Christ, *prefigured* Christ?

If the New Testament is making *ontological* claims about Christ's Old Testament presence, the Christophany identification receives strong apostolic support. If it is making *typological* claims, the identification is less direct. Both readings have been held by serious exegetes throughout Christian history. The NT texts do not include a footnote clarifying which hermeneutic they employ.

### 4. The Agency Principle

Does the Jewish *shaliah* doctrine adequately explain the Angel of the LORD data?

The agency principle is elegant and well-attested. In Jewish law, "a man's agent is as himself" (*shaliah shel adam kemoto*). An authorized representative speaks in the first person as the sender, acts with the sender's full authority, and can be addressed as the sender. Applied to the Angel of the LORD, this explains why the Angel speaks as God, is called God, and exercises God's authority --- without requiring any trinitarian theology, and in terms the original Israelite audience could have understood.

But the principle also has limits. Consider Jacob's deathbed blessing:

> "The Angel which redeemed me from all evil, bless the lads; and let my name be named on them, and the name of my fathers Abraham and Isaac; and let them grow into a multitude in the midst of the earth."
> --- Genesis 48:16 (KJV)

Jacob places "the Angel" (*ha-mal'akh*) in direct grammatical and theological parallel with "God, before whom my fathers Abraham and Isaac did walk" and "the God which fed me all my life long unto this day" (Genesis 48:15). The Angel is not merely authorized to speak for God; the Angel *is* Jacob's redeemer --- his *go'el*. In the Hebrew Bible, the *go'el* is a kinsman-redeemer, a deeply personal title. Jacob does not say the Angel *delivered a message about* redemption. He says the Angel *redeemed him*.

Consider Hosea:

> "Yea, he had power over the angel, and prevailed: he wept, and made supplication unto him: he found him in Bethel, and there he spake with us; Even the LORD God of hosts; the LORD is his memorial."
> --- Hosea 12:4--5 (KJV)

Hosea identifies the angel with whom Jacob wrestled as "the LORD God of hosts" --- not as one who *represented* the LORD God of hosts, but as the LORD himself. The agency principle can stretch far, but at some point the language of agency gives way to the language of identity. Reasonable interpreters disagree about where that point is.

### 5. The Arian Shadow

Does calling Christ "the Angel" diminish him?

This tension is not merely academic. It shaped the trajectory of Christian theology for seventeen centuries. The concern is pastoral and doctrinal: if the Son is called "the Angel," does this open the door to the claim that he is a creature?

The pre-Nicene fathers did not think so. Justin called the Angel both "God" and "Angel" in the same breath, insisting the Angel was a "second God" who shared the Father's will and nature while being personally distinct. For them, *mal'akh* described a role, not an ontological category. A king who serves as his own ambassador does not cease to be king by delivering his own message.

But Arius weaponized the title. If the Son functioned as a *mal'akh*, Arius argued, he belongs to the category of created messengers. Nicaea (325) and Constantinople (381) rejected Arianism, but the aftermath left the Angel identification under suspicion. Augustine's move to sever it can be read as prudential: even if exegetically sound, the title had become a weapon in the wrong hands.

The question is whether the danger lies in the title itself or in what heresy does with it. Every christological title can be misused. "Son of God" has been twisted to imply biological generation. "Firstborn" has been twisted to imply creation. "Servant" has been twisted to imply inferiority. In each case, the church's response was not to abandon the title but to define it more carefully. Was the Angel-of-the-LORD title the one exception --- too dangerous to keep? Or was Augustine's retreat an overreaction that cost the church a rich christological tradition?

## The SCAR Priority Resolution

The SCAR Quadrilateral has an internal priority structure: Scripture first, then Christocentrism, then Antiquity, then Reality. When the pillars converge, the conclusion is strong. When they diverge, the priority order provides guidance --- not a verdict, but a framework for responsible reasoning.

### Scripture

The Old Testament data is genuinely ambiguous. The identification-distinction paradox exists in the text itself and does not resolve in any single direction without the aid of interpretive frameworks external to the passages themselves. A reader approaching Genesis 16, Exodus 3, Judges 6, or Judges 13 with no prior theological commitments would be struck by the strangeness of the figure --- the way he speaks as God, is called God, yet is also "sent" and "distinguished" --- but would not be compelled by the text alone to choose one of the four views over the others. The Hebrew Bible presents the data. It does not provide the systematic conclusion.

Scripture does NOT settle this question with the clarity it settles, for example, the resurrection of Christ, the deity of YHWH, or the sinfulness of humanity. The Angel of the LORD is a second-order theological question --- vitally important, devotionally rich, but not a matter on which Scripture speaks with one unambiguous voice.

### Christocentrism

The New Testament tips the balance. Paul says "that Rock was Christ" (1 Cor 10:4). John says Isaiah "saw his glory" (John 12:41). Stephen recounts the angel in the bush as the figure who gave the law (Acts 7:30--38). Jude's manuscript tradition reads "Jesus" as the one who saved Israel from Egypt (Jude 5). The apostolic church identified Christ --- not the Father, not a created angel --- with specific OT theophanic events.

But the Hebrews counterargument keeps the question open. If Hebrews corrects against identifying the Son *with* angels, the Christophany identification is weakened. If Hebrews corrects against identifying the Son *as a created being* (which is what the argument addresses), the identification survives. The Christocentric pillar leans toward the Christophany reading. It does not lock the door.

### Antiquity

The pre-Nicene consensus strongly favors the Christophany reading. Justin, Irenaeus, Tertullian, Novatian, Eusebius --- different schools, regions, centuries --- all reached the same conclusion. This was convergent reading, not a single inherited tradition. The post-Nicene shift is historically explicable (the Arian crisis), but for three centuries the church held one view.

The Jewish parallels confirm that the "second power" reading is not a Christian imposition. Philo's Logos, the Targum Memra, and the Talmud's warning against "two powers in heaven" all arise from the same OT texts, independently of Christian theology.

### Reality

Multiple communities practice their worship fruitfully under different readings. The pastoral fruit test does not point in one direction; it confirms that God works through multiple honest readings of difficult texts.

### What SCAR Priority Says

When Scripture is ambiguous, the SCAR framework instructs us to look to Christocentrism. The New Testament leans toward identifying Christ with the Old Testament theophanies --- not univocally, but substantially. When Christocentrism leans in one direction but faces a serious counterargument (Hebrews 1--2), the framework instructs us to look to Antiquity. The pre-Nicene consensus is strong, unanimous, and geographically diverse. The post-Nicene shift, while theologically serious, is historically conditioned.

The cumulative weight of the evidence tilts toward the Christophany reading. This is not a verdict. The evidence *tilts*; it does not *settle*. A thoughtful Christian who holds the Theophany view, the Created Angel view, or the Literary Device view can do so with integrity, marshal serious arguments, and produce genuine pastoral fruit. The SCAR framework does not excommunicate minority readings. It provides a principled way of saying: "If I must weigh the evidence, this is where the weight falls --- but the scales are not locked."

The reader is asked to take the evidence presented in Chapters 1--4, apply the SCAR priority framework personally, and reach a conviction before God.

## Questions for the Reader

The following questions are not rhetorical. They are not leading. They are designed to help the reader engage with the evidence honestly, test each view against the strongest arguments of the others, and seek the Spirit's guidance in forming a conviction. No question presupposes its answer.

### From Scripture

1. When Jacob places "the Angel which redeemed me from all evil" in direct parallel with "God, before whom my fathers Abraham and Isaac did walk" (Genesis 48:15--16), calling both his personal *go'el* --- what is the most natural reading of the relationship between the Angel and God?

2. In Exodus 33, Moses pleads for God's presence, and YHWH responds by promising that his *panim* (face/presence) will go with Israel. Earlier, in Exodus 23:20--23, YHWH promised to send "an Angel" bearing his name. Is the Angel the *panim*? Does the promise of the presence resolve the promise of the Angel, replace it, or fulfill it?

3. In Zechariah 3:1--2, the Angel of the LORD stands before Joshua the high priest, and "the LORD" rebukes Satan. But the speaker is the Angel. He says, "The LORD rebuke thee" --- speaking of YHWH in the third person while being identified elsewhere in Zechariah as YHWH himself. Why does the Angel sometimes speak as YHWH and sometimes speak *about* YHWH, if they are the same figure? And if they are different, why is the Angel elsewhere identified as YHWH?

4. When Manoah realizes he has seen the Angel of the LORD, he cries: "We shall surely die, because we have seen God" (Judges 13:22). Why does Manoah conclude he has seen *God*, not merely an angel? And when the Angel tells Hagar "I will multiply thy seed exceedingly" (Genesis 16:10) --- who, other than God, has the authority to multiply a woman's descendants?

### From Christocentrism

5. When Paul writes that "that Rock was Christ" (1 Corinthians 10:4), does he mean Christ was *literally present* as the source of water in the wilderness, or that the Rock *typologically pointed to* Christ? What criteria would distinguish these two readings?

6. How do you weigh the Hebrews 1--2 argument? The author labors to show that the Son is superior to angels. Does this mean the Son cannot have functioned as a *mal'akh* (messenger), or does it mean the Son is not a *created being* in the angelic category? Hebrews 3:1 calls Christ "the Apostle" (*apostolos*, "one who is sent") --- if the Son can bear that title without being reduced to the category of human apostles, can he be called "Messenger" without being reduced to created angels?

7. John 12:41 says Isaiah "saw his glory, and spake of him" --- referring to Christ. Isaiah 6 describes a vision of "the Lord sitting upon a throne, high and lifted up." If John is correct that Isaiah saw *Christ's* glory, what does this imply about who appears in Old Testament theophanies? And if the NT authors did not intend to identify Christ with the Angel, why did they consistently identify Christ --- rather than the Father or the Spirit --- with these events?

### From Antiquity

8. The pre-Nicene fathers --- Justin, Irenaeus, Tertullian, Novatian, Eusebius --- unanimously identified the Angel with the pre-incarnate Son. These men disagreed fiercely on many theological questions, yet on this they spoke with one voice. Several were within a generation or two of the apostles. How much weight should this consensus carry? And if it was wrong, what explains the unanimity?

9. Augustine broke with three centuries of consensus because the Arian heresy was using Angel-Christology to argue that the Son was a creature. Was Augustine correcting an error the early fathers had made, or was he making a strategic concession that cost the church a legitimate christological tradition?

10. Philo of Alexandria, a Jew who never encountered Christianity, independently described the Logos as a "second God" derived from the same OT passages the church fathers would later use. The Targums substituted "the Memra (Word) of the LORD" where God acts visibly. The Talmud warned against "two powers in heaven." What does it tell us about the *texts themselves* --- independent of Christian theology --- that Jewish interpreters were independently reaching "second power" conclusions?

### From Reality

11. Which reading of the Angel of the LORD generates the most worship in your own devotional life? Does seeing Christ in the burning bush, at the oaks of Mamre, and wrestling with Jacob deepen your understanding of the incarnation --- or does another reading draw you closer to the God who drew near?

12. Whatever view you hold, can you charitably engage with the strongest reasons for the opposing view? If you hold the Christophany view, can you grant that Augustine's concern --- protecting the Son's deity from Arian misuse --- was legitimate? If you hold the Theophany or Created Angel view, can you account for why the NT authors consistently identified *Christ* with OT theophanic events, and why the entire pre-Nicene church reached the Christophany conclusion?

13. Godly, careful scholars hold each of these four views. Does this suggest the question is less important than it appears, or that the evidence is genuinely difficult and humility is appropriate even for those who hold strong convictions?

## A Word About Certainty and Charity

The identity of the figure who appeared to the patriarchs, spoke from the bush, wrestled with Jacob, and announced the birth of Samson --- this is a question about the character and activity of God. It deserves our most careful thought.

But the evidence, honestly weighed, does not compel uniformity. This is, in the language of theology, an *adiaphoron* of the second order --- not a matter of indifference, but a matter on which faithful Christians may disagree without breaking fellowship. The question is important enough to study carefully. It is not important enough to divide over.

Hold your view with confidence. The evidence supports convictions. But hold your brother with charity. He has good reasons too.

The Apostle Paul, who identified Christ with the Rock in the wilderness, also wrote:

> "For now we see through a glass, darkly; but then face to face: now I know in part; but then shall I know even as also I am known."
> --- 1 Corinthians 13:12 (KJV)

We see through a glass, darkly. The Angel of the LORD passages are among the most luminous --- and most mysterious --- windows in all of Scripture. They reveal God drawing near, God speaking, God redeeming, God wrestling with his people in the dust. What we see through them is real. But we do not yet see it face to face.

And the Lord who will one day resolve all our theological questions left us a standard for the meantime:

> "A new commandment I give unto you, That ye love one another; as I have loved you, that ye also love one another. By this shall all men know that ye are my disciples, if ye have love one to another."
> --- John 13:34--35 (KJV)

The Angel of the LORD --- whoever he is --- came to Hagar in her abandonment, to Jacob in his fear, to Moses in his obscurity, to Gideon in his weakness. He came with the name of God, the authority of God, and the tenderness of God. That much is certain. And that much, for now, is enough to send us back to the text with gratitude, and to our brothers and sisters with love.

## Summary

The SCAR Quadrilateral, applied to the Angel of the LORD, reveals a question that is richer and more complex than any single theological tradition has fully captured.

**All four views agree** on the extraordinary nature of the figure, the reality of the divine encounters, the significance of the "name" and authority language, and the identification-distinction paradox at the heart of the texts. The common ground is substantial.

**The genuine tensions** --- the Hebrews question, the pre-Nicene consensus vs. Augustinian correction, the hermeneutics of reading forward vs. backward, the limits of the agency principle, and the Arian shadow over christological titles --- remain unresolved. Each tension has strong arguments on both sides, and intellectual honesty requires acknowledging this.

**The SCAR priority framework** provides a principled way of weighing the evidence. Scripture is ambiguous. Christocentrism leans toward the Christophany reading. Antiquity strongly supports it. Reality confirms that multiple readings produce genuine fruit. The cumulative weight tilts toward seeing the Angel of the LORD as the pre-incarnate Christ --- but the tilt is a lean, not a lock.

What all four SCAR components point to, beyond any single view, is this: **the God of Israel is a God who draws near.** He does not remain in abstract transcendence. He enters the wilderness, the threshing floor, the wrestling ring, the flame. He speaks with human voices, appears in human forms, meets his people in their specific, individual, desperate circumstances. The Angel of the LORD passages are the Old Testament's most vivid testimony to this truth.

Whatever conclusion the reader reaches, may it be reached in humility, held in charity, and lived in worship of the God who has always --- from the wilderness to the incarnation to the present hour --- refused to leave his people alone.
