## Jewish Sources

### Philo of Alexandria, *On the Confusion of Tongues* (*De Confusione Linguarum*) 146 (c. 20 BC -- 50 AD)

> "And even if there be not as yet any one who is worthy to be called a son of God, nevertheless let him labour earnestly to be adorned according to his first-born word, the eldest of his angels, as the great archangel of many names; for he is called, the authority, and the name of God, and the Word, and man according to God's image, and he who sees, that is Israel."

**Source:** C.D. Yonge translation
**Context:** Philo calls the Logos God's "first-born word" and "the great archangel of many names." Language strikingly parallel to Colossians 1:15 and Exodus 23:21 ("my name is in him"), arrived at from Jewish Torah exegesis without Christian influence.

---

### Philo of Alexandria, *On Dreams* (*De Somniis*) I.228--230 (c. 20 BC -- 50 AD)

> "And the same allegory, as it seems to me, is conveyed in the passage of the scripture which is at present before us. For it is said to the person who is practising, 'I am the God who was seen by thee in the place of God.' Now here any one may rightly be surprised at the expression, 'I am the God who was seen by thee,' not in the place of me, but 'in the place of God,' as if it related to some other being. What then ought we to say? There is one true God only; but they who are called Gods, by an allegory, are numerous. On which account the holy word has on the present occasion pointed out him who is truly God by the use of the article, saying, 'I am **the** God,' but him who is so called by an allegory without the article, saying simply, 'Who was seen by thee in the place,' not of **the** God, but simply 'of God.' And it is he who is the Word of the first God, who said, 'I am the God who was seen by thee in the place of God;' as if his subordinate beings were ranged in rank beneath him."

**Source:** C.D. Yonge translation
**Context:** Interpreting Genesis 31:13. Philo distinguishes "the God" (the supreme deity) from "God" without the article (the Logos who appeared to Jacob). A Jewish "two powers" reading: the Angel is the Word of the first God --- genuinely called "God" yet subordinate to the transcendent Father.

---

### Philo of Alexandria, *On the Life of Abraham* 118--119 (c. 20 BC -- 50 AD)

> "What, then, are we to say? One of the three is the true God, the Father of the universe, and the other two are His Powers; the creative Power, according to which He made the world by His Word; and the governing Power, according to which He rules what He has made. The central Being is attended by each of His Powers as by body-guards, and presents to the mind, which has the power of seeing, sometimes the appearance of one Being, and sometimes of three."

**Source:** C.D. Yonge translation
**Context:** On the three visitors at Mamre (Genesis 18). The central figure is the true God; the two companions are his creative and governing Powers. A Jewish reading positing complex divine manifestation, structurally parallel to the Christian reading though philosophically rather than trinitarianly framed. *[Verify section numbering against Loeb Classical Library edition.]*

---

### Philo of Alexandria, *Questions on Genesis* IV.2 (c. 20 BC -- 50 AD)

> "Why does the angel say to Jacob, 'I am the God who was seen by thee in the place of God'? He here confirms most plainly what we have said above, that there is a difference between God and the Word, and that the Word is the image of God, and the first-born of all His works."

**Source:** *[Verify: C.D. Yonge's 1854-55 translation may not include Questions on Genesis, which survives primarily in Armenian. The standard English is Ralph Marcus's Loeb Classical Library edition (Philo Supplement I, 1953). Verify both the attribution to Yonge and the exact wording against Marcus.]*
**Context:** Philo's most explicit identification of the Angel with the Logos --- "the image of God, and the first-born of all His works." Language anticipating Colossians 1:15 by at least a generation.

---

### Targum Onqelos on the Pentateuch (c. 100--200 AD)

The Targum Onqelos is the standard Aramaic translation of the Pentateuch in the Babylonian Jewish tradition. It systematically introduces the *Memra* ("Word") of the LORD in passages where the Hebrew text describes God appearing or acting directly.

**Genesis 3:8** --- Where the Hebrew says "they heard the voice of the LORD God walking in the garden," Onqelos renders:

> "They heard the voice of the **Memra** of the LORD God walking in the garden."

**Genesis 28:20--21** --- Where Jacob makes his vow, "If God will be with me...the LORD shall be my God," Onqelos translates:

> "If the **Memra** of the LORD will be with me... the **Memra** of the LORD shall be my God."

**Exodus 19:17** --- Where the Hebrew says Moses "brought forth the people out of the camp to meet God," Onqelos renders:

> "...to meet the **Memra** of the LORD."

**Source:** Aramaic text; English renderings from Bernard Grossfeld. Critical edition: Alexander Sperber, *The Bible in Aramaic* (Brill, 1959--1973).
**Context:** The *Memra* substitutions show Jewish interpreters felt the same tension driving this study: God appears visibly yet is transcendent. The "Word" (*Memra*) of the LORD is the Aramaic counterpart to the Greek *Logos* --- a buffer term protecting divine transcendence while preserving divine agency.

---

### Targum Pseudo-Jonathan on Genesis 16:13 (c. 200--400 AD)

Where Hagar says of the Angel of the LORD, "Thou God seest me" (Hebrew: *'El Ro'i*), Targum Pseudo-Jonathan renders:

> "Thou art the God of seeing, for she said, Behold, I have seen the glory of the **Shekinah** of the LORD."

**Source:** English rendering from Michael Maher, *Targum Pseudo-Jonathan: Genesis* (Liturgical Press, 1992).
**Context:** The direct encounter between Hagar and the Angel/God is mediated through the *Shekinah* --- another buffer term like *Memra* that acknowledges divine presence while protecting divine transcendence.

---

### Targum Jonathan on Isaiah 63:9 (c. 200--400 AD)

Where the Hebrew reads "the angel of his presence saved them," Targum Jonathan renders this with reference to God's Word or presence acting on Israel's behalf, maintaining the pattern of divine action through an intermediary.

**Source:** English rendering from Bruce Chilton, *The Isaiah Targum* (Liturgical Press, 1987). *[Verify precise rendering --- wording varies across manuscripts.]*
**Context:** Isaiah 63:9 connects "the angel of his presence" with God's saving acts. The Targum follows the standard pattern: divine action mediated through an intermediary.

## Apostolic Era (c. 70--150 AD)

### Ignatius of Antioch, *To the Ephesians* 7 (c. 110 AD)

> "There is one Physician who is possessed both of flesh and spirit; both made and not made; God existing in flesh; true life in death; both of Mary and of God; first passible and then impassible --- even Jesus Christ our Lord."

**Source:** Roberts-Donaldson translation, ANF Vol. 1
**Context:** Ignatius does not develop Angel-Christology specifically --- his concern is anti-Docetic. But this passage affirms Christ's pre-existence and deity ("both made and not made; God existing in flesh") in terms compatible with the tradition.

---

### Epistle of Barnabas (c. 70--130 AD)

The *Epistle of Barnabas* does not explicitly discuss the Angel of the LORD passages but consistently reads the OT as speaking of Christ --- the same hermeneutic Justin Martyr will develop systematically.

**Source:** Roberts-Donaldson translation, ANF Vol. 1
**Context:** Included for completeness. Represents the christological OT hermeneutic at its earliest post-apostolic stage.

---

## Ante-Nicene Era (c. 150--325 AD)

### Justin Martyr, *Dialogue with Trypho* 56 (c. 155--160 AD)

**On the three men at Mamre and the destruction of Sodom (Genesis 18--19):**

> "Moses, the blessed and faithful servant of God, declares that He who appeared to Abraham under the oak in Mamre is God, sent with the two angels in His company to judge Sodom by Another who remains ever in the supercelestial places, invisible to all men, holding personal intercourse with none, whom we believe to be Maker and Father of all things. For he speaks thus: 'God appeared to him under the oak in Mamre, as he sat at his tent-door at noontide. And lifting up his eyes, he saw, and behold, three men stood before him; and when he saw them, he ran to meet them from the door of his tent; and he bowed himself toward the ground, and said...' And after a little: 'And the men rose up from thence, and looked toward Sodom. And the Lord said, Shall I hide from Abraham that thing which I do...' And again, after a little: 'The Lord went His way, as soon as He had left communing with Abraham; and Abraham returned to his place. And there came two angels to Sodom at even; and Lot sat in the gate of Sodom...' And again: 'Then the Lord rained upon Sodom brimstone and fire from the Lord out of heaven.'"

**Source:** Roberts-Donaldson translation, ANF Vol. 1
**Context:** Justin's opening argument from Genesis 18--19. Genesis 19:24 ("the LORD rained...from the LORD") is read as describing two LORDs: one on earth (the Son) and one in heaven (the Father).

---

### Justin Martyr, *Dialogue with Trypho* 56 --- Trypho's Response and Justin's Thesis (c. 155--160 AD)

> "When I had quoted this passage, I asked them if they had understood it. And they said they had understood it, but that the passages adduced brought forward no proof that there is any other God or Lord, or that the Holy Spirit says so, besides the Maker of all things."

Followed by Justin's thesis statement:

> "I shall attempt to persuade you, since you have understood the Scriptures, [of the truth] of what I say, that there is, and that there is said to be, another God and Lord subject to the Maker of all things; who is also called an Angel, because He announces to men whatsoever the Maker of all things --- above whom there is no other God --- wishes to announce to them."

**Source:** Roberts-Donaldson translation, ANF Vol. 1
**Context:** Justin's programmatic thesis. "Another God and Lord" --- truly divine, yet subject to the Father. Called "Angel" because he announces (*angellei*) the Father's messages, not because he is a creature. Argued from the Jewish Scriptures to a Jewish interlocutor.

---

### Justin Martyr, *Dialogue with Trypho* 56 --- Numerical Distinction (c. 155--160 AD)

> "The Scriptural passages which I have adduced prove this clearly. I shall now recite from the same Scriptures, and endeavour to prove from them that He who was seen by Abraham, Jacob, and Moses, and is called God, is distinct from Him who made all things --- numerically, I mean, not [distinct] in will. For I affirm that He has never at any time done anything which He who made the world --- above whom there is no other God --- has not wished Him both to do and to engage Himself with."

**Source:** Roberts-Donaldson translation, ANF Vol. 1
**Context:** "Numerically, I mean, not in will" --- remarkably precise trinitarian language for the mid-second century, predating Nicaea by over 150 years. Guards against both modalism and ditheism.

---

### Justin Martyr, *Dialogue with Trypho* 58--59 --- The Burning Bush (c. 155--160 AD)

> "The word of God says to Moses through the Angel in a flame of fire: 'I am that I am, the God of Abraham, the God of Isaac, the God of Jacob, the God of thy fathers: go down into Egypt, and bring forth my people.' And those who wish can learn what followed; for it is not possible to put down all the writing here. But so much is written for the manifestation of the fact that He who is both Angel and God, and Lord, and who appeared as a man to Abraham, and who wrestled with Jacob in the likeness of a man, was seen by Moses and talked with him in the flame of the bush."

**Source:** Roberts-Donaldson translation, ANF Vol. 1
**Context:** The burning bush (Exodus 3). "He who is both Angel and God" --- the paradox of the tradition in five words. Justin connects the bush to Abraham and Jacob's wrestling as appearances of the same divine figure.

---

### Justin Martyr, *Dialogue with Trypho* 61 --- The Titles of the Son (c. 155--160 AD)

> "I shall give you another testimony, my friends, from the Scriptures, that God begat before all creatures a Beginning, [who was] a certain rational power [proceeding] from Himself, who is called by the Holy Spirit, now the Glory of the Lord, now the Son, again Wisdom, again an Angel, then God, and then Lord and Logos."

**Source:** Roberts-Donaldson translation, ANF Vol. 1
**Context:** The proliferation of titles is itself the point --- Glory, Son, Wisdom, Angel, God, Lord, Logos all name the same divine figure "begotten before all creatures." "Angel" is one title among many and does not reduce the figure to a creature.

---

### Justin Martyr, *Dialogue with Trypho* 126 --- The Son Ministers to the Father (c. 155--160 AD)

> "I shall now refer to the Scriptures, and I shall endeavour to persuade you that He to whom all these titles belong ministers to the Father of all things, according to His will, and that He was born of the Virgin, and became man, and suffered, and was raised again, and ascended into heaven... I shall endeavour to prove from these very Scriptures that this person is not the Father, but His Word and Firstborn and Power; and that He afterwards became man through the Virgin, as we have learned from the memoirs."

**Source:** Roberts-Donaldson translation, ANF Vol. 1
**Context:** Justin connects the OT theophanies to the incarnation: the same person who bore all the divine titles "afterwards became man through the Virgin." One continuous story of the Son's ministry.

---

### Justin Martyr, *Dialogue with Trypho* 128 --- The Fire-from-Fire Analogy (c. 155--160 AD)

> "And that this power which the prophetic word calls God, as has been also amply demonstrated, and Angel, is not numbered [as different] in name only like the light of the sun, but is indeed something numerically distinct, I have discussed briefly in what has gone before; when I asserted that this power was begotten from the Father, by His power and will, but not by abscission, as if the essence of the Father were divided; as all other things partitioned and divided are not the same after as before they were divided: and, for the sake of example, I took the case of fires kindled from a fire, which we see to be distinct from it, and yet that from which many can be kindled is by no means made less, but remains the same."

**Source:** Roberts-Donaldson translation, ANF Vol. 1
**Context:** The fire-from-fire analogy: a torch lit from another is genuinely distinct yet the original is not diminished. The Son proceeds from the Father "not by abscission." This image echoes into the Nicene Creed: "Light of Light, true God of true God."

---

### Justin Martyr, *First Apology* 63 (c. 155 AD)

> "And that the prophets are inspired by no other than the Divine Word, even you, as I fancy, will grant. And that He suffered, and ascended to heaven after His crucifixion, and that He will come again as Judge of all men who have ever lived, beginning with Adam himself; and that He was known by Moses to be the God of Abraham, and of Isaac, and of Jacob, and that He appeared to Moses and to Abraham, and to other patriarchs before Moses."

**Source:** Roberts-Donaldson translation, ANF Vol. 1
**Context:** Addressed to Emperor Antoninus Pius. The same identification made more briefly for a pagan audience. Justin treats it as established fact, suggesting the identification was already traditional by the mid-150s.

---

### Irenaeus of Lyon, *Against Heresies* III.6.1 (c. 180 AD)

> "Since, therefore, the Father is truly Lord, and the Son truly Lord, the Holy Spirit has fitly designated them by the title of Lord. And again, referring to the destruction of the Sodomites, the Scripture says, 'Then the LORD rained upon Sodom and upon Gomorrah fire and brimstone from the LORD out of heaven.' For it here points out that the Son, who had also been talking with Abraham, had received power to judge the Sodomites for their wickedness. And this [text], 'The LORD rained from the LORD,' --- that [person] from whom also He received [the commission to act]; that is, [the tradition] of the Father who made the Son."

**Source:** Roberts-Donaldson translation, ANF Vol. 1
**Context:** Follows Justin's reading of Genesis 19:24: the earthly LORD is the Son, the heavenly LORD is the Father. Represents the received tradition of the second-century church.

---

### Irenaeus of Lyon, *Against Heresies* IV.20.7 (c. 180 AD)

> "For by means of the creation itself, the Word reveals God the Creator; and by means of the world [does He declare] the Lord the Maker of the world; and by means of the formation [of man] the Artificer who formed him; and by the Son that Father who begat the Son: and these things do indeed address all men in the same manner, but all do not in the same way believe them."

**Source:** Roberts-Donaldson translation, ANF Vol. 1
**Context:** The Word/Son is the permanent mediator of divine revelation --- in creation, in history, and in the theophanies. The Father is invisible by nature; the Son is the visible image through whom God has always disclosed himself.

---

### Theophilus of Antioch, *To Autolycus* II.22 (c. 180 AD)

> "God, then, having His own Word internal within His own bowels, begat Him, emitting Him along with His own wisdom before all things. He had this Word as a helper in the things that were created by Him, and by Him He made all things... But the God and Father, indeed, of all cannot be contained, and is not found in a place, for there is no place of His rest; but His Word, through whom He made all things, being His power and His wisdom, assuming the character of the Father and Lord of all, went to the garden in the person of God, and conversed with Adam."

**Source:** Roberts-Donaldson translation, ANF Vol. 2
**Context:** Bishop of Antioch. The Father "cannot be contained" --- therefore the one who walked in Eden was the Word, "assuming the character of the Father." Angel-Christology applied to the earliest theophany in Scripture (Genesis 3:8).

---

### Clement of Alexandria, *Stromata* VII.2 (c. 200 AD)

> "For the Son is the power of God, as being the Father's most ancient Word before the production of all things, and His Wisdom. He is then properly called the Teacher of the beings formed by Him. Nor does He ever abandon care for men, by being turned from them. For He assumed the nature of flesh, which is susceptible of suffering, that, for our sake, He might both endure suffering and teach us patience."

**Source:** Wilson translation, ANF Vol. 2. *[Verify: confirm this quotation is from Stromata VII.2 specifically. Clement's Stromata is loosely organized, and the chapter/section numbering varies across editions.]*
**Context:** Head of the catechetical school in Alexandria. The Son is "the Father's most ancient Word before the production of all things" who never abandoned care for humanity. The incarnation is the culmination, not the beginning, of the Son's engagement with the world.

---

### Tertullian, *Against Praxeas* 14 (c. 213 AD)

> "Learn then, with me, as this heresy is now to be handled, how it is possible for the Father to be different from the Son, inasmuch as He was not personally visible. Let us go further back, and inquire whether He was always in the habit of thus visibly appearing. For if even from the beginning He was always being seen, He could not be the Father, who was invisible. And even if the Son was the visible one, still it was necessary that He should be called an Angel, because He was carrying out the Father's mission."

**Source:** Peter Holmes translation, ANF Vol. 3
**Context:** Against the modalist Praxeas. If the Father himself had appeared, there would be no need for the "Angel" title. The title proves personal distinction within the Godhead.

---

### Tertullian, *Against Praxeas* 16 --- The Son's Progressive Revelation (c. 213 AD)

> "It is the Son, therefore, who has been from the beginning administering judgment, throwing down the haughty tower, and dividing the tongues, punishing the whole world by the violence of waters, raining upon Sodom and Gomorrah fire and brimstone, as the LORD from the LORD. For He it was who at all times came down to hold converse with men, from Adam on to the patriarchs and the prophets, in vision, in dream, in mirror, in dark saying; ever from the beginning laying the foundation of the course [of His dispensations], which He meant to follow out to the very last. Thus was He ever learning even as God to converse with men upon earth, being no other than the Word which was to be made flesh."

**Source:** Peter Holmes translation, ANF Vol. 3
**Context:** One of the most striking pre-Nicene passages. The Son "was ever learning even as God to converse with men" --- the OT appearances were the Logos on his way to the incarnation. Each theophany was a step toward Bethlehem. "The LORD from the LORD" echoes Genesis 19:24 and anticipates the Nicene Creed.

---

### Tertullian, *Against Praxeas* 16 --- "The Angel of the Great Counsel" (c. 213 AD)

> "Thus the Father also was revealed by the Son, who is in the Father's bosom. He also always 'rained from the LORD' --- that is, from Himself --- upon Sodom and Gomorrah that fire which was the judgment of the Father; He also was seen by Abraham, as the LORD also, face to face, as it is written; and He is also 'the LORD [who] talked with Moses' at the bush. And yet, in all these manifestations, He it was who also appeared as 'the angel of the great counsel.'"

**Source:** Peter Holmes translation, ANF Vol. 3
**Context:** "The angel of the great counsel" (*magni consilii angelus*) comes from the LXX of Isaiah 9:6, linking the Angel of the LORD tradition directly to the messianic prophecy. The same person appeared to Abraham, spoke to Moses, and was prophesied by Isaiah.

---

### Tertullian, *Against Marcion* III.9 (c. 207 AD)

> "It is, then, the Christ of the Creator whom the Old Testament prophets described, since the Christ of another god would have had no prophets of the rival god to predict Him. See whether this Christ comes to do what His Father had commanded Him: whether the Son was really sent by the Father, or whether the Father came Himself. But how could the Father come, seeing that He is invisible? And how could the Son come, if it was the Father who appeared to Abraham by the oaks of Mamre?"

**Source:** Peter Holmes translation, ANF Vol. 3. *[Verify: this quotation's argument about the Father's invisibility and who appeared at Mamre reads more like Against Praxeas than Against Marcion III.9. Confirm exact chapter reference.]*
**Context:** Against Marcion, who claimed the OT God was a different deity. Tertullian responds that the Son was present in both testaments. The Angel-Christology served against multiple heresies: modalism and Marcionism alike.

---

### Origen of Alexandria, *Against Celsus* (*Contra Celsum*) V.53 (c. 248 AD)

> "For we do not assert that the body of the Logos is that which was seen by those of old; since the Logos divested Himself of His body, and, being again as He was before that, will not appear again in that shape in which He appeared. And that it is not impossible for the divine nature to appear in a bodily form --- a fact which Celsus considers absurd --- is proved by the passages of Scripture in which it is related that God appeared to Abraham, or to Jacob, or to any other of the saints."

**Source:** Crombie translation, ANF Vol. 4
**Context:** Responding to the pagan Celsus. The Logos who appeared to the patriarchs did not appear in the incarnation body --- but it was the Logos who appeared. The OT theophanies prove "it is not impossible for the divine nature to appear in a bodily form."

---

### Origen of Alexandria, *On First Principles* (*De Principiis*) I.3.5 (c. 230 AD)

> "The God and Father, who holds the universe together, is superior to every being that exists, for He imparts to each one from His own existence that which each one is; the Son, being less than the Father, is superior to rational creatures alone (for He is second to the Father); the Holy Spirit is still less, and dwells within the saints alone. So that in this way the power of the Father is greater than that of the Son and of the Holy Spirit, and that of the Son is more than that of the Holy Spirit."

**Source:** Crombie translation, ANF Vol. 4
**Context:** Reflects subordinationist tendencies typical of pre-Nicene theology. Included because it illustrates the framework within which Angel-Christology operated: the Son was genuinely divine but "second to the Father" --- mapping onto the Angel as truly God yet sent by the Father.

---

### Novatian of Rome, *On the Trinity* (*De Trinitate*) 18 (c. 250 AD)

> "For if it had been the Father who appeared to Abraham, why did He call Himself an Angel? since the Father is not an Angel; or if it was an Angel, how said He, 'I am God Almighty'? But because He was neither the Father, who could not be called an Angel; nor a mere Angel, who could not be called God Almighty: but it remained that He who was both Angel and God --- that is, the Son of God --- should be called God and Angel: God, because He is the Son of God; Angel, because He announces the Father's purpose."

**Source:** ANF Vol. 5 translation
**Context:** Crystalline logic: the figure cannot be the Father (never called "Angel") nor a mere angel (never called "God Almighty"). He must be the Son --- "Angel" describes function, "God" describes nature. The most systematic pre-Nicene treatment of the question.

---

### Novatian of Rome, *On the Trinity* 19 (c. 250 AD)

> "This is He who was seen by Jacob, who wrestled with him as a man. And when Jacob asked him who He was, He would not say that He was an Angel, but God. For He said, 'I am the God who appeared to thee in the place of Bethel.' And yet He was at the same time called an Angel. And thus from all these passages it is gathered that this is not the Father who was seen, but the Son, who both usually and always was seen of the patriarchs."

**Source:** ANF Vol. 5 translation
**Context:** On Genesis 31--32 (Jacob). The figure identifies himself as God yet is called an Angel. "Usually and always was seen of the patriarchs" --- the Son's appearances were the normal mode of God's self-revelation.

---

### Novatian of Rome, *On the Trinity* 26 (c. 250 AD)

> "If, then, the same Person is shown to be the Angel and God; if the Angel is not the Father, and yet the Angel is called God; and if this God was always seen by the ancient fathers: it follows that it was the Son who was seen, inasmuch as the Son is both God and the Angel of great counsel."

**Source:** ANF Vol. 5 translation
**Context:** Concluding syllogism. "The Angel of great counsel" echoes Tertullian and the LXX of Isaiah 9:6. Novatian represents the mature pre-Nicene consensus, a century after Justin.

---

### Eusebius of Caesarea, *Ecclesiastical History* I.2.7--8 (c. 315 AD)

> "But it is not possible to understand these things otherwise than as a reference to the first and only Word of God, who before all worlds, and before all creation, and before all ages, was with the Father... who was called by the prophetic Spirit the Son of God, the visible Son, who was always present with the Father, but who was also set forth as the God of all men, both Gentile and Jew."

**Source:** NPNF translation
**Context:** Eusebius writes as a historian summarizing received tradition, not arguing for a novel view. Present at the Council of Nicaea (325 AD).

---

### Eusebius of Caesarea, *Ecclesiastical History* I.2.9 (c. 315 AD)

> "And that not corporeally but in the divine power of the Word, those who are said to have appeared from time to time to the men of old presented themselves. For it was fitting that the Creator of all things should be the one to provide for the needs of all; this then was the Son, the very Word of God, who appeared from time to time to the ancient holy men; who was the one who appeared to Abraham sitting at the oak of Mamre."

**Source:** NPNF translation
**Context:** Explicitly identifies the one at Mamre as "the Son, the very Word of God." Appearances were "not corporeally but in the divine power of the Word." Summarizes the consensus Justin articulated 160 years earlier.

## Post-Nicene (For Context)

### Augustine of Hippo, *De Trinitate* II.17.32 (c. 400 AD)

> "When therefore we read in those old books that God appeared to our fathers, we must be careful not to suppose that any person of the Trinity was seen in His own substance. For the substance of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit, being invisible, incorruptible, and unchangeable, could not have been visible in itself."

**Source:** Translation from *A Select Library of the Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers*, Series 1, Vol. 3
**Context:** The definitive break with the pre-Nicene consensus. No person of the Trinity was seen in his own substance --- the OT theophanies were mediated through created phenomena. Guards divine transcendence but severs the personal Christ-Israel connection the pre-Nicene fathers affirmed.

---

### Augustine of Hippo, *De Trinitate* III.11.22 (c. 400 AD)

> "For the will of God regarding any visible creature through which God appeared, could be formed from any one of the three persons of the Trinity: since the Trinity itself, which is inseparable, works inseparably. So it does not necessarily follow, because the Father is not the Son, and the Son is not the Father, and the Holy Spirit is neither the Father nor the Son, that the sensible creature through which God was manifested was formed solely by the Son. For it was formed indivisibly by the Father, Son, and Holy Spirit."

**Source:** Translation from *A Select Library of the Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers*, Series 1, Vol. 3
**Context:** Rests on inseparable operations (*opera Trinitatis ad extra indivisa sunt*): whatever God does externally, all three persons do together. Therefore the theophanies cannot be assigned to the Son specifically. A philosophical argument, not an exegetical one --- it does not arise from new evidence in the biblical text.

## Rabbinic Sources

### Babylonian Talmud, *Sanhedrin* 38b (c. 200--500 AD)

The Talmudic discussion records that a *min* (heretic --- often understood as referring to a Jewish-Christian or a Gnostic) argued from Exodus 24:1 ("Come up unto the LORD") that the speaker must be someone other than God, since he refers to God in the third person. Rabbi Idi responded by identifying the figure as Metatron, "the angel whose name is like the name of his Master" --- a reference to Exodus 23:21, "my name is in him."

The passage continues:

> "If so, we should worship him! [Metatron, that is, since God's name is in him.] The same passage says, 'Do not rebel against him' --- do not exchange Me for him. 'He will not forgive your transgressions' --- he does not even have the ability to forgive; he is merely a messenger."

**Source:** Babylonian Talmud; Soncino translation (ed. I. Epstein, 1935--1952) or Koren/Steinsaltz edition. *[Verify exact wording against preferred edition.]*
**Context:** The rabbis' energetic polemic is itself evidence that the "two powers" reading was widespread --- you do not warn repeatedly against a position nobody holds. Metatron (a created being) is the rabbinic alternative to the Christian identification. See Alan Segal, *Two Powers in Heaven* (1977).

### Exodus Rabbah on Exodus 23:20 (c. 400--900 AD)

The midrashic collection *Exodus Rabbah* (also known as *Shemot Rabbah*) comments on Exodus 23:20 ("Behold, I send an angel before thee") with the observation:

> "His name is like the name of his Master."

This echoes the Talmudic tradition in *Sanhedrin* 38b and derives from Exodus 23:21 ("my name is in him"). The midrash identifies the angel as Metatron and uses the principle "his name is like the name of his Master" to explain how a created angel could bear the divine name without being divine himself.

**Source:** *Exodus Rabbah* (Midrash Rabbah series); Soncino translation (eds. H. Freedman and M. Simon, 1939). *[Verify section number --- varies by edition.]*
**Context:** "His name is like the name of his Master" --- the rabbinic solution to Exodus 23:21. The Angel is Metatron, an exalted creature whose name resembles God's without implying divinity. Both Jewish and Christian traditions read the same text and confront the same puzzle.

### 3 Enoch (*Sefer Hekhalot*) 12:5 and Related Passages (c. 5th--6th century AD)

In *3 Enoch* (also known as *Sefer Hekhalot*, "Book of the Heavenly Palaces"), the figure of Metatron is identified as the translated patriarch Enoch, elevated to the status of "the lesser YHWH" (*YHWH ha-qatan*):

> "Rabbi Ishmael said: Metatron, the Angel of the Presence, said to me: The Holy One, blessed be He, called me 'the Lesser YHWH' in the presence of His whole household in the height, as it is written, 'My name is in him.'"

**Source:** P. Alexander translation in *Old Testament Pseudepigrapha*, ed. J.H. Charlesworth (Doubleday, 1983), Vol. 1. *[Verify exact wording.]*
**Context:** The most developed Jewish parallel to Angel-Christology. Metatron bears God's name and acts as vice-regent --- yet is explicitly a created being (the translated Enoch). The structural parallel:

| Attribute | Angel of the LORD (OT) | Metatron (3 Enoch) | Christ (NT) |
|-----------|----------------------|-------------------|-------------|
| Bears God's name | Exodus 23:21 | 3 Enoch 12:5 | Philippians 2:9 |
| Called "Lord" / divine titles | Judges 6:14, Genesis 16:13 | "Lesser YHWH" | John 20:28 |
| Mediates God's presence | Exodus 33:14--15 | Throne-attendant | Hebrews 1:3 |
| Sent by God | Exodus 23:20 | Sent on missions | John 17:3 |

Both traditions respond to the same OT data. The Jewish tradition resolves the tension by making the figure an exalted creature; the Christian tradition identifies him as the eternal, divine Son.
