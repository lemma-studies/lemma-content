# Appendix F: Edward Irving --- Prophet, Forerunner, Cautionary Tale

Edward Irving (1792--1834) stands at one of the most consequential crossroads in the history of eschatology. A Scottish Presbyterian minister of extraordinary gifts, he helped revive premillennialism, translated the work that seeded futurism in Protestant thought, helped lead the conferences that laid the groundwork for dispensationalism, and ignited the first modern tongues movement --- all before dying of tuberculosis at forty-two.

Irving is claimed as a forefather by Pentecostals, dispensationalists, and charismatics alike, yet he belonged fully to none of them. His story illuminates how eschatology, pneumatology, and ecclesiology can become entangled --- and how a movement born in genuine spiritual hunger can produce consequences its founder never imagined.

---

## The Man

Edward Irving was born on August 4, 1792, in Annan, Dumfriesshire, Scotland. He entered the University of Edinburgh at thirteen and was licensed to preach by the Church of Scotland in 1815. After several years as a schoolmaster and assistant minister, he was called to the Caledonian Chapel in Hatton Garden, London, in 1822.

Within months, Irving became the most famous preacher in London. His congregation overflowed with members of Parliament, literary figures, and society elites. George Canning, Samuel Taylor Coleridge, and Thomas Carlyle (the essayist, his close friend) all attended. A new church was built for him at Regent Square in 1827 to accommodate the crowds.

But Irving was never merely fashionable. He was deeply learned, passionately earnest, and increasingly consumed by two convictions: that Christ's return was imminent, and that the apostolic gifts of the Spirit had never been meant to cease.

## The Counter-Reformation Origins of Futurism

To understand what Irving unleashed, we must go back two and a half centuries before him --- to the Counter-Reformation.

> **A note on sources and framing:** The genealogy traced in this section --- Ribera to Alcazar to Lacunza to Irving --- is based on primary sources that are digitized and publicly available (see the table at the end of this appendix). The most frequently cited secondary source in English is LeRoy Froom, *Prophetic Faith of Our Fathers* (4 vols., 1946--1954), which documents this chain meticulously. Froom was a Seventh-day Adventist with an institutional interest in defending historicism, and his *framing* is polemical, but his *citations* of primary sources are generally reliable and can be independently verified. Readers should be aware of this lens. The facts of textual transmission presented below are not in scholarly dispute; the question of *motive* --- whether these Jesuit commentaries were calculated apologetics or sincere exegesis --- is.

### The Problem: Protestant Historicism

By the mid-sixteenth century, virtually every major Protestant Reformer --- Luther, Calvin, Knox, Cranmer, the Westminster divines --- had identified the papacy as the Antichrist of 2 Thessalonians 2 and the beast of Revelation 13. This was not fringe polemic; it was the standard Protestant position, built on **historicist** interpretation: the prophecies of Daniel and Revelation were being fulfilled across the sweep of church history, and the papal office fit the description.

This identification was devastating to Rome's authority. Two Jesuit scholars, working independently, produced commentaries on Revelation that --- whatever their authors' conscious intentions --- both had the effect of removing the papacy from prophetic scrutiny. Whether these were calculated apologetic responses or sincere exegetical conclusions that happened to serve Catholic interests is a question readers must weigh for themselves. What is not in dispute is that the commentaries exist, that they were written by Jesuits during the Counter-Reformation, and that they offered two alternative frameworks to Protestant historicism.

### Strategy One: Futurism --- Francisco Ribera (1590)

**Francisco Ribera** (1537--1591), a Spanish Jesuit at the University of Salamanca and confessor to Teresa of Avila, published *In Sacram Beati Ioannis Apostoli & Evangelistae Apocalypsin Commentarii* in 1590 --- a 500-page Latin commentary on Revelation that became the foundation of systematic futurism.

Ribera's core argument: the first few chapters of Revelation applied to ancient pagan Rome in John's own time, but chapters 4--22 referred to a future period of **three and a half literal years** immediately before the Second Coming. The Antichrist would be a single individual who would rebuild the temple in Jerusalem, abolish Christianity, be received by the Jews, and conquer the world --- all in this brief period.

The apologetic effect was immediate: by placing the Antichrist in the distant future as a single individual, Ribera's scheme removed the papacy from the equation entirely. Whether this was Ribera's primary motivation or a consequence of sincere exegesis is debated; what is clear is how his Protestant contemporaries received it. Thomas Brightman, upon encountering Ribera's work, observed:

> "Is it even so (said I) doe the Papists take heart again, so as that book which of a long time before they would scarce suffer any man to touch, they dare now take in hand to intreat fully upon it?... dare proclaime to the world, that any other thing rather is poynted at in it than their Pope of Rome?"
> --- Thomas Brightman, *A Revelation of the Apocalyps*, Preface (original orthography), as reproduced in Froom, *Prophetic Faith of Our Fathers*, Vol. 2, pp. 512-513

**Roberto Bellarmine** (1542--1621), Cardinal and Doctor of the Church, refined Ribera's position in his monumental *Disputationes de Controversiis* (1581--1593). Bellarmine's arguments were more systematic and drew on patristic authority:

> "Antichrist will come particularly on account of the Jews, and will be received by them as if he were a Messiah."

> "He is going to be born from the nation and race of the Jews, be circumcised, and shall observe the Sabbath, at least for a time."

> "Therefore, just as Christ first came to the Jews to whom he had been promised and by whom he had been awaited, and at length also joined the nations to himself, so also Antichrist will first come to the Jews, by whom he is awaited..."
> --- Bellarmine, *De Romano Pontifice*, Book 3 (trans. Ryan Grant, Mediatrix Press)

Ribera's 1590 Latin text is digitized on the Internet Archive but has never been translated into English. Bellarmine's Antichrist section is available in English translation (Grant, Mediatrix Press), now digitized on the Internet Archive; the three quotations above are verified verbatim against Book 3, Chapter XII ("On the Begetting of Antichrist") of the Grant translation (Vol. II: Books 3-5).

### Strategy Two: Preterism --- Luis de Alcazar (1614)

Simultaneously, another Spanish Jesuit pursued the opposite approach. **Luis de Alcazar** (1554--1613) published *Vestigatio arcani sensus in Apocalypsi* (Investigation of the Hidden Sense of the Apocalypse) posthumously in 1614 --- a 900-page commentary arguing that *everything* in Revelation except the last three chapters had already been fulfilled in the early centuries.

Alcazar identified the Antichrist as Nero. Babylon was pagan Rome. The seals and trumpets were God's judgments on Judaism, culminating in the destruction of Jerusalem in AD 70. The beasts represented the pagan empire, not the papal office.

Alcazar knew he contradicted Ribera --- and said so explicitly. Yet both interpretations, futurist and preterist, achieved the same practical result: **lifting the prophetic symbols of Babylon and Antichrist out of contemporary history, thus releasing Rome from the identification that Protestant historicists had pressed.**

Whether this convergence reflects coordinated institutional strategy or two independent scholars arriving at different conclusions that happened to serve the same apologetic interest is a question the sources do not settle definitively. What the sources *do* show is that two Jesuit commentaries, published within twenty-four years of each other, offered two mutually exclusive alternatives to Protestant historicism --- and that both gained traction within Catholic scholarship.

### Lacunza: The Jesuit Who Turned the Weapon Around

Two centuries later, **Manuel Lacunza** (1731--1801), a Chilean Jesuit, took Ribera's futurist framework and filled it with content that would have horrified its creator.

Lacunza was expelled from Chile in 1767 when Charles III banished all Jesuits from Spanish territories. In 1773, Pope Clement XIV dissolved the entire Jesuit order (*Dominus ac Redemptor*), reducing Lacunza to secular status. He withdrew to a solitary house on the outskirts of Imola, Italy, where he spent the next two decades in austere isolation, studying Scripture.

Writing under the pseudonym **Juan Josafat Ben-Ezra** --- styled as a converted Jewish rabbi --- Lacunza completed his three-volume *La Venida del Mesias en Gloria y Majestad* around 1790. The pseudonym served multiple purposes: fear of the Index, strategic credibility, and a desire to reach Protestant readers who would distrust a Jesuit author.

His conclusions diverged sharply from both Catholic orthodoxy and from the original purpose of Ribera's futurism:

**On the Antichrist as a corporate entity, not an individual:**

> "How then can this beast, and all the particular things spoken of it, be accommodated to, be possibly conceived of, an individual and singular person?"
> --- Lacunza, *The Coming of Messiah in Glory and Majesty* (Irving's 1827 translation), Vol. I, p. 202

> "Now the new beast, the moral body composed of many seducers, will doubtless in those times prove infinitely more prejudicial than all the first beast."
> --- Lacunza, Vol. I, p. 219

**On the Catholic Church's own apostasy:**

> "Yes, my friend, our priesthood: this it is, and nothing else, which is here signified... shall prove in those times its greatest scandal and most perilous snare."
> --- Lacunza, Vol. I, p. 220

> "Rome, not idolatrous but Christian... may very well, without ceasing from this dignity, at some time or other incur the guilt, and before God be held guilty of fornication with the kings of the earth..."
> --- Lacunza, Vol. I, p. 252

**On literal interpretation against the Church Fathers:**

> "All this, and much more, which is in Scripture, must one day be verified, for till now they have not been verified."
> --- Lacunza, Vol. I, p. 152

**On a two-stage coming of Christ** (the passage that most directly foreshadows later rapture theology):

> "...upon his coming forth from heaven, and much before his arrival at the earth, he will give his orders... all those of this class then living shall be joined to the dead in Christ already raised, shall ascend from the earth..."
> --- Lacunza, Vol. I, pp. 99--100

> "...there shall forthwith begin to be accomplished over this orb of the earth those great and horrible things which are announced for that day..."
> --- Lacunza, Vol. II, p. 216

The differences from Ribera were systematic:

| Element | Ribera (1590) | Lacunza (~1790) |
|---------|--------------|-----------------|
| **Antichrist** | Single future individual | Corporate "moral body" of apostasy |
| **The Church** | Exonerated; papacy is not the Antichrist | Implicated; Catholic Church will apostatize |
| **Israel** | Standard replacement theology | Literal future restoration |
| **Millennium** | Amillennial (standard Catholic) | Premillennial; two literal resurrections |
| **Christ's return** | Single event | Two-stage coming (seed of rapture concept) |
| **Purpose** | Defend the papacy from Protestant historicists | Indict the papacy from within |

The irony is extraordinary: a Jesuit tool designed to protect the papacy was reworked by a defrocked Jesuit exile into a system that *indicted* the papacy --- then translated by a Scottish Presbyterian into the seed of modern dispensationalism.

### The Vatican Responds

The manuscript circulated "from Havana to Cape Horn" before being printed. The first edition appeared in Cadiz in 1812. On **September 6, 1824**, Pope Leo XII placed *La Venida del Mesias* on the **Index of Prohibited Books** (*Index Librorum Prohibitorum*). The Spanish Inquisition had already prohibited it on January 15, 1819.

The primary reason was Lacunza's claim that the Catholic Church itself would apostatize --- placing the official church on the wrong side in the final struggle. The Congregation for the Doctrine of the Faith's deliberations, which lasted over two years, addressed his millenarianism, his "Judeophilia," and his "polemical representations of Rome as Babylon."

The Vatican's broader rejection of millenarianism was made explicit in a **decree of the Holy Office dated July 21, 1944**, approved by Pope Pius XII:

> *"Postremis hisce temporibus non semel ab hac Suprema S. Congregatione S. Officii quaesitum est, quid sentiendum de systemate Millenarismi mitigati, docentis scilicet Christum Dominum ante finale iudicium, sive praevia sive non praevia plurium iustorum resurrectione, visibiliter in hanc terram regnandi causa esse venturum."*
>
> *"Re igitur examini subiecta... respondendum decreverunt:*
>
> ***Systema Millenarismi mitigati tuto doceri non posse."***
>
> "In recent times, on several occasions, this Supreme Sacred Congregation of the Holy Office has been asked what must be thought of the system of mitigated Millenarianism, which teaches, namely, that Christ the Lord before the final judgment, whether or not preceded by the resurrection of the many just, will come visibly to this earth to reign.
>
> "The matter therefore having been submitted to examination... they decreed the response:
>
> **The system of mitigated Millenarianism cannot be safely taught."**
> --- Holy Office decree, July 21, 1944; *Acta Apostolicae Sedis* 36 (1944), p. 212; Denzinger-Schonmetzer 3839

This decree --- which remains in force --- condemns precisely the eschatological system Lacunza had advocated. It is notable that no corresponding Vatican decree addresses futurism or historicism as interpretive methods. The Vatican has never formally chosen between them.

### Irving's Translation: The Bridge to Protestantism

In 1826, Edward Irving discovered Lacunza's Spanish text and was so captivated that he studied Castilian for the sole purpose of translating it. He published *The Coming of Messiah in Glory and Majesty* in 1827, adding a 203-page **Preliminary Discourse** that shaped how English-speaking Protestants received Lacunza's ideas.

Irving's preface was more radical than Lacunza's text. He applied the coming judgment explicitly to the Protestant churches:

> "This body of baptized men, which I call the Gentile church... standeth threatened in the Holy Scriptures because of its hypocrisies, idolatries, superstitions, infidelity, and enormous wickedness, 'because it hath transgressed the laws, changed the ordinance, and broken the everlasting covenant.'"
> --- Irving, Preliminary Discourse to *The Coming of Messiah* (1827), Vol. I, pp. v-vi, citing Isaiah 24:5

> "These judgments upon the Gentile nations... he will finish by His own personal appearance in flaming fire."
> --- Irving, Preliminary Discourse, Vol. I, p. vi

Irving's translation was the critical bridge. Without it, Lacunza's work would have remained confined to Catholic and Spanish-speaking circles. With it, futurism entered the mainstream of British evangelical thought --- and from there, through the Albury conferences and Darby's systematization, it would eventually displace the historicism that had been the standard Protestant position for three centuries.

Both Irving's 1827 English translation and the original 1812 Spanish edition are digitized and freely available on the Internet Archive.

## The Albury Park Conferences (1826--1830)

In 1826, Henry Drummond (1786--1860), a wealthy banker and member of Parliament, invited Irving and approximately twenty other clergy and laymen to his estate at Albury Park, Surrey, for a series of prophetic conferences. These gatherings continued annually through 1830.

The Albury conferences established a six-point premillennial platform:

1. The present dispensation will end in judgment, not universal conversion
2. The Second Advent **precedes** the millennium
3. The Jews will be restored to their own land
4. The judgments poured out on Christendom are those of the apocalyptic vials and trumpets
5. The present Christian dispensation has failed, as previous dispensations did
6. The times of the Gentiles are near their close

These conclusions, published through the journal *The Morning Watch* (1829--1833), helped normalize premillennialism among educated evangelicals and provided the intellectual seedbed from which John Nelson Darby would later develop dispensationalism.

> **Note on influence:** The Albury conferences are sometimes credited as the origin of the pre-tribulation rapture doctrine. The relationship is more complex than either "Irving invented it" or "Darby developed it independently" allows. What is now primary-source-verifiable is that (a) the Albury circle revived premillennialism and, through Irving's translation of Lacunza, brought futurism into English-speaking Protestantism; (b) *The Morning Watch* (the circle's journal) by 1829 published proto-two-stage material and by 1833 published fully proto-pretribulational sequencing; (c) Robert Baxter's 1833 hostile-witness *Narrative of Facts* documents mature pretribulational doctrine — saints caught up before "days of vengeance," Enoch/Elijah/Noah/Lot typology, Holy-Spirit-and-church removed as restrainer — being taught in the Irvingite meetings by 1832; and (d) J. N. Darby himself, on his own writings, was historicist and post-tribulational in the early 1830s, with his mature pretribulational teaching not appearing until the late 1840s. Whether Darby's mature scheme borrowed directly from the Irvingite circle or arrived independently through common sources (Bernard Lambert per Stunt; Lacunza per Ice) remains genuinely contested. See §"The Macdonald question" and §"Did the Irvingite circle publish pre-trib doctrine before Darby?" below.

## The Tongues Outbreak (1830--1831)

While the Albury conferences addressed eschatology intellectually, events in Scotland were about to connect prophecy with spiritual experience.

### Scotland (1830)

In late March 1830, **Mary Campbell**, a young woman living on the Garloch in western Scotland, spoke in an unknown language during a prayer meeting. Within days, **Margaret Macdonald** of Port Glasgow received what was described as a word of prophecy. Her brother James Macdonald also manifested spiritual gifts.

On April 20, 1830, **John Bate Cardale** --- a London solicitor who would later become the first apostle of the Catholic Apostolic Church --- visited Port Glasgow to investigate. He reported his findings to *The Morning Watch* in August 1830.

Irving received the news with joy:

> "I did rejoice with great joy when the tidings were read to me, coming through a most authentic channel, that the bridal attire and jewels of the Church had been found again."
> --- Irving, "Facts Connected with Recent Manifestations of Spiritual Gifts," *Fraser's Magazine* 4 (January 1832)

#### The Macdonald question: did the pre-trib rapture begin here?

Because Margaret Macdonald's 1830 utterance sits at this point in the story, the origins dispute belongs here, both sides at strength.

**MacPherson's case** (Dave MacPherson, *The Incredible Cover-Up*, 1975; *The Rapture Plot*, 1994): Macdonald's prophecy "dramatically separated the sign of the Son of man from the coming of the Son of man," and "she equated the sign with the Rapture --- a Rapture that would occur before the revealing of Antichrist" (as quoted in Ice, BSac 147 [1990]: 158-59). On his reading, her vision is the seed a two-stage coming grew from, transmitted through the Irving circle to Darby. MacPherson's own primary-source anchor is a direct quotation from Samuel Prideaux Tregelles --- a Brethren biblical scholar writing as a first-generation contemporary (Tregelles was seventeen in 1830 and moved in Brethren circles from the mid-1830s) --- who states: *"I am not aware that there was any definite teaching that there would be a secret rapture of the Church at a secret coming, until this was given forth as an 'utterance' in Mr. Irving's Church, from what was there received as being the voice of the Spirit"* (MacPherson, *The Incredible Cover-Up*, 25 and 29, quoting Tregelles). The full textual chain reaches back through Robert Norton, *Memoirs of James & George Macdonald, of Port-Glasgow* (1840), pp. 171-176 --- Norton is the source who first identifies "Miss M. M." as Margaret Macdonald and prints her vision (MacPherson, 55-56).

**An independent first-generation Catholic Apostolic account further tests the origins claim.** Rev. W. W. Andrews, writing in *Bibliotheca Sacra* in 1866 as a sympathetic insider to the Catholic Apostolic movement — the very church that grew directly out of the 1830 Scotland and 1831 London events — summarizes the doctrinal content of the 1830-1832 prophetic utterances this way: "The burden of the prophetic utterances was the speedy coming of the Lord to set up his kingdom in the earth, the judgments about to fall upon Christendom, the sorrow of God over his scattered and desolate flock, his love which still lingered and longed to save, the humiliation and glory of Christ, and the necessity of a work of recovery and rebuilding in the church to prepare his way" (Andrews, "The Catholic Apostolic Church," BSac 23:89 [Jan 1866]: 124). Andrews's characterization is thoroughly historicist premillennial — visible second coming, judgments on Christendom, ecclesiological restoration; and he credits Irving with the same theology, saying that "none more powerfully" preached the Lord's coming in the years before 1830 (p. 122). On Andrews's account, the 1830-1832 utterances neither taught nor implied a two-stage coming or a secret pretribulation rapture; the coming they proclaimed was the visible, kingdom-establishing return of Christ in glory. This is the strongest kind of witness against the MacPherson thesis: not a pre-trib partisan defending Darby's independence, but a Catholic Apostolic insider testifying from within the very tradition MacPherson claims birthed the pre-trib rapture. Andrews also adds one background detail: Mary Campbell (whose 1830 tongues-speaking preceded the Macdonalds') was a sister of Isabella Campbell, whose devotional *Memoirs* had been widely circulated in the 1820s (p. 123) — her family was not obscure revivalist stock but part of the era's Scottish spiritual-writing network.

**Even the leading pre-tribulational defender of Darby's origins acknowledges that Irving and the Albury circle did not hold the doctrine MacPherson attributes to them.** Thomas Ice, executive director of the Pre-Trib Research Center, writes in a 2022 systematic refutation: "While Irving and the Albury group had a few eschatological ideas that were unique, a belief in the pretribulational rapture was not one of those aspects. For one to follow the historicist approach and also believe that the rapture will occur prior to the tribulation is not possible, since historicists believe that the tribulation began hundreds of years ago. What is also true is that Irvingites spoke of a soon coming of Christ to translate believers to heaven, but this view was part of their second coming belief that they could have derived from Manuel Lacunza's writings, which were not the product of futurism at that point. Such a view has similar elements as seen in Robert Gundry's version of posttribulationism" (Ice, "A Refutation of False Origin of the Rapture Theories," *Journal of Dispensational Theology* [JODT] 26:73 [Autumn 2022]: 155-56). On Ice's account, Darby developed pretribulationism "in the process of shifting to futurism" — the interpretive framework change, not any single utterance, is what enabled the innovation. That the Catholic Apostolic insider (Andrews 1866) and the leading modern pre-trib defender (Ice 2022) converge on the same historical judgment — the 1830-1832 charismatic utterances themselves were not proto-pretribulational — is a strong cross-camp convergence of independent testimony from otherwise-opposed traditions. (For context: Margaret Macdonald, 1815-1840, was fifteen years old at the time of her April 1830 utterance; Ice, JODT 26:73 [Autumn 2022]: 150.)

**The rebuttal on Macdonald specifically** (Thomas Ice, "Why the Doctrine of the Pretribulational Rapture Did Not Begin with Margaret Macdonald," BSac 147 [1990]: 155-68): "MacPherson has misinterpreted Macdonald's words by equating her use of 'sign' with a rapture. Rather, she is saying that only those who are spiritual will see the secret sign of the Son of Man that will precede the single, posttribulational second coming of Christ" (pp. 158-59). Notably, scholars with no pre-trib commitments concur on Macdonald's own vision text: Ernest Sandeen calls the Macdonald-origin thesis "a groundless and pernicious charge," since "neither Irving nor any member of the Albury group advocated any doctrine resembling the secret rapture" (quoted at p. 162, with similar judgments from F. F. Bruce and Timothy Weber); Sweetnam and Gribben reach the same verdict on academic grounds (JETS 52 [2009]: 569-77). Both sides of the modern scholarly debate read Macdonald's vision itself as posttribulational or partial-rapturist in shape --- which is why the question of where Darby's doctrine came from does not settle what Scripture teaches (see the anti-genetic-fallacy note at this appendix's close).

**Scope of the Macdonald rebuttal — important qualification.** The Ice / Sandeen / Bruce / Weber convergence is on **Margaret Macdonald's specific 1830 vision text as printed by Norton in 1840**. It is a narrower claim than "the Irvingite circle did not teach pretribulational doctrine." The distinction matters because the Irvingite output during 1829-1833 is not exhausted by Macdonald's one vision. Andrews 1866, quoted above, is describing the **charismatic utterances** at Scotland and London; his characterization is TRUE for the utterances themselves. Ice 2022 is defending Darby's doctrinal originality against MacPherson's Macdonald-specific charge; his rebuttal is coherent within that scope. But *The Morning Watch* — the Irvingite quarterly journal edited by John Tudor (later the 8th Catholic Apostolic apostle) — was publishing PROSE ARTICLES with proto-two-stage sequencing throughout the same 1829-1833 window, and Robert Baxter's 1833 hostile-witness *Narrative of Facts* documents mature pretribulational doctrine as the STANDING TEACHING of the Irvingite meetings by 1832. Whether those broader primary sources should count as "Irving/Albury holding the doctrine" — and whether they warrant reopening the origins question against the current Ice consensus — is the argument the next sub-section takes up.

#### Did the Irvingite circle publish pre-trib doctrine before Darby?

Since the 1970s a small academic literature has argued that they did. **Mark Patterson and Andrew Walker**, in "'Our Unspeakable Comfort': Irving, Albury, and the Origins of the Pretribulation Rapture," *Fides et Historia* 31 (Winter 1999): 66-81, argue that "the mature articulation of the doctrine [of the pretribulation rapture] in *The Morning Watch*, coupled with historical realities regarding both Edward Irving and John Nelson Darby, suggest that Irving and his circle, not Darby as often supposed, are the true originators of the prophetic premillennial school known as dispensationalism" (Patterson-Walker, quoted in J. Lanier Burns's review, *Bibliotheca Sacra* 157:627 [Jul-Sep 2000]: 363-65). Burns partially rebuts but concedes the chronology Patterson-Walker document: pre-trib teaching in Tudor's Morning Watch article of 1829 versus Darby's crystallization at the Powerscourt Conference in 1833. Burns's concession, in the middle of an otherwise critical review, is significant: "It also leads them to speculate on precisely when Darby held the doctrine (1833, the Powerscourt Conference) as opposed to the Irvingite circle (1829, according to Tudor's article in *The Morning Watch*). If the Irvingite circle's views were not as precise as a 'mature doctrine' of the pretribulation rapture, one may surmise that at least part of the reason was their historicist hermeneutic" (Burns, BSac 157:627: 364).

**The primary evidence Patterson-Walker point to — and which we have verified directly against the original texts on archive.org — comprises three items, all pre-dating Darby's own mature pretribulational writings by 16-20 years.**

**First: "On the Structure of the Apocalypse," attributed to John Tudor**, *The Morning Watch* Vol I (1829): 293-315 (verified via archive.org identifier `morningwatchorq02hendgoog`; the article carries no byline in the OCR itself, but Patterson-Walker 1999 and standard Morning Watch scholarly attribution assign it to Tudor as editor of the journal and author of the identifiable three signed pieces in Vol I; the concluding "JOHN TUDOR" signature appears at the end of the article-cluster the piece belongs to). Tudor reads Revelation with an interpretive move that anticipates the classic pre-trib schema:

> "the church is translated at the end of harvest (xiv. 13), when the second, or ox cherub, should come in; and with her translation the realization of the opening vision (chap. iv.) begins, and there would be an incongruity in representing any of the cherubim otherwise employed at that time."
> --- Tudor, *The Morning Watch* Vol I (1829): 313

Tudor identifies Rev 14:13 as the church's translation event and Rev 4 (the throne scene) as post-translation --- the same reading Scofield and later dispensationalists made central to their systems. Tudor's framework retains historicist elements (1260 years, papal usurper) elsewhere in the same article; his scheme is thus proto-pre-trib within a still-historicist frame, not the mature future-tribulation scheme Darby would systematize. But the interpretive move is present in 1829, in Irving's own journal, under Tudor's editorship. (Patterson's doctoral study of the journal dates the "first explicit mention of a pretribulation rapture" slightly later, to December 1829 --- "On the Interpretation of the Apocalypse," TMW I:574, the second part of the same Tudor series --- while his catalog of the journal's pretribulational loci independently includes the two Vol VII passages this study quotes below, at TMW VII:30 and VII:51: an independent doctoral-level corroboration of the passages selected here. Patterson, *Designing the Last Days*, KCL PhD thesis.)

**Second: *The Morning Watch* Vol VII (1833)** — two articles in this volume contain fully proto-two-stage sequencing (verified via archive.org identifier `morningwatchorq00hendgoog`). Vol VII was published in the year Irving was deposed from the Church of Scotland.

The clearest partial-rapture articulation is in "The Glory of God in the Land of the Living" (Vol VII: 1-37, unsigned in the OCR):

> "After the translation of the saints to that region of heaven, from which the evil angels shall then be cast down; in the midst of that tribulation and woe which shall be wrought upon the earth by Satan; there shall be **two bands of elect men** witnessing for God against the utmost rage of Satan and of Antichrist... 'For then shall be great tribulation, such as was not since the beginning of the world to this time.' The first band of these elect is the foolish virgins [Matt 25]... The second band of elect is the Jewish people..."
> --- "The Glory of God in the Land of the Living," *The Morning Watch* Vol VII (1833): 30-31

The sequence: translation of the saints → tribulation and woe on earth → two bands of elect (foolish virgins, Jewish people) remain during tribulation → Antichrist and followers destroyed at the close → New Jerusalem descends. This is structurally close to partial-rapture pretribulationism.

A parallel article in the same volume, "The Bride, the Lamb's Wife" (pp. 44-51, unsigned in the OCR), articulates the same sequence in a different register — morning-star coming and translation of saints usher in the day of the Lord, during which Antichrist is destroyed and the New Jerusalem descends. (Note: the archive.org OCR of Morning Watch Vol VII systematically misreads printed "5" as "6" on the running headers of the pages within this article range due to a paper/ink artifact; the printed Contents list at the front of the volume corroborates the pp. 44-51 span for this article, since the next article — "On the Prophetical Aspect of all God's Works and Ways" — begins at printed p. 52.)

> "The time of the manifestation of the glory to the world, is the end of that day of the Lord which the morning star and the translation of the saints usher in. During that day, and before its close, Antichrist and his followers shall be destroyed; and at the close, the holy city, the New Jerusalem, shall descend from heaven..."
> --- "The Bride, the Lamb's Wife," *The Morning Watch* Vol VII (1833): 50-51

That two independent Vol VII articles carry the proto-two-stage sequence — one framed as partial-rapture with two elect bands (Glory of God), one framed as morning-star-inaugurates-day-of-the-Lord (Bride, Lamb's Wife) — is stronger evidence than any single article that the Morning Watch by 1833 had settled into a two-stage frame.

**Third and most decisively: Robert Baxter's 1833 hostile-witness *Narrative of Facts, Characterizing the Supernatural Manifestations in Members of Mr. Irving's Congregation***. Baxter, a lawyer, briefly participated in the Irvingite prophetic gifts at Newman Street, London (1831-1832), delivered utterances "in the power," experienced a crisis of conscience, and published this book to warn Christians against the movement. Baxter is thus a **contemporaneous eyewitness from inside the meetings who had by publication turned hostile** --- the highest evidentiary standard. What Baxter reports as the Irvingite teaching (which he initially rejected, briefly embraced under prophetic utterance, then repudiated) is essentially mature pretribulational doctrine:

> "...Christ, and before the setting in upon the world of the 'days of vengeance,' emphatically so called in the Scriptures, the saints would be caught up to heaven like Enoch and Elijah; and would be thus saved from the destruction of this world, as Noah was saved in the ark, and as Lot was saved from Sodom. **This was an opinion I never could entertain**..."
> --- Baxter, *Narrative of Facts* (London: James Nisbet, 1833), p. 17

Baxter goes on to report a prophetic utterance in the meetings that read Matthew 24:40-41 as the rapture: "'This is the translation of the saints, whilst the rest of the world are left in their usual occupations'" (p. 17) --- the classic pre-trib reading of a text historicists and post-tribs read as judgment. And most strikingly, Baxter documents the Irvingite version of the SAME restrainer identification that C. I. Scofield would publish as his own in 1914 (Philadelphia Bible Conference, reprinted BSac 108:429 [Jan 1951]: 113, cited in Chapter 4 pillar 7 of this study):

> "at the end of the three years and a half from the beginning of the prophecy of the witnesses, would God **take away his Spirit and his church altogether from the earth**, by causing his **faithful spiritual church to be caught up to heaven like Elijah**; and the earth being then **without a witness for God**, Satan should take to himself the sovereignty, and stand forth in all hideous power in the person of one man, to receive the worship of all the earth: --- that this in particular and in fulness was the '**man of sin, whom the Lord shall consume with the spirit of his mouth, and destroy with the brightness of his coming**.'"
> --- Baxter, *Narrative of Facts* (London: James Nisbet, 1833), from the chapter reporting the "prophecy of the 1260 days testimony and going up of the saints" (see Baxter's Table of Contents entry "Revelation of translation of the saints"), passage on the utterance's interpretation of the "faithful spiritual church" and the "man of sin"

The doctrinal package Baxter documents in the Irvingite meetings of 1832 --- saints caught up like Enoch/Elijah, saved from "days of vengeance" as Noah from the flood and Lot from Sodom; Matt 24:40-41 as rapture; Spirit-and-church removed together as the restraining agent whose removal frees the man of sin to be revealed --- is the essential shape of mature pretribulationism. The Irvingites had also made a dated prediction: from the utterance received 14 January 1832, Baxter records the formula as "1260 days, and three days and a half," and the movement's expected terminus was 14 July 1835 — three and a half calendar years after the utterance (Baxter's *Narrative*, chapter on the "prophecy of the 1260 days"; the day-count formula and the calendar-year terminus do not precisely reconcile, a tension present in Baxter's own account). When the prediction failed, the movement spiritualized it.

**None of these three primary sources are quoted or engaged in Ice 2022** (verified by a full grep of Ice's JODT article for "Patterson," "Walker," "Fides," "Tudor," "Morning Watch," "Baxter" --- zero occurrences). Ice's rebuttal, published in a defense-of-Darby's-originality register, addresses the Macdonald-specific charge without engaging the broader Morning Watch prose material or Baxter's hostile-witness testimony. This is a real gap in the current pretribulational-defense literature.

#### Darby's own chronology — from his writings

The scholarly response to the Patterson-Walker case has largely turned on chronology. Sweetnam and Gribben, "J. N. Darby and the Irish Origins of Dispensationalism," JETS 52:3 (Sep 2009): 569-577, present the position that pre-trib "was in the air in the 1820s and 1830s among eager students of unfulfilled prophecy" (quoting F. F. Bruce) and that Darby's own mature pre-trib emerged from the Powerscourt conferences (1830-1838) rather than from any single tradition. Their key finding, decisive for the timeline: "This also clearly displayed Darby's historicism, his belief that the events foretold in Revelation and other prophetic Scriptures had and were being fulfilled. **Futurism would come later. His expectation of Christ's return was also post-tribulational --- it would be some time before the pre-wrath and 'secret' Rapture would fully emerge in his thought**" (JETS 52:3 [2009]: 572).

Darby's own writings, accessible in Kelly's Collected Writings (hosted freely at stempublishing.com), confirm Sweetnam-Gribben's chronology and add texture:

- **1829 (Dublin) --- "Reflections upon the Prophetic Inquiry"** (Collected Writings, Vol 2, p. 1): Darby argues AGAINST an unnamed opponent's "two comings" interpretation, treating it as forced reading: "he settles that this means two comings, and the catching up must belong to the latter, and the change does not mean from corruption to incorruption, but merely from a short life to a long one." **In 1829, Darby was not teaching pre-trib. He was arguing against a version of it.**
- **1830 --- "On 'Days' signifying 'Years' in prophetic language"** (Collected Writings, Vol 2, p. 32, letter to editor of *The Christian Herald*): Darby's later editorial footnote when the tract was reprinted admits "the latter fails just in the point which the first part condemns... I have no doubt that the latter part is wrong... Subsequent and far more elaborate papers have brought the truth as to Matthew 14 and the 1260 days into a sufficiently clear light. It may serve to shew historically the progress made in the apprehension of truth." **Darby himself documented that his 1830 view was superseded by later development.**
- **1842 (Geneva) --- "Notes on the Apocalypse gleaned at lectures in Geneva, 1842"** (Collected Writings Vol 5): established prophetic teaching, but the "Rapture of the Saints" essay is still years away.
- **~1849-1850 --- "The Rapture of the Saints and the Character of the Jewish Remnant"** (Collected Writings, Vol 11, p. 118): mature pre-trib in Darby's own voice, opening: "The rapture of the saints to meet the Lord in the air, before His manifestation to the earth, and the existence of a Jewish remnant, in whom the Spirit of God is graciously working, before the Lord manifests Himself to them for their deliverance, is happily attracting the attention of Christians." Uses "rapture" as a technical term 28 times; explicit "before the tribulation"; full church/Israel distinction.

**The gap:** The Irvingite documentation runs from Tudor's 1829 proto-pre-trib reading through Irving's March 1830 translation-as-escape prose to Baxter's 1832-teaching report (published 1833). Darby's own mature pretribulational writing is not before ~1849-1850. That is a gap of **roughly two decades** during which the substantive doctrine circulated in Irvingite prose and preaching, while Darby's own writings show him first arguing against a two-comings interpretation (1829), later admitting error on the 1260 days (1830), and by the mid-1830s still historicist and post-tribulational (per Sweetnam-Gribben). Whether Darby's mature scheme borrowed from Irvingite content he must have encountered — Irvingites attended the early Powerscourt conferences per Sweetnam-Gribben, including **Irving himself in 1831** per Lee's doctoral study (Lee, *The Shaping of John Nelson Darby's Eschatology*, UWTSD PhD, 2010) --- or arrived independently through common sources (Bernard Lambert per Timothy Stunt; Lacunza per Ice) remains genuinely open. The chronology, however, is now primary-source-verifiable.

#### Irving's own development: historicist in 1829, translation-as-escape by March 1830

The evidence supports a distinction the earlier literature has tended to blur — but the line runs through **Irving's own development across 1829-1830**, not (as one might first suppose) between Irving personally and the circle around him. **Irving's last book-length prophetic system**, the *Discourses on Daniel's Vision of the Four Beasts* (London: James Nisbet, 1829, verified via archive.org identifier `churchstaterespo00irviuoft`), was **classic Protestant historicism**: day-year hermeneutic ("time, times, and half a time... becometh 1260 years"), papal antichrist (little horn = Pope), church of Christ = saints under the little horn's mastery for the 1260 years, Justinian → Napoleon calculation — no technical "rapture" language, no two-stage coming.

**But within months, Irving's own Morning Watch prose had moved.** In "Signs of the Times in the Church" (*The Morning Watch* Vol II [March 1830]: 156-158; attributed to Irving by Patterson), Irving takes the Pella typology — the Jerusalem believers whom God "drew out of the city, and saved in the town of Pella" before the destruction — and applies it forward: "there will be a dealing with the Gentile church somewhat analogous to this. We have a promise of escape out of the awful judgment with which the Gentile church is to be consumed." Reading Luke 21:36 ("accounted worthy to escape all these things") with 1 Cor 15 and 1 Thess 4, he writes: "These two passages reveal to us that great mystery of the **translation of the living saints**, by an instantaneous transition, from the state of mortality into the state of immortality; and this, and no other, do I believe to be **the way of our escape**" (verified verbatim against the TMW Vol II text, archive.org identifier `morningwatchorq03hendgoog`). Mark Patterson's doctoral study of the journal concludes that Irving "was, above and before anything else a pretribulational-premillennial theologian" — while noting that Irving's frame remained "a modified historicist perspective" (Patterson, *Designing the Last Days*, KCL PhD thesis, p. 228). In this corpus, historicist chronology and a translation-before-judgment escape are not mutually exclusive: the Irvingite scheme grafted the two-stage escape onto a historicist timeline, which is precisely why Ice's structural objection (historicism is incompatible with a *future-tribulation* rapture) does not settle what the Morning Watch actually printed.

The development was a group phenomenon with Irving at its center: Tudor's 1829 article introduces the church-translated-at-Rev-14 reading; Irving's own March 1830 prose articulates translation-as-escape; the "Glory of God" and "Bride, Lamb's Wife" articles (1833) carry the two-stage sequence; Baxter's report from inside the Newman Street meetings documents the mature Enoch/Elijah/Noah/Lot / Spirit-and-church-removed / man-of-sin-unleashed pattern as standing teaching by 1832 — all in a shared institutional context whose leading interpreters included Tudor (editor), Cardale (later first apostle), Drummond (host of Albury), and Irving himself (until his death in December 1834, before any post-1829 systematic prophetic work reached print).

**The apparent contradiction between Andrews 1866 and Baxter 1833 resolves on chronology and genre, not on persons.** Andrews's characterization matches Irving's preaching and published system through 1829 and the visible-coming emphasis of the charismatic utterances themselves; Baxter and the Morning Watch document what the movement's prose theology and meeting instruction had become by 1830-1832 — with Irving's own journal writing among the earliest witnesses to the shift. Ice's rebuttal, following Andrews, addresses the utterances and Irving's earlier system; it does not engage the Morning Watch prose corpus where the development is documented.

#### The scholarly debate today

Five positions occupy the current literature:

1. **"Ancient / recovered"** (Ryrie, LaHaye, Marotta) --- pre-trib was implicit in the NT and occasional teachers appear pre-Darby (e.g., Morgan Edwards 1788).
2. **"Darby independent"** (Ice, Huebner, Bruce, Sandeen, Weber) --- Darby developed pre-trib himself through fresh biblical study; the Irvingite/Macdonald charge is groundless. Sandeen, Bruce, and Weber wrote before Patterson-Walker's 1999 case and were addressing the Macdonald-specific charge; whether they would maintain their broader rejection in light of the Morning Watch prose material and Baxter's testimony is not directly addressed by their published texts. Ice engages the Macdonald-specific charge in 2022 but his article does not name Patterson-Walker or Baxter (verified by direct grep of the Ice 2022 JODT text).
3. **"Common milieu, common sources"** (Sweetnam-Gribben, Stunt) --- ideas "were in the air"; Bernard Lambert (Dominican Jansenist) influenced both Irving and Darby per Stunt.
4. **"Irvingite origin"** (Patterson-Walker, MacPherson, Tregelles, Reese) --- pre-trib originated in the Irvingite circle and was adopted by Darby.
5. **The position this study now takes:** MacPherson's specific Macdonald thesis is unsustainable — Macdonald's own vision text is not clearly pre-trib. But the broader claim that **mature pretribulational doctrine was circulating in the Irvingite circle 16-20 years before Darby's own mature pretribulational writings** is documented from primary sources (Tudor 1829 Morning Watch, "Bride" 1833 Morning Watch, Baxter 1833 *Narrative*). Whether Darby's mature scheme was CAUSALLY DEPENDENT on the Irvingite content or an INDEPENDENT parallel development from common sources is genuinely open. What is now closed is the popular claim that Darby developed pre-trib in a vacuum: the doctrine was in print and being preached in Irvingite circles years before Darby's own mature prophetic voice.

The transmission history this study documents in Chapter 4 pre-trib pillar 7 (Scofield 1914 → Aldrich 1937 → Chafer 1948 → Walvoord 1957 → Harrison 1958) may therefore extend one link further back: **the restrainer identification --- "the Holy Spirit and the church are the restrainer; when the church is removed the man of sin is unleashed" --- appears in Baxter's 1833 report of Irvingite teaching in essentially the same form Scofield would articulate in 1914.** Whether Scofield's 1914 identification depended on the Darby → Kelly → Scofield chain, or drew independently from the Irvingite prehistory Scofield may have encountered through his own study of dispensational sources, is a further question this study does not attempt to resolve.

### London (1831)

On April 30, 1831, **Mrs. Cardale** spoke in tongues in London --- the first such occurrence in Irving's circle outside Scotland. Through the summer and autumn, manifestations increased.

On **October 16, 1831** (the conventional date --- Oliphant records that witnesses differed among October 16, October 30, and November 13; *Life of Edward Irving*, vol. 2, p. 203), during a public service at Regent Square Church, the worship was interrupted by an outbreak of tongues. Irving permitted the manifestations to continue, arguing that the Spirit must not be quenched:

> "The spiritual gifts and the spiritual office-bearers, as they are enumerated in Scripture... are not accidental and temporary occurrences of a miraculous kind... but substantial and permanent forms of operation."

He described the utterances as:

> "Regularly formed, well proportioned, deeply felt discourse, which evidently wanteth only the ear of him whose native tongue it is to make it a very masterpiece of powerful speech."

### Irving's Theology of Tongues

Irving's defense of glossolalia rested on a distinctive and carefully reasoned theology:

> "It is of the essence of the tongue that it should be unknown; and the definition of it is, 'He that speaketh in a tongue speaketh not unto men, but unto God; for no man understandeth.'"
> --- Irving, "Facts Connected with Recent Manifestations of Spiritual Gifts," *Fraser's Magazine* 4 (January 1832)

> "If it were understood by the speaker or the hearer, it would not serve its end of proving that the speaker is not man, but the Holy Ghost."
> --- Irving, *Fraser's Magazine* 4 (January 1832), the sentence following the previous quote

Irving held that tongues were "foundational to all spiritual gifts" --- "that which is the root and the stem of them all, out of which they all grow, and by which they are all nourished." He connected this to his Christology: if the Holy Spirit empowered Christ's human nature, the same Spirit could empower believers with the same gifts.

This Christological argument --- that Christ took on **fallen** human nature and was kept sinless by the Spirit's power --- would prove his undoing.

## Deposition and Death

The tongues outbreak at Regent Square caused a scandal. The London Presbytery acted against Irving, but not on the grounds one might expect. The formal charge was not about tongues but about **Christology**: Irving's teaching that Christ assumed fallen human nature was ruled heretical.

- **March 1833**: Irving was deposed from the ministry of the Church of Scotland by the Presbytery of Annan.
- **April 5, 1833**: He was constituted as "Angel" (chief pastor) of the Newman Street congregation --- the emerging Catholic Apostolic body.
- **December 7, 1834**: Irving died in Glasgow of tuberculosis, at age forty-two.

He died before the movement he helped birth was fully organized. The twelve apostles would not be formally separated until July 14, 1835 --- seven months after his death. Irving was never one of the twelve apostles. His role was understood by his followers as analogous to John the Baptist: the forerunner, not the founder.

## The Catholic Apostolic Church

### The Twelve Apostles

Between 1832 and 1835, twelve men were "called" to the apostolate through prophetic utterance. The full twelve were formally separated (set apart) at Albury Park on July 14, 1835.

| # | Name | Notes |
|---|------|-------|
| 1 | **John Bate Cardale** (1802--1877) | Solicitor; "Pillar of the Apostles"; called October 31, 1832; compiled the liturgy |
| 2 | **Henry Drummond** (1786--1860) | Banker, MP; hosted Albury conferences |
| 3 | **Spencer Perceval Jr.** (1795--1859) | MP; eldest son of the assassinated Prime Minister |
| 4 | **Nicholas Armstrong** | Called before Irving's death |
| 5 | **Francis Valentine Woodhouse** (1805--1901) | Barrister; called August 13, 1834; last surviving apostle |
| 6 | **Henry King-Church** | Clergyman |
| 7 | **Henry Dalton** | Called after Irving's death (1835) |
| 8 | **John Oliver Tudor** | Called after Irving's death (1835) |
| 9 | **Thomas Carlyle** | Civil servant (not the famous essayist) |
| 10 | **Francis Sitwell** | Called after Irving's death (1835) |
| 11 | **William Dow** | Called after Irving's death (1835) |
| 12 | **Duncan Mackenzie** | Called after Irving's death (1835) |

Six apostles were called during Irving's lifetime; the remaining six after his death.

### Governance and Liturgy

The Catholic Apostolic Church (CAC) developed an elaborate structure modeled on Ephesians 4:11 and the book of Revelation:

- **Apostles**: Supreme governing authority, each allocated to a nation ("tribe")
- **Prophets**: Spoke by direct inspiration
- **Evangelists**: Missionary ministry
- **Angels** (Pastors): Chief pastor of each congregation
- **Elders/Priests**: Twenty-four per congregation (modeled on Revelation 4:4)
- **Deacons**: Seven per congregation

The liturgy, compiled principally by Cardale and published as *The Liturgy and Other Divine Offices of the Church*, drew deliberately from Anglican, Roman Catholic, and Eastern Orthodox traditions. Services featured vestments, candles, incense, and anointing of the sick. The Eucharistic theology affirmed a real mystical presence while rejecting both transubstantiation and consubstantiation.

### The Time of Silence

The CAC held that apostles were divinely appointed by Christ alone through prophetic revelation. There was no human mechanism for appointing successors. When apostles began dying --- Spencer Perceval in 1859, Henry Drummond in 1860 --- no replacements were called.

This was a deliberate theological position: the church believed it was living in the last days, and that the twelve apostles would live to see the Second Coming. When they did not, no adjustment was made.

The last apostle, Francis Valentine Woodhouse, died on **February 3, 1901**. The church entered what it called the "Time of Silence" --- no new ordinations could occur because only apostles could ordain. Existing clergy continued to minister until they themselves died. The last Eucharist was celebrated on **Christmas Day, 1970**. The last priest died in 1971; the last deacon in 1972 (in Sydney, Australia). No active congregations remain today.

### The Hamburg Schism and the New Apostolic Church

In 1863, with the number of living apostles fallen to six, the prophet Heinrich Geyer in Hamburg called new apostles by prophetic utterance. The existing apostles rejected these callings. On January 27, 1863, the Hamburg priest Friedrich Wilhelm Schwartz was excommunicated, and the Hamburg congregation expelled.

The expelled group eventually became the **New Apostolic Church** (NAC), which affirmed ongoing apostolic appointments. The NAC today claims millions of members worldwide --- vastly larger than the original CAC ever was, precisely because it solved the succession problem that destroyed the parent body.

## Irving and Pentecostalism

Irving is sometimes called the "grandfather of Pentecostalism." The label is suggestive but overstates the connection.

### What Irving Proved

Irving demonstrated that charismatic gifts --- particularly glossolalia --- could be sought and experienced in modern churches. He articulated tongues as the "standing sign" of the Spirit's presence and argued that the gifts had never been withdrawn. This conceptual breakthrough was genuine: before Irving, the assumption across nearly all Protestant traditions was that miraculous gifts had ceased with the apostolic age.

### Why the Connection Is Indirect

The **actual theological DNA of Pentecostalism** comes overwhelmingly from the Wesleyan-Holiness tradition:

- **John Wesley** (1703--1791): "Entire sanctification" as a second work of grace
- **John Fletcher** (1729--1785): First to call this second experience "baptism in the Holy Spirit"
- **R.C. Horner** (1890s): Argued Spirit baptism was a *third* work of grace, distinct from sanctification
- **Charles Parham** (1901): Added tongues as the *necessary evidence* of Spirit baptism --- the specifically Pentecostal innovation
- **William Seymour** (1906): Carried this teaching to the Azusa Street Revival

This is a well-documented, institutionally traceable chain. The Irving connection, by contrast, runs through conceptual parallels and possible indirect contacts rather than documented theological transmission.

### The Dowie Link

The strongest case for a substantive Irving--Pentecostalism connection, argued by D. William Faupel in *The Everlasting Gospel* (1996), runs through **John Alexander Dowie** (1847--1907). Dowie founded the Christian Catholic Apostolic Church in 1896 --- a name echoing Irving's Catholic Apostolic Church. He attended the same university as Irving (Edinburgh), and many early Pentecostal leaders came out of Dowie's community in Zion, Illinois. However, whether Dowie was *consciously* influenced by Irving or merely arrived at similar conclusions independently is not definitively established.

### Key Differences

| Feature | Irving's Movement | Pentecostalism |
|---------|------------------|----------------|
| **Ecclesiology** | Hierarchical, sacramental, liturgical | Congregational, populist, anti-liturgical |
| **Social class** | London elite, MPs, intellectuals | Working-class, racially integrated |
| **Tongues theology** | "Standing sign" of Spirit's presence | "Initial evidence" of Spirit baptism (Parham's innovation) |
| **Christology** | Christ took fallen human nature, kept sinless by Spirit | No parallel doctrine |
| **Durability of gifts** | Tongues ceased within the CAC before 1878 | Sustained emphasis on ongoing experience |
| **Goal** | Restore full apostolic *structure* (offices, sacraments) | Restore apostolic *experience* (power, gifts, signs) |

As one scholar observed: "For the CAC, the sign was the restored Apostolate, determined by prophecy. For Pentecostals, it was the restored prophecy which determined apostolicity."

### The Scholarly Consensus

| Scholar | Work | Assessment |
|---------|------|-----------|
| **Vinson Synan** | *The Holiness-Pentecostal Tradition* (1997) | Irving is one of three British precursors; "was not successful in his quest" |
| **Gordon Strachan** | *The Pentecostal Theology of Edward Irving* (1973) | Irving's theology was more coherent than Pentecostalism's; warns against anachronistic reading |
| **Walter Hollenweger** | *The Pentecostals* (1972) | Treats Irvingites as a footnote, not a primary ancestor |
| **D. William Faupel** | *The Everlasting Gospel* (1996) | Strongest case for connection through Dowie |

The most defensible summary: **Irving proved the concept; the Holiness movement provided the theology; Parham synthesized them; Seymour globalized the result.**

## Irving and the Millerite Movement

Irving is sometimes linked to William Miller (1782--1849) and the Millerite movement that culminated in the "Great Disappointment" of October 22, 1844. The connection is contextual rather than causal.

### Parallel Movements, Shared Sources

Both Irving and Miller belonged to a broader international wave of historicist prophetic interpretation in the early nineteenth century. They read many of the same older authorities --- Sir Isaac Newton, Thomas Newton, George Stanley Faber, Alexander Keith --- and both used the year-day principle (each prophetic day = one literal year).

But their calculations differed significantly:

| Feature | Irving | Miller |
|---------|--------|--------|
| **Primary text** | Daniel 12 (1,260/1,290/1,335 days) | Daniel 8:14 (2,300 days) |
| **Starting point** | AD 533 (Justinian's decree) | 457 BC (decree of Artaxerxes) |
| **Calculated endpoint** | 1792/1793 (1,260 days); ~1864 or 1868 (1,335 days) | 1843/1844 |
| **Method** | Broadly historicist with futurist elements | Strictly historicist |

Irving wrote: "There can be little doubt that the one thousand two hundred and sixty days concluded in the year 1792, and the thirty additional days in the year 1823; we are already entered upon the last days, and the ordinary life of a man will carry many of us to the end of them."

The parallel extends to failed date-setting. Still historicist in method, the Irvingites fixed the terminal points of the 1,260 days by prophetic utterance --- January 14, 1832 to July 14, 1835, with Christ's return expected at the close. Irving, who died in December 1834, "was not alive to witness the British parallel to the Great Disappointment" of the Millerites in 1844; when the date passed, the Irvingites, like the early Adventists after them, spiritualized the failure (Stallard, "Émile Guers: An Early Darbyite Response to Irvingism," CTJ 1 [1997]: 35-36). Guers --- a Genevan pastor and direct disciple of Darby --- published a book-length repudiation of Irvingism as heresy within twenty-five years of the events, evidence that the earliest Continental Darbyites themselves drew a hard line between the two movements (Stallard, CTJ 1 [1997]: 31-46). July 14, 1835, the endpoint of the Irvingite calculation, is also the date on which the twelve Catholic Apostolic apostles were formally separated at Albury (see above).

### No Direct Influence Documented

No source has demonstrated that Miller read Irving or that Irving's translation of Lacunza reached Miller directly. The Seventh-day Adventist tradition recognizes Lacunza as a precursor, but Lacunza's framework was futurist while Miller's was historicist --- a significant methodological difference.

*The Morning Watch*, the journal of the Albury circle (1829--1833), may have circulated in limited American scholarly circles --- copies are held today at Princeton Theological Seminary, Fuller, and other American libraries --- but no evidence of contemporary American readership during its publication has been found.

The scholarly consensus, reflected in the volume *Anglo-American Millennialism, from Milton to the Millerites* (Brill, 2004), treats Irving and Miller as parallel movements within a shared Anglo-Protestant intellectual tradition, not as a case of one causing the other.

## Why Irving Matters for This Study

Edward Irving stands at the hinge point of eschatological history --- and the genealogy traced in this appendix reveals just how improbable that hinge point was.

The story begins with a Jesuit strategy: Ribera (1590) and Alcazar (1614) deployed futurism and preterism as twin weapons to deflect Protestant historicism from the papacy. For two centuries, these remained Catholic tools, ignored by Protestants. Then a defrocked Jesuit exile in Italy (Lacunza) reworked futurism into a system that indicted the very institution it was designed to protect. Then a Scottish Presbyterian in London (Irving) translated that reworked system into English and carried it into the heart of British evangelicalism. Then an Irish Anglican turned Plymouth Brethren leader (Darby) systematized it into dispensationalism. Then American revivalists (Scofield, Blackstone, Moody) popularized it until it became the dominant eschatological framework of twentieth-century Protestantism.

Darby's own account must also be weighed: in his retrospective letter to Professor Tholuck he traces his development not to Albury but to his convalescence readings of 1827: "In my retreat, the 32nd chapter of Isaiah taught me clearly, on God's behalf, that there was still an economy to come... The consciousness of my union with Christ had given me the present heavenly portion of the glory... I saw in that word the coming of Christ to take the church to Himself in glory" (*Letters of J. N. Darby*, vol. 3, Letter 226, pp. 297ff). Showing a historical pathway for futurist concepts does not in itself invalidate the exegetical arguments later advanced for them.

The result: **the futurist method now held by hundreds of millions of evangelical Protestants can be traced --- through a chain of texts and translations --- as far as Irving, back to a sixteenth-century Jesuit commentary on Revelation.** The Ribera-to-Lacunza-to-Irving links are documented textual transmission. The final link --- from Irving's circle to Darby's dispensationalism --- is a matter of shared milieu and continuing historical debate, not documented borrowing (as noted above, whether Darby drew from Albury or arrived at his conclusions independently remains contested). It is a chain worth knowing about; it is not, by itself, an argument --- truth can arrive through unlikely channels, and each thinker in the chain made his own exegetical decisions.

Before Irving, the dominant Protestant position was historicist: the antichrist was the papacy, the prophetic timeline was already unfolding, and believers would endure tribulation before Christ's return. This was the view of Luther, Calvin, the Westminster Confession, Jonathan Edwards, and Isaac Newton.

After Irving --- and through the movements he helped catalyze --- three new developments emerged:

1. **Futurism entered Protestant thought** through his translation of Lacunza, displacing historicism as the dominant framework over the next century.
2. **The pre-tribulation rapture** was circulating in the Irvingite circle by 1830-1832 — Irving's own translation-as-escape prose in the circle's journal (March 1830), the further Morning Watch articles of 1833, and the standing teaching Baxter documents at the Newman Street meetings — and was crystallized in Darby's mature systematization (~1849-1850); whether Darby's scheme depended on the Irvingite content or arose independently remains genuinely open.
3. **The charismatic renewal** began with the tongues outbreak in his congregation, planting seeds that would eventually produce Pentecostalism, the largest Protestant movement of the twentieth century.

Yet Irving himself held none of these positions in the mature forms they later took. He was a historicist who translated a futurist. He helped lead the conferences that seeded pre-tribulationism, and by March 1830 his own journal prose was teaching the translation of the saints as "the way of our escape" — an escape grafted onto a historicist timeline, not Darby's future-tribulation scheme, which reached its mature form only after Irving's death. He ignited a tongues movement whose theology differed fundamentally from later Pentecostalism.

The irony is profound: the man whose work did more than perhaps anyone else's to reshape modern eschatology began closer to the ante-Nicene fathers than to the movements that claim him. His 1829 *Discourses on Daniel* expected the antichrist (on the historicist reading, already reigning) before the gathering, and believers enduring under him; within a year his own journal was teaching the translation of the living saints as the way of escape from the coming judgment. The man moved with the movement he made.

Irving's story is both an inspiration and a warning. An inspiration, because genuine spiritual hunger and rigorous biblical study can recover truths long forgotten. A warning, because the movements those truths generate may carry them in directions their discoverer never intended --- and because no amount of spiritual gifting insulates a movement from the consequences of getting its ecclesiology wrong.

The Catholic Apostolic Church, built on the conviction that twelve living apostles would see the Lord's return, died when the last of them did not. Its buildings stand empty. Its liturgy is unsung. Its prophecy fell silent.

The question Irving's story presses upon every generation is the one Paul pressed upon the Thessalonians: *What are you building, and will it stand?*

## Modern Popular Reception: the "1,800 Years" Argument

The historicist reception of the story summarized above --- that Darby's pre-tribulation rapture is a *novel* teaching that departs from what the church held for eighteen centuries --- persists as a popular argument today. It surfaces in three independent modern texts, each attributing it to a shared source:

**Alexander Reese**, *The Approaching Advent of Christ* (Marshall, Morgan & Scott, 1937), gave the argument its twentieth-century locus classicus: the "any moment" view of Christ's return "only originated about 1830, when Darby gave forth at the same time the mistaken theory of the Secret Coming and Rapture; but all down the centuries there had existed Christians who longed for the Revelation of Christ, whilst expecting that Antichrist would come first" --- and Reese pressed the corollary: "Only in 1830 did a school arise that treats with intolerance, and often with contempt, the attitude of those who had looked for Him in the manner just named. Not the slightest respect was paid to a view that had held the field for 1,800 years" (Reese, quoted in MacPherson, *The Incredible Cover-Up*, 27). Reese is not, however, the argument's originator: the "eighteen centuries" framing appears earlier in nineteenth-century post-trib and Brethren literature, notably Samuel P. Tregelles, *The Hope of Christ's Second Coming* (1864), and continues through Nathaniel West's 1878 Prophetic Conference essays and Robert Cameron, *Scriptural Truth about the Lord's Return* (1922). Reese's 1937 statement is the form that later authors most frequently reproduce.

**Marvin Rosenthal**, opening his pre-wrath treatment, credits Reese as "the classic defense of posttribulationism" (*The Pre-Wrath Rapture of the Church* [Thomas Nelson, 1990], 59) --- a signal that even a scholar arguing against strict post-trib recognizes Reese's book as the standard historical-argument text.

**David Bercot**, in his lecture *Dispensationalism – Part 1* (Divine Secrets Lesson 7, thehistoricfaith.com, retrieved 2026-08-06), reframes the same argument for a popular pre-Nicene-scholarly audience: "The father of dispensationalism was J. N. Darby... For 1,800 years, virtually all Christians recognized that there were different covenants and time periods in which God worked with mankind... They all believe that Christ would return in glory, and at that time the saints would be resurrected, those living would be caught up in the air with Christ, and this would be immediately followed by Judgment Day. But J. N. Darby had a novel view of things."

That the same "1,800 years" argument runs through Reese (1937), MacPherson (1975), Rosenthal (1990), and Bercot (a modern popular teacher in the Anabaptist / pre-Nicene-scholarly orbit) suggests it has become the standard historicist response to pre-tribulation, transmitted for nearly a century across otherwise divergent scholarly camps.

**The argument runs earlier still.** Henry C. Thiessen, reviewing John J. Scruby's *The Great Tribulation: The Church's Supreme Test* in *Bibliotheca Sacra* 91:363 (July 1934): 376-77, records Scruby's charge that the pre-trib rapture "originated with a 'Satan-deluded' woman in one of the Irvingite meetings, about a hundred years ago" and had been "spread by J. N. Darby of Ireland and his followers" — three years before Reese's 1937 monograph and forty-one years before MacPherson's 1975 *Incredible Cover-Up*. Scruby was a Dayton, Ohio lay Bible teacher who had held premillennial views for forty-five years (since ~1889), and Thiessen (Dallas Theological Seminary systematic theologian) responds with a standard pre-trib defense package — Rev 3:10 removal-reading, Dan 9 seventieth week as Israel's future, the Lot-in-Sodom analogy, imminent return. Reese remains the *canonical twentieth-century form* of the historicist argument, but the family runs earlier and had already reached American lay Bible-teaching circles by the interwar period.

**And the premise of the "1,800 years" argument — that the pre-two-stage reading of these texts was the received academic reading before dispensationalism — is directly attested in mid-nineteenth-century American academic exegesis.** Calvin Ellis Stowe's seventy-three-page 1850 article "The Eschatology of Christ, With Special Reference to the Discourse in Matt. 24 and 25" in *Bibliotheca Sacra* (BSac 7:27 [Jul 1850]: 452ff) contains no mention of Irving, Darby, rapture, antichrist, pre-tribulation, or any two-stage coming. Stowe — Lane Seminary, husband of Harriet Beecher Stowe, and a leading American Congregationalist biblical scholar — reads Matt 24:29-31 as one continuous eschatological narrative from tribulation to Son-of-man coming to trumpet to gathering of the elect. His own list of cross-references for Matt 24:31 explicitly names "1 Thess. 4:15–17" alongside "1 Cor. 15:51, 52" and "2 Thess. 1:7-10" (Stowe, p. 457) — that is, the post-tribulation gathering of the elect and the "meeting the Lord in the air" are on Stowe's page the same event. This is 1850 mainstream American academic exegesis — twenty-five years before Darby's system reached serious American traction, eighty-seven years before Reese.

**A survey of mid-nineteenth-century BSac writers on the same texts confirms the pre-dispensational American academic diversity.** Between 1850 and 1872, *Bibliotheca Sacra* published academic treatments of the Trumpet-relevant passages that split on how to identify the man of sin — Stowe 1850 on unified Olivet sequence; "Rev. Joseph" on 2 Thess 2:4 as fulfilled in the papal ceremonial ("Paganized Ecclesiasticism, the Chief Antagonist of the Modern Missionary," BSac 11:44 [Oct 1854]: 801ff, applying Paul's words verbatim to the pope on his throne receiving prostrations); and Henry Cowles on 2 Thess 2:3-9 as future individual-personal antichrist ("On 'The Man of Sin,' 2 Thess. 2:3-9," BSac 29:116 [Oct 1872]: 623ff). These three articles diverge substantially — realized-historicist papacy (Joseph), unified-Olivet (Stowe), individual-futurist antichrist (Cowles) — but *none takes a two-stage or pretribulational rapture reading.* The dispensational vocabulary is absent from all three. Pre-trib was not among the American academic options on offer for these texts at mid-century.

**One extension the study makes.** The 1,800-years narrative, as Bercot transmits it, starts the origin story at Darby (1830s) and moves directly to Blackstone (1891). It does not engage the pre-Darby Jesuit-and-Irving chain this appendix documents: Ribera (1590) → Alcazar (1614) → Lacunza (~1790, English 1827) → Irving's *Preliminary Discourse* (1827) → the Irvingite circle's own *Morning Watch* (1829-1833) and the doctrine Baxter reports as its standing teaching by 1832. The 1,800-years point survives the extension in modified form: Ribera to the Irvingite circle is still less than three centuries of any *proto*-pretribulational thought, and Ribera and Alcazar do not teach a two-stage rapture. But **Lacunza's 1827 English text contains the seed** ("when the Lord returns from heaven to earth, upon his coming forth from heaven, and much before his arrival at the earth, he will give his orders... [followed by the saints' ascent and a 'great and universal deluge of fire' on earth before Christ 'arrives at the earth, with all his company']"), and the Irvingite output during 1829-1833 gave the seed its first mature articulation. The framing that presents Darby as having *imagined* a special understanding of Scripture understates the layered scholarly history that fed his systematization; but so does the framing that treats the Irvingite prehistory as absent. This study neither weakens the historicist argument nor strengthens the dispensationalist reply by making the fuller genealogy visible; it merely trusts the reader to weigh the full chain.
