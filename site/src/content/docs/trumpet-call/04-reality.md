## The Views in Practice

This chapter presents the major views on whether the gathering of believers or the antichrist comes first, as their best advocates would present them. Because this question has been answered differently across Christian traditions, we include voices from dispensationalist, Reformed, Baptist, Catholic, Orthodox, Adventist, and Anabaptist communities. We examine pastoral fruit and honest challenges for each.

### Pre-Tribulation Rapture

**Held by:** Conservative evangelical churches, particularly in Baptist, Pentecostal, non-denominational, and broader evangelical churches in the United States. Popularized by dispensationalist teachers: John Nelson Darby, C.I. Scofield, Tim LaHaye, John Walvoord, Chuck Smith, John MacArthur.

**Core claim:** The rapture is a separate event from Christ's second coming. Believers will be caught up to meet the Lord in the air (1 Thessalonians 4:17) before the seven-year tribulation begins. This is distinct from Christ's return in glory at the end of tribulation.

**The ordering question:** **Gathering first, then antichrist.** This is the only major view that places the gathering of believers *before* the antichrist's appearance.

**In their own words:**

John Nelson Darby (systematizer of the view):
> "It is this passage which, twenty years ago, made me understand the rapture of the saints before --- perhaps a considerable time before --- the day of the Lord, that is, before the judgment of the living."
> --- J. N. Darby, "Short but Serious Examination" of Gaussen's *Daniel the Prophet* (c. 1850), *Collected Writings*, vol. 11 (Prophetic 4), from p. 63; quoted in William Kelly, "The Rapture of the Saints," *Bible Treasury*, New Series, vol. 4 (1903): 314-18

John MacArthur:
> "The second coming must be distinguished from the rapture of the church prior to the seven-year tribulation."
> --- John MacArthur, *Because the Time Is Near* (Moody, 2007), p. 287, opening the "Christ's Glorious Return (19:11-21)" section (verified verbatim via Perlego, 2026-08-10)

**The strongest case:**

1. **Imminent return:** Believers are instructed to look directly for Christ's appearing --- a signless expectation --- rather than for intermediate signs such as the antichrist: "Looking for that blessed hope, and the glorious appearing of the great God and our Saviour Jesus Christ" (Titus 2:13); "to wait for his Son from heaven" (1 Thessalonians 1:10). If antecedent events had to occur first, the watching commands would point at those events instead of at Christ himself. (The developed case: Brindle, "Biblical Evidence for the Imminence of the Rapture," BSac 158 [2001]: 138-51 --- his passages include Titus 2:13, 1 Thess 1:10, John 14:1-3, 1 Cor 1:7, 15:51-52; Stanton, *Kept from the Hour* [1956], ch. 6.)

2. **Promise of escape from wrath:** "For God hath not appointed us to wrath, but to obtain salvation by our Lord Jesus Christ" (1 Thessalonians 5:9). The church is promised salvation, not tribulation.

3. **Israel and the church are distinct:** The tribulation in Revelation focuses on Israel (the 144,000, the two witnesses), not the church. God is working with Israel separately from the church during this period. In its classic form: ἐκκλησία (church) appears nineteen times in Revelation 1-3 and never in the tribulation narrative of Revelation 4-18; Walvoord identified the twenty-four elders of Revelation 4-5 as the church already in heaven.

4. **The "catching away" in Revelation 3:10:** Jesus promises to keep the Philadelphia church "from the hour of temptation, which shall come upon all the world." This suggests escape. (Pre-trib exegetes themselves differ on how much weight the verse bears: Townsend argues *tērēsō ek* promises preservation *outside* the hour [BSac 137 (1980): 252-53], while Svigel concludes "the key element missing from Rev 3:10 is the Rapture itself," relegating the verse to "secondary significance or corroborative evidence" and proposing Revelation 12:5 as the Apocalypse's actual rapture text [TrinJ 22 (2001): 28, 56-74].)

5. **Linguistic arguments:** The Greek term "harpazō" (caught up) in 1 Thessalonians 4:17 suggests a sudden removal. The trumpet in 1 Corinthians 15:52 indicates a gathering, not a descent to earth.

6. **The Day of the Lord opens with the church already gathered:** The pre-trib answer to the 2 Thessalonians 2:3 sequence: "that day" is the extended Day of the Lord (encompassing the seventieth week), and the apostasy and man of sin are its *opening contents*, not signs the church must first witness --- Paul's point to the shaken Thessalonians is that the Day cannot be present because its contents are absent. "Paul was teaching a pre- or onset day-of-the-Lord rapture... Stated more succinctly, the rapture is pretribulational" (Blaising, "The Day of the Lord and the Rapture," BSac 169 [2012]: 259-70; W. K. Harrison, BSac 115 [1958], corroborating).

7. **The restrainer removed before the man of sin (2 Thessalonians 2:6-8):** If "he who now letteth" is the Holy Spirit indwelling the church — the standard dispensational reading systematized in the mid-twentieth century — then the restrainer's removal "out of the way" *before* the Wicked is revealed gives pre-trib an explicit sequence text: the indwelt church is taken, then the antichrist appears. The identification's earliest primary-source documentation in the Irvingite circle is Baxter's 1833 *Narrative of Facts*, reporting a 1832 utterance in Mr. Irving's congregation that "...would God take away his Spirit and his church altogether from the earth, by causing his faithful spiritual church to be caught up to heaven like Elijah; and the earth being then without a witness for God, Satan should take to himself the sovereignty, and stand forth in all hideous power in the person of one man" (Baxter, *Narrative of Facts* [1833]; see Appendix F for full context and the question of causal relationship to Darby's later scheme). The primary-source articulation for the twentieth-century American dispensational stream is C. I. Scofield's 1914 Philadelphia Bible Conference lecture, reprinted in *Bibliotheca Sacra* in 1951: "Where shall we look for some being who was in the earth in Paul's day, who is in the world at present and who is powerful enough to restrain — keep from its worst excesses — this evil mystery of iniquity? My friends, there is but one; and He is the Holy Spirit... The Holy Spirit of God baptizes every believer into the body of Christ (1 Cor 12:12) and dwells in every believer (1 Cor 6:18-19), and the presence of the Christian church in this mass of worldliness... has been — and is even yet — that which restrains and has restrained this mystery of iniquity. The man of sin... cannot be manifested until the professing church becomes largely apostate, and the Holy Spirit (considered as indwelling the real believers constituting the true church, the body of Christ) will be taken out of the way. 'Then shall that Wicked be revealed.' It is by no means meant that the Spirit of God departs: **it is the Spirit-filled church which goes**" (Scofield, "The Course and End of the Age, Part 3," BSac 108:429 [Jan 1951]: 113, from the 1914 Philadelphia lectures). The Scofield identification became the standard dispensational teaching by the mid-1930s. Willard Maxwell Aldrich, in *Bibliotheca Sacra* 94:374 (April 1937), articulated Scofield's line with an added grammatical refinement — reading the neuter and masculine participles of 2 Thess 2:6-7 as two referents ("an agency and an agent are in view, i.e., the Holy Spirit working through the agency of the church," Aldrich, BSac 94:374 [Apr 1937]: 229) — and quoting Arno C. Gaebelein (editor of *Our Hope* magazine and Scofield's collaborator on the Reference Bible) as the authoritative statement of the identification:

> "It is the Holy Spirit of God. When the church leaves the earth then this restraining power and person, who dwells in the church and therefore is here on earth, will be taken out of the way... The Holy Spirit, who came down from heaven on the day of Pentecost to form the church, the body of Christ, will be withdrawn when that body is complete and taken to glory to be joined to the Head, the Lord Jesus Christ."
> --- Gaebelein, *The Annotated Bible*, Philippians-Hebrews vol., p. 137, quoted in Aldrich, BSac 94:374 [Apr 1937]: 229-30

Aldrich also cites Rollin Thomas Chafer's classroom lectures on the mysteries throughout the article — R. T. Chafer, managing editor of *Bibliotheca Sacra*, was a brother of Lewis Sperry Chafer (DTS founder and author of the *Systematic Theology* cited below); both Chafers taught the mysteries in the DTS curriculum by 1937 — indicating that the identification was standard Dallas Theological Seminary teaching well before its formal codification. The doctrine reached its mid-twentieth-century systematizers next: L. S. Chafer's *Systematic Theology* (1948), John F. Walvoord's *The Rapture Question* (1957), and W. K. Harrison's *Bibliotheca Sacra* article (115 [1958]: 22-23) all articulate the Scofield line in progressively more developed form. The documented tradition of transmission — a dispensational tradition of teaching within a shared institutional milieu, not a claim of strict linear literary dependence at every step — runs from Scofield's 1914 Philadelphia Bible Conference lectures (taught orally that year; not reprinted in *Bibliotheca Sacra* until 1951), through Scofield's collaborator Arno C. Gaebelein (formulation in the *Annotated Bible* commentary on 2 Thessalonians 2 and in *Our Hope* magazine, transmitted to Scofield's circle via personal collaboration on the Scofield Reference Bible rather than through the 1914 text itself), to Aldrich 1937, L. S. Chafer 1948, Walvoord 1957, and Harrison 1958. This documented dispensational convergence structurally mirrors the historicist "1,800 years" transmission history in Appendix F, though the surviving evidence establishes doctrinal continuity within a shared teaching environment rather than direct author-to-author borrowing at every arrow. This may be the view's most textually direct argument --- and its hinge is the identification itself, on which "more than a dozen views" exist (Powell, BSac 154 [1997]: 320; see Chapter 1's restrainer survey, including Dixon's evil-restraint reading, JETS 33 [1990]: 445-49).

**Pastoral fruit:**

- **Comfort in times of trouble:** Believers can face present persecution with hope, knowing they will be delivered
- **Urgency for evangelism:** The "any moment" return creates urgency for mission
- **Practical holiness:** The expectation of Christ's return motivates holy living
- **Assurance of deliverance:** Knowing we won't face God's wrath can be deeply comforting

**Honest challenges:**

- **No explicit "church removed before tribulation" text:** Critics argue this is an inference, not an explicit teaching — a point pre-trib scholars themselves grant: "Pretribulationists commonly admit that the Scriptures include no explicit statement on the timing of the rapture... As John Walvoord often noted, this is a matter of inference" (Blaising, BSac 169 [2012]: 259 n. 1). The concession has a documented history in the pre-trib literature. Scofield in his 1914 Philadelphia lecture asserted as historical background: "The Thessalonians had been taught that the church would be caught up before the day of the Lord; but now it seemed that this terrible day had come upon them" (BSac 108:429 [Jan 1951]: 111-12) — presenting the pre-trib rapture-before-day-of-the-Lord doctrine as prior *teaching* Paul is defending, and adding rhetorically, "If this passage does not mean that, it does not mean anything." Ninety-eight years later Blaising grants what Scofield's rhetoric refused: no explicit text teaches the timing; the doctrine is an inference the pre-trib exegete brings to the passage. This is a difference in confidence between two influential pre-trib formulations — Scofield's assertive early framing and Blaising's conceding modern one.
- **The restrainer's identity is textually indeterminate on older readings:** the pre-trib pillar 7 argument depends on identifying the restrainer as the Holy Spirit indwelling the church, but the older mainstream exegetical tradition is explicitly agnostic. Augustine, *City of God* 20.19: "I frankly confess I do not know what he means." Henry Cowles, in an 1872 *Bibliotheca Sacra* treatment of 2 Thess 2, wrote: "having no revelation on the subject, I do not know. To what secondary agency, if any, Paul had special reference, he has not told us, and I have no knowledge... So far as this prophecy of Paul is concerned, we are left in absolute ignorance" (Cowles, BSac 29:116 [Oct 1872]: 630). Two major exegetes fourteen hundred years apart agree the restrainer's identity is textually indeterminate — the confidence Scofield's identification requires is not, on their read, warranted by the text. Powell's "more than a dozen views" (BSac 154 [1997]: 320) documents the same spectrum in modern scholarship.
- **Pre-Darby "rapture" language in 17th-century Puritan sources is pre-conflagration, not pre-tribulation:** Pre-trib historical defenses (notably William C. Watson, *Dispensationalism Before Darby*, Silverton, OR: Lampion, 2015) advance Puritan-era voices — especially Nathaniel Homes (1599-1678), an English Independent divine — as evidence pre-trib rapture existed before Darby. Homes does write of "the rapture or taking up the saints then alive, to meet the Lord in the clouds," but the language is directly quoted from Joseph Mede, and the purpose Homes and Mede assign to the rapture is *preservation during the conflagration of the earth* — the fire of 2 Peter 3:10, on the Noah-and-the-Ark analogy (Homes, *The Resurrection Revealed*, quoting Mede; digitized on archive.org as `resurrectionreve00homerich`, the conflagration section). Homes writes: "they may be preserved during the conflagration of the earth... as Noah and his family were preserved from the deluge, by being lifted up above the waters in the ark; so should the saints at the conflagration, be lifted up in the clouds, unto their ark Christ, to be preserved there from the deluge of fire, wherein the wicked shall be consumed." This is Mede's / Homes's **pre-conflagration** premillennial frame (removal before the final fire, followed by earthly millennium) — not modern **pre-tribulationism** (removal before a future seven-year tribulation, with the man of sin revealed only after the church is gone). The ruin of Babylon and the raising of the saints are concurrent in the Mede-Homes scheme; the antichrist is already in view. This is the same pattern Gumerlock documents in the earlier removal texts (see above)
- **Early church didn't hold this view:** Pre-Nicene fathers expected to face tribulation. (Pre-trib advocates point to scattered pre-Darby "removal" texts — the *Apocalypse of Elijah* [3rd c.], pseudo-Ephraem, the *Narration of Isaiah* [before 1092], the *History of Brother Dolcino* [1316] — but in each of these, as their modern publisher Gumerlock notes, "the Antichrist is already on the world scene persecuting the saints" when the removal occurs, which differs from modern pretribulationism — Gumerlock, BSac 170 [2013]: 425; BSac 176 [2019]: 87)
- **Revelation's tribulation language is descriptive of all history:** Some argue "tribulation" language applies to all of church history, not a specific future period
- **The ἐκκλησία-absence argument cuts both ways:** "saints" are present throughout Revelation 6-18, and ἐκκλησία is likewise absent from Revelation 19-20, where the church is undisputedly present. G. K. Beale, in the NIGTC Revelation commentary, cites Gundry's classic treatment: "this is an argument from silence which proves nothing, since the word 'church' is also not found with reference to the people of God in heaven, yet, on a typical dispensational futurist reading, the church is purportedly in heaven as a result of a pretribulational rapture (preceding the events of chs. 4ff.); (2) several Gospels and Epistles do not have the word 'church,' yet this is not taken to prove [its absence]" (Beale, *Book of Revelation* [NIGTC, 1999], 186 n. 56, citing Gundry, *Church and Tribulation*, 78)
- **The rapture may be simultaneous with Christ's return:** Many passages describe Christ's coming as singular
- **The futurist interpretive framework has a traceable genealogy:** The method of reading Revelation as primarily future (rather than historically unfolding) was first systematized by Jesuit commentators Francisco Ribera (1590) and Roberto Bellarmine (1581--1593) during the Counter-Reformation, then reworked by the exiled Chilean Jesuit Manuel Lacunza (~1790), then translated into English by Edward Irving (1827), before being systematized by Darby. Whether the Jesuit origins constitute an argument against the method is a question readers must decide --- truth can arrive through unlikely channels --- but the genealogy is documented and worth knowing. See Appendix F ("Edward Irving") for the full textual chain with primary sources.

### Mid-Tribulation Rapture

**Held by:** A smaller group of evangelical scholars. Notable advocates: Norman Harrison, Gleason Archer, J. Oliver Buswell Jr. A refined variant called the "Pre-Wrath Rapture" is held by Marvin Rosenthal and Alan Hultberg.

**Core claim:** The rapture occurs at or after the midpoint of Daniel's 70th week (after 3.5 years), typically connected with the seventh trumpet of Revelation 11. The church endures the "wrath of man" (persecution) but is removed before the "wrath of God" (the bowl judgments). The pre-wrath variant places the rapture between the sixth and seventh seals (see Rosenthal's definition below: the Day of the Lord begins at the seventh seal, and the rapture immediately precedes it).

*Note on the "variant" framing:* Rosenthal and other pre-wrath advocates explicitly reject the description of pre-wrath as a "refined variant" of mid-tribulationism; they locate pre-wrath between mid-trib and post-trib as a distinct position, with different terminus for the Great Tribulation and a different identification of the seventieth-week wrath. This section groups them together for taxonomic economy; the reader should weigh Rosenthal's own words below on their own terms.

**The ordering question:** **Antichrist first (partially), then gathering.** The antichrist appears and persecutes the church during the first half; the church is gathered before God's final wrath is poured out.

**In their own words:**

Norman B. Harrison, *The End: Re-Thinking the Revelation* (Harrison Service, 1941; page pins cited from the 1948 reprint via archive.org identifier `end0000norm`):
> "The Day of Wrath has only now come (11:18). This means that nothing that precedes in the Seals and Trumpets can rightfully be regarded as wrath."
> --- Norman B. Harrison, *The End*, pp. 118-120 (the argument spans these pages)

Harrison also stated the sixth-seal antecedent of the modern pre-wrath position over four decades before Rosenthal:

> "The first intimation of how far we are being carried is in the sixth Seal. (We found the same in the sixth of the Churches: Philadelphia reached to the Rapture --- 3:10, 11.) 'The great day of their wrath is come.' This is the final assertion concerning the Seals. 'Wrath' is a word reserved for the Great Tribulation --- see 'wrath of God' in 14:10, 19; 15:7; 16:1, etc."
> --- Norman B. Harrison, *The End*, p. 97

On the internal exegesis of the mid-trib rapture:

> "To place the Rapture here is to disprove the unity of Scripture. St. Paul, by inspiration of the Spirit, definitely places the Resurrection and the Rapture of the saints through the coming of Christ 'at the last trumpet' (1 Cor. 15:51, 52). This is a specific locating of the event. Unquestionably the Holy Spirit revealed the fact and inspired the recording of it."
> --- Norman B. Harrison, *The End*, p. 80

And against locating the rapture at Revelation 4:1 (the pre-trib pillar):

> "In this scene is added a conclusive proof that the Rapture has not taken place at Rev. 4:1. The Elders are heard saying, not 'us' (as in A. V.) as [if] they included the redeemed, but 'them' as of a company apart from themselves. Moreover, if so great a company of redeemed were present, their failure to appear and join in the New Song is utterly inexplicable."
> --- Norman B. Harrison, *The End*, p. 89

Gleason Archer, *Three Views on the Rapture* (Zondervan, 1984):
> "This interpretation makes a clear division between the first half as the period of the wrath of man, and the second half as the period of the wrath of God... the final generation of the pre-Rapture church will be subjected to the wrath of man, but spared from the wrath of God."
> --- Gleason Archer, *Three Views on the Rapture*, p. 139

J. Oliver Buswell Jr., *Our Hope* (June 1950):
> "I do not believe that the Church will go through any part of that period which the Scripture specifically designates as the wrath of God, but I do believe that the abomination of desolation will be a specific signal for a hasty flight followed by a very brief but a very terrible persecution, and that followed very quickly by the rapture of the Church preceding the outpouring of the vials of the wrath of God."
> --- J. Oliver Buswell Jr., *Our Hope* LVI (June 1950), p. 720 (quoted in Walvoord, "Premillennialism and the Tribulation, Part VIII: Midtribulationism," *Bibliotheca Sacra* 113 [1956]: 102)

Marvin Rosenthal (Pre-Wrath variant), *The Pre-Wrath Rapture of the Church* (Thomas Nelson, 1990), in his own words:

> "The Day of the Lord is the time of divine wrath. It will be recognized as about to begin by the cosmic disturbances associated with the sixth seal (Joel 2:10-11, 30-31; Rev. 6:12-17; cf. Matt. 24:29) and will begin with the opening of the seventh seal (Rev. 8:1). The Rapture of the church will immediately precede the Day of the Lord."
> --- Marvin Rosenthal, *The Pre-Wrath Rapture of the Church*, p. 37

> "In Revelation 6 the first six seals are opened in chronological sequence. After each seal is opened, certain events occur. After the sixth seal is opened, John states that 'the great day of his wrath is come.' There is no justification for making God's wrath retroactive and thus to include the first six seals."
> --- Rosenthal, *Pre-Wrath Rapture*, p. 177

> "The wrath of God is a major topic in the book of Revelation. No less than eight times the word wrath is used. The first occasions are in Revelation 6:16-17 in anticipation of the opening of the seventh seal. It is significant to note that not once is the word wrath used before Revelation 6:16-17 or in describing the six seals. Only with the opening of the seventh seal and beyond is the word wrath mentioned in the book."
> --- Rosenthal, *Pre-Wrath Rapture*, p. 173

For a critical evaluation of Rosenthal's thesis from within the pretribulation camp, see John A. McLean, "Another Look at Rosenthal's 'Pre-Wrath Rapture,'" *Bibliotheca Sacra* 148 (1991): 387-98.

**The strongest case:**

1. **The seventh trumpet (Revelation 11:15-18):** The mid-trib view identifies the "last trump" of 1 Corinthians 15:52 with the seventh trumpet of Revelation 11:15. Harrison argued that "all the elements involved in the Coming are here" (*The End*, p. 117, as quoted in Walvoord, BSac 113 [1956]: 102-3) --- the trumpet sounds, the kingdom is proclaimed, and the dead are judged.

2. **Wrath of man vs. wrath of God:** The first half of the tribulation is human persecution; God's wrath (*orgē*) begins only at the trumpets or bowls. Believers are promised escape from *God's* wrath (1 Thessalonians 5:9), not from *human* persecution.

3. **"The great tribulation" (Matthew 24:21):** Jesus calls the second half (after the abomination of desolation) "the great tribulation." The church endures the first half but is delivered before the worst.

4. **Daniel's midpoint:** Daniel 9:27 describes the antichrist causing sacrifice to cease "in the midst of the week." This midpoint event triggers the church's removal.

**The pre-wrath variant's own case** (in its advocates' terms): Rosenthal's central claim is that "the Day of the Lord wrath will commence immediately after the church is raptured" (*The Pre-Wrath Rapture*, p. 139, as quoted in Fish, EMJ 1 [1992]) --- and that Scripture names events that come *before* that Day: Elijah (Malachi 4:5), the apostasy and the man of sin (2 Thessalonians 2:3), and Joel's cosmic signs --- "The sun shall be turned into darkness, and the moon into blood, before the great and the terrible day of the LORD come" (Joel 2:31). The sixth seal's cosmic disturbances (Revelation 6:12-14) mirror Joel's, and Matthew 24:29 places the same signs "immediately after the tribulation of those days" --- so on this reading the Great Tribulation is cut short, the Day of the Lord occupies the remainder of the seventieth week, and the church endures the antichrist's persecution but is gathered before God's own wrath (1 Thessalonians 5:9; Fish, EMJ 1 [1992], summarizing Rosenthal; the fullest recent statement is Hultberg's chapter in *Three Views on the Rapture*, 2nd ed., Zondervan, 2010).

**Academic grammatical support for the sixth-seal-as-Day-of-the-Lord reading** comes from David Aune's Word Biblical Commentary on Revelation: "The article used with ἡμέρα, 'day,' and with the restrictive attributive adjective μεγάλη, 'great,' indicates that the author is referring to a very specific day, one that is well known to the readers, namely, the Day of the Lord" (Aune, *Revelation 6-16* [WBC 52B, 1998], p. 97). Aune's grammatical point is that the definite-article-plus-restrictive-attributive construction in Rev 6:17 identifies THE Day of the Lord — a technical eschatological term the original readers would recognize. This is a WBC-tier academic voice on the *identification* half of the pre-wrath side's key exegetical move (Rev 6:17 = Day of the Lord). But it is a double-edged verse for pre-wrath: whether the aorist ἦλθεν ("is come") supports Rosenthal's seventh-seal-onset chronology or the pre-trib critics' sixth-seal-onset reading (Stanton, McLean below in the Honest challenges list) is a further step Aune himself does not take. Aune's grammar reinforces the identification pre-wrath needs, but the *timing* his aorist implies is precisely the timing his critics use to challenge Rosenthal's schema.

**Pastoral fruit:**

- **Balance of hope and endurance:** Believers prepare for persecution while trusting God's protection from final wrath
- **Biblical specificity:** Places the rapture within the structural flow of Revelation
- **Distinguishes tribulation from wrath:** A key exegetical distinction (see Appendix A: *thlipsis* vs. *orgē*)

**Honest challenges:**

- **Arbitrary midpoint:** Why would God rapture at exactly the midpoint?
- **The "last trump" identification:** Paul wrote 1 Corinthians c. 55 AD, decades before Revelation (c. 95 AD). Could Paul have been referring to a trumpet sequence not yet revealed? Svigel presses the sharpest published form of this objection: even on a Neronic dating of Revelation, "1 Corinthians, written c. A.D. 55, is still thirteen years too early to draw on the imagery from Revelation," and Revelation 11:15 never calls its trumpet "last" and says nothing of resurrection (TrinJ 22 [2001]: 41-42)
- **The sixth-seal wrath declaration (pre-wrath variant):** Revelation 6:16-17 announces "the great day of his wrath is come" at the *sixth seal* --- before Rosenthal's proposed rapture point. Critics argue his system "is embarrassed, if not refuted" by this, since his response requires reading the aorist "is come" as referring to an event "about to occur" (Stanton, BSac 148 [1991]: 101; McLean, BSac 148 [1991]: 387-98)
- **The Old Testament terminus (pre-wrath variant):** Cole argues the pre-wrath terminus a quo drawn from Joel is "too early for the view," and that the Great Tribulation and the Day of the Lord overlap --- the Antichrist's wrath and God's wrath running concurrently, contrary to the pre-wrath contention that they cannot (DBSJ 9 [2004]: 45)
- **Limited support:** Fewer Bible teachers hold this view than pre-trib or post-trib

### Post-Tribulation Rapture

**Held by:** Historic premillennialists and many evangelical scholars who hold a future tribulation and a single return. Notable advocates: George Eldon Ladd, Robert Gundry, Douglas Moo, Craig Blomberg, John Piper, F.F. Bruce, Millard Erickson; N.T. Wright reaches the same ordering from a new-creation framework. This is called "historic premillennialism" when combined with a future millennial expectation. (Amillennial, Catholic, and Orthodox traditions concur on the ordering --- single return, gathering after tribulation --- without belonging to this camp; they are treated in their own sections below.)

**Core claim:** The rapture occurs at the same time as Christ's visible return at the end of the tribulation. Believers will go through the tribulation but will be preserved through it, then caught up to meet Christ as He returns. There is one return, not two.

**The ordering question:** **Antichrist first, then gathering.** The gathering occurs at Christ's single, visible return, after the antichrist has been revealed and the tribulation has run its course.

**In their own words:**

George Eldon Ladd, *The Blessed Hope* (Eerdmans, 1956):
> "The hope of the Church throughout the early centuries was the second coming of Christ, not a pretribulation rapture. If the Blessed Hope is in fact, a pretribulation rapture, then the Church has never known that hope through most of its history, for the idea... did not appear until the nineteenth century."
> --- George Eldon Ladd, *The Blessed Hope*, p. 31

> "Certainly if one can make anything of language at all, no distinction can be made between the parousia, the apocalypse, and the epiphany of our Lord."
> --- Ladd, *The Blessed Hope*, p. 51

N.T. Wright, *Surprised by Hope* (2008):
> "When Paul speaks of 'meeting the Lord in the air,' the point is precisely not --- as in the popular rapture theology --- that the saved believers would then stay up in the air somewhere, away from earth. The point is that, having gone out to meet their returning Lord, they will escort him royally into his domain, that is, back to the place they have come from."
> --- N.T. Wright, *Surprised by Hope*, p. 133

Robert Gundry, *The Church and the Tribulation* (Zondervan, 1973):
> "That day cannot begin until after the revelation of the Antichrist and the apostasy, after the ministry of Elijah, after the celestial phenomena between the tribulation and the posttribulational advent, in short, not until after the tribulation."
> --- Robert Gundry, *The Church and the Tribulation*

On the *apantēsis* (meeting) language of 1 Thessalonians 4:17:
> "This connotation points toward our rising to meet Christ in order to escort Him immediately back to earth."
> --- Gundry, *The Church and the Tribulation*, p. 104

Charles Hodge (postmillennialist --- quoted for the older Protestant consensus on the sequence), *Systematic Theology*, vol. III:
> "According, then, to the common faith of the Church, the three great events which are to precede the second advent of Christ, are the universal proclamation of the Gospel or the conversion of the Gentile world; the national conversion of the Jews; and the appearance of Antichrist."

Craig Blomberg, in Blomberg and Sung Wook Chung, eds., *A Case for Historic Premillennialism* (Baker, 2009):
> "Without exception every relevant Scripture supports posttribulationism over pretribulationism."
> --- Craig Blomberg, in Blomberg & Chung, eds., *A Case for Historic Premillennialism*, p. 83

G. K. Beale --- whose own commitments are amillennial-idealist rather than post-trib historic premillennial --- describes the closest futurist neighbor to post-trib in his NIGTC commentary this way (*The Book of Revelation*, Eerdmans/Paternoster, 1999):
> "A second version of futurism may be referred to as 'modified futurism.' It does not interpret as literally as the popular view and does not hold as strictly that the visions represent the chronological sequence of future history. In particular, this version can affirm that the church is true Israel and that there will be no 'pretribulation rapture.' Rather, Christians will pass through the final period of trial."
> --- G. K. Beale, *The Book of Revelation* (NIGTC, 1999), p. 115

**The strongest case:**

1. **Christ's return is singular:** The NT uses three terms for Christ's return --- *parousia* (coming), *apokalypsis* (revelation), and *epiphaneia* (appearing) --- interchangeably. Ladd demonstrated they cannot be separated into two distinct events.

2. **The *apantēsis* metaphor:** When Paul says believers are "caught up... to *meet* the Lord in the air" (1 Thessalonians 4:17), the Greek *apantēsis* was used of citizens going out to greet an arriving dignitary and escorting him *back into the city*. Gundry and Wright both argue this points to the "rapture" as a royal welcome procession, not an evacuation. (The lexical claim is contested: Cosby's study of the ancient evidence found *apantēsis* "was not a technical term" and the formal-reception elements absent from 1 Thess 4:15-17, while conceding the text "leaves open" the escort-back-to-earth question — BBR 4 [1994]: 15-33; see Appendix H.)

3. **The sequence in 2 Thessalonians 2:** The man of sin must be revealed BEFORE "that day" (Christ's return). The church must be present to witness the antichrist before Christ comes.

4. **The saints overcome tribulation:** "Here is the patience of the saints: here are they that keep the commandments of God, and the faith of Jesus" (Revelation 14:12). The saints are *in* the tribulation, not removed from it.

5. **The Olivet Discourse:** Jesus describes believers enduring "the great tribulation" (Matthew 24:21), not escaping it. "Immediately *after* the tribulation of those days... he shall send his angels with a great sound of a trumpet, and they shall gather together his elect" (Matthew 24:29-31). The gathering is *after* the tribulation. (The pre-trib counter-reading takes the Discourse as Israel-focused rather than church-focused, and the gathering of Matthew 24:31 as the second-coming assembly of the elect rather than the church's rapture --- Pettegrew, TMSJ 13 [2002]: 173-90.)

6. **Historical continuity:** This view was held by the early church fathers, by the Reformers, and by virtually every Christian tradition before the 19th century.

**Pastoral fruit:**

- **Realistic hope:** Doesn't promise escape but promises preservation
- **Emphasizes endurance:** Christians have always faced tribulation; this view prepares for it
- **Unity with historic Christianity:** Connects with the early church, the Reformers, and the majority tradition
- **Less speculative:** Doesn't require complex timeline calculations

**Honest challenges:**

- **"God's wrath" on believers?** Critics argue that the tribulation is God's wrath, which believers should escape. Proponents distinguish between tribulation (*thlipsis*) and wrath (*orgē*) --- believers endure the former but are exempt from the latter.
- **Weakened urgency?** If tribulation must come first, does "any moment" urgency diminish?
- **Comforting the suffering:** Can "you must endure the worst first" truly comfort believers facing persecution?
- **Is the continuity claim precise?** Walvoord argued that "most modern forms of posttribulationism differ greatly from that of the early church or of the Protestant Reformers and are actually just as new or perhaps newer than pretribulationism" --- the fathers held imminent expectation, not Ladd's futurist scheme of a literal future seven-year tribulation (BSac 134 [1977]: 300). He also pressed the millennial-population problem: if all saints are raptured and glorified at the tribulation's end, who populates the millennium in natural bodies?
- **Inference cuts both ways:** Ladd himself conceded, "Nor does the Word of God explicitly place the Rapture at the end of the Tribulation" (*The Blessed Hope*, p. 165, quoted in Walvoord's review, BSac 113 [1956]: 297).

### A Note on the Partial-Rapture View

One further timing view deserves brief mention for completeness, though it has few living advocates. The **partial-rapture** view --- originating with Robert Govett in the 1850s and later held by G. H. Pember and G. H. Lang --- teaches that only watchful, faithful believers are caught up at Christ's coming; the rest of the church passes through the tribulation. Its strongest case rests on passages that condition participation on watchfulness (Matthew 25:1-13; Luke 21:36; Hebrews 9:28), read straightforwardly: those "accounted worthy... to escape" are taken. Its documented challenges: Walvoord's treatment (BSac 112 [1955]: 193-212) argues that translation as a reward for conduct sits uneasily with translation by grace, and Mayhue adds five objections, beginning with 1 Corinthians 15:51 --- "we shall *all* be changed" --- and the absence of any parallel partial resurrection in Scripture (TMSJ 13 [2002]: 243). Notably, both Ice and the anti-pretribulationist Bray read Margaret Macdonald's 1830 vision as partial-rapture in shape rather than pre-tribulational (Ice, BSac 147 [1990]: 158-60).

### Historicist View

**Held by:** The Protestant Reformers and their confessional heirs (16th-19th century). Notable advocates: Martin Luther, John Calvin, John Wesley, John Knox. Enshrined in the Westminster Confession of Faith (1646). Also held by Seventh-day Adventists today. Dominant Protestant position for roughly 300 years.

**Core claim:** The prophecies of Daniel and Revelation are fulfilled progressively throughout church history. The antichrist is not a future individual but the papal system, already present and active for centuries. Since the antichrist has already been revealed, the gathering of believers occurs at Christ's single, visible return --- which will destroy the antichrist system.

**The ordering question:** **Antichrist first (already here), then gathering.** The question of "which comes first" is moot: the antichrist has been operating for centuries. Christ's return will simultaneously destroy the antichrist and gather the saints.

**Key quotations:**

Martin Luther, *Smalcald Articles* (1537), Part II, Article IV:
> "This teaching shows forcefully that the Pope is the very Antichrist, who has exalted himself above, and opposed himself against Christ because he will not permit Christians to be saved without his power, which, nevertheless, is nothing, and is neither ordained nor commanded by God."

John Calvin, *Commentary on 2 Thessalonians* 2:3:
> "Paul, however, does not speak of one individual, but of a kingdom, that was to be taken possession of by Satan, that he might set up a seat of abomination in the midst of God's temple --- which we see accomplished in Popery."

Westminster Confession of Faith (1646), Chapter 25, Section 6:
> "There is no other head of the Church, but the Lord Jesus Christ; nor can the Pope of Rome, in any sense, be head thereof; but is that Antichrist, that man of sin, and son of perdition, that exalteth himself, in the Church, against Christ and all that is called God."

John Wesley, *Explanatory Notes upon the New Testament*, on 2 Thessalonians 2:3:
> "But the man of sin, the son of perdition --- Eminently so called, is not come yet. However, in many respects, the Pope has an indisputable claim to those titles. He is, in an emphatical sense, the man of sin, as he increases all manner of sin above measure."

(Wesley straddles the camps: his note applies the passage to the papacy in the present --- and his notes on 2:6-7 identify the restrainer with the Roman emperors, classic historicism --- while holding, with Bengel, that the *eminent* man of sin "is not come yet." Neither the historicist nor the futurist column can claim him cleanly.)

The Reformer identification of the papacy as antichrist survived into mid-nineteenth-century American academic exegesis. A *Bibliotheca Sacra* article of 1854 — "Paganized Ecclesiasticism, the Chief Antagonist of the Modern Missionary" (by "Rev. Joseph," BSac 11:44 [Oct 1854]: 801ff) — applies 2 Thess 2:4 verbatim to the modern papal ceremonial: as the Pope receives prostrations at the altar, "he exalteth himself above all that is called God, or that is worshipped; so that he, as God, sitteth in the temple of God, showing himself that he is God." The article treats the Pauline prophecy as realized in the historical papacy — the classic Reformer historicist reading, still on the American academic menu three centuries after Luther.

Joseph Mede, *Clavis Apocalyptica* (Key of the Revelation, 1627):

Mede, a Fellow of Christ's College, Cambridge, revived premillennialism after over a thousand years of suppression through his method of **synchronism** --- demonstrating that Revelation's visions describe parallel events from different angles rather than a strictly sequential narrative. He identified the antichrist as "the Metropolis of Christian Apostasy, the mystic Babylon" and placed the millennium *after* the antichrist's destruction. His work was so influential it was translated into English by Richard More, MP, and published by authorization of the House of Commons in 1643, directly shaping Isaac Newton's prophetic interpretation.

Isaac Newton, in *Observations upon the Prophecies of Daniel and the Apocalypse of St. John* (1733), read the little horn of Daniel 7 and the Apocalypse's Beast as the papal power --- the standard historicist identification of his era. (A widely circulated "power of the sword" quotation attributed to this work could not be located anywhere in the 1733 text and has been removed from this study as apocryphal; see Primary-Sources.)

Newton on the progressive corruption of the church and the revealing of the man of sin:

> "The mystery of iniquity which consisted in these heresies and began to work in the Apostles' days, worked more and more till it brake in upon the Church and caused that Christian religion was purest in the first age of Christianity and decayed gradually till the man of sin was revealed."
> --- Newton, theological manuscripts (Newton Project, THEM00222)

> "The Church of Rome began to be prejudiced against the religion of the Nazarenes and to lay stress upon ceremonies and things indifferent and to err in the faith."
> --- Newton, theological manuscripts (Newton Project)

Newton privately calculated that the world would not end before **2060**, based on 1,260 years from the rise of papal temporal power. In a manuscript note (Yahuda MS 7.3, c. 1704), he wrote:

> "It may end later, but I see no reason for its ending sooner. This I mention not to assert when the time of the end shall be, but to put a stop to the rash conjectures of fanciful men who are frequently predicting the time of the end, and by doing so bring the sacred prophecies into discredit."
> --- Newton, Yahuda MS 7.3 (Jewish National and University Library, Jerusalem)

**Gregory I (Pope Gregory the Great, c. 540-604)** --- A pope who condemned the very claim later popes would make:
> "Now I confidently say that whosoever calls himself, or desires to be called, Universal Priest, is in his elation the precursor of Antichrist, because he proudly puts himself above all others."
> --- Gregory I, *Registrum Epistolarum*, Book VII, Letter 33, to the Emperor Mauricius (NPNF, Series 2, Vol. 12, trans. Barmby)

Gregory argued that no bishop --- not even the Bishop of Rome --- should claim universal jurisdiction. Historicist interpreters from Wycliffe to Luther to Edwards cited Gregory against his own successors.

**The 1,260-year calculation:** Many historicists identified the starting point of the prophetic 1,260 "year-days" (Daniel 7:25, Revelation 13:5) with the rise of papal temporal power. Key dates include 538 AD (Belisarius's liberation of Rome from Ostrogothic siege, enabling papal consolidation of power) and 606 AD (Emperor Phocas recognized the Bishop of Rome as "universal bishop"). Jonathan Edwards considered starting dates of 479, 606, and 756, leaving the question open. Newton privately calculated 2060 based on the 1,260 years. (For the full historical arc from the *Pontifex Maximus* title through the Justinian decree to the Reformation, see Appendix E.)

Ellen G. White (Seventh-day Adventist), *The Great Controversy* (1888), ch. 20:
> "Not till after the great apostasy, and the long period of the reign of the 'man of sin,' can we look for the advent of our Lord."

SDA Fundamental Belief #25:
> "The second coming of Christ is the blessed hope of the church, the grand climax of the gospel. The Saviour's coming will be literal, personal, visible, and worldwide. When He returns, the righteous dead will be resurrected, and together with the righteous living will be glorified and taken to heaven."

**Pastoral fruit:**

- **Prophetic confidence:** Believers can trace God's hand through history
- **Courage against corruption:** Fueled the Reformation's challenge to institutional abuse
- **No speculative future timeline:** Focus on present faithfulness, not date-setting

**Honest challenges:**

- **Anti-Catholic polemic:** Identifying the papacy as antichrist has caused deep division and may not serve the love and unity test
- **The Reformation is over:** Many Protestants today do not identify the pope as antichrist; the view has waned significantly since the 19th century
- **Revelation's imagery:** Does the "man of sin" really describe an institution rather than an individual?

### Preterist View

**Held by:** Partial preterists within Reformed and evangelical traditions. Notable advocates: R.C. Sproul, Kenneth Gentry, Gary DeMar. Partial preterism is considered orthodox; full preterism (all prophecy fulfilled by AD 70) is widely regarded as heterodox.

**Core claim:** Most biblical prophecy --- including the "great tribulation" of Matthew 24, the antichrist, and the events of Revelation --- was fulfilled in the destruction of Jerusalem in AD 70 and the Roman persecution. Christ's bodily return, the general resurrection, and the final judgment remain future.

**The ordering question:** **Antichrist already past, gathering still future.** The antichrist (Nero, the Roman persecuting power) came and went in the first century. The "gathering" of believers occurs at Christ's future bodily return, with no antichrist precondition remaining.

**The strongest case:**

1. **Jesus's own timeline:** "Verily I say unto you, This generation shall not pass, till all these things be fulfilled" (Matthew 24:34). Partial preterists take "this generation" literally --- the events of Matthew 24 (including the tribulation) occurred within 40 years.

2. **Nero as the Beast:** The Hebrew gematria of *Neron Kaiser* (נרון קסר) yields exactly 666 (Revelation 13:18). Nero's persecution of Christians (AD 64-68) fits the description of the Beast.

3. **The fall of Jerusalem:** The destruction of the temple in AD 70 fulfills Jesus's prophecy: "There shall not be left here one stone upon another" (Matthew 24:2).

   *What this does to the study's central sequence text:* on the partial-preterist reading, Matthew 24:29-31 --- including the "gathering" of the elect --- was fulfilled in AD 70: the cosmic language is stock prophetic imagery for national judgment (Isaiah 13; 34), and the "gathering" is the messengers'/angels' in-gathering of the elect through gospel proclamation after the Jewish age ends. On that reading the Olivet sequence says nothing about a future rapture at all. Partial preterists divide the discourse differently (many break at 24:36; Gentry and France draw the line differently), and the strain of reading vv. 29-31 as AD 70 is itself the standard objection they must answer.

4. **R.C. Sproul:** "The coming of Christ in AD 70 was a coming in judgment on the Jewish nation, indicating the end of the Jewish age... But this was not the final or ultimate coming of Christ." (*The Last Days According to Jesus* [Baker, 1998], p. 158)

**Pastoral fruit:**

- **Removes speculative anxiety:** No need to identify current events as "signs of the end"
- **Takes Jesus's words seriously:** "This generation" means what it says
- **Focus on the present:** The kingdom is here; live it

**Honest challenges:**

- **Does it empty future hope?** If most prophecy is past, what is left to expect?
- **The rapture passage (1 Thess 4:17):** Even partial preterists acknowledge this is future --- but how does it connect to the "already fulfilled" tribulation passages?
- **Full preterism is a slippery slope:** Critics warn that partial preterism can slide into full preterism, which claims *all* prophecy --- including the bodily return, the resurrection, and the final judgment --- was fulfilled by AD 70. Full preterists argue that the "gathering" already happened, that the "new heavens and new earth" are spiritual realities, and that the church has been living in the "New Jerusalem" for 2,000 years. This position is considered heterodox by virtually all branches of Christianity --- Roman Catholic, Eastern Orthodox, and Protestant --- because it contradicts the Apostles' Creed ("He shall come again to judge the living and the dead"), the Nicene Creed, and every major confession of faith. Critics compare it to the Hymenaean heresy: "who concerning the truth have erred, saying that the resurrection is past already; and overthrow the faith of some" (2 Timothy 2:18).

### Anabaptist / Mennonite View

**Held by:** Mennonites, Amish, Hutterites, Church of the Brethren, and other Anabaptist communities. Rooted in the Radical Reformation of the 1520s-30s.

**Core claim:** The Anabaptist tradition has historically focused on discipleship, nonviolence, and the suffering church rather than on eschatological timelines. Their confessions affirm Christ's return to judge the living and the dead but do not speculate about the antichrist's identity or the rapture's timing. Because Anabaptists were being *martyred* when they wrote their confessions, any theology of "escape before tribulation" was foreign to their experience.

**The ordering question:** **Not directly addressed.** The silence is theologically significant --- Anabaptists focus on faithfulness in present suffering, not on eschatological sequencing.

**In their own words:**

Dordrecht Confession of Faith (1632), Article XVIII:
> "In the last day all men who shall have died, and fallen asleep, shall be awaked and quickened, and shall rise again, through the incomprehensible power of God... they, together with those who then will still be alive... shall be placed before the judgment seat of Christ, and the good be separated from the wicked."

Confession of Faith in a Mennonite Perspective (1995), Article 24:
> "We place our hope in the reign of God and in its fulfillment in the day when Christ our ascended Lord will come again in glory to judge the living and the dead. He will gather his church, already living under the reign of God according to the pattern of God's future."

**Pastoral fruit:**

- **Theology of suffering:** The cross, not escape, is the pattern of Christian life
- **Present faithfulness over speculation:** Focus on following Jesus now, not calculating timelines
- **Radical community:** The kingdom is lived out in the gathered body, not waited for in the future

**Honest challenges:**

- **Underdeveloped eschatology:** Critics note that avoiding the question is not the same as answering it
- **Does suffering alone define the Christian life?** The hope of Christ's return is also a biblical theme

### Eastern Orthodox View

**Held by:** The Eastern Orthodox Churches (Greek, Russian, Antiochian, etc.). Notable advocates: St. John of Damascus, St. Cyril of Jerusalem.

**Core claim:** The antichrist will be a real, personal figure who appears *before* Christ's visible return. The Orthodox reject the pre-tribulation rapture, premillennialism, and dispensationalism as Western innovations with no basis in the Church Fathers or the ecumenical councils.

**The ordering question:** **Antichrist first, then Christ's return.** Unambiguous.

St. John of Damascus, *An Exact Exposition of the Orthodox Faith*, Book IV, ch. 26 (c. 730 AD):
> "It should be known that the Antichrist is bound to come... First, therefore, it is necessary that the Gospel should be preached among all nations."

**Pastoral fruit:**

- **Continuity with the Fathers:** The Orthodox read eschatology through the same patristic lens as Augustine, Chrysostom, and the councils
- **Liturgical hope:** The Eucharist itself is an eschatological event --- "proclaiming the Lord's death till He come"

**Honest challenges:**

- **Limited engagement with modern eschatological debate:** The Orthodox tradition has not produced the volume of systematic eschatological writing that Western traditions have
- **Diverse internal views:** While the official position is clear, individual Orthodox theologians vary in their treatment of Revelation

### Pentecostal and Charismatic Views

The Pentecostal and Charismatic world is not monolithic on this question. There is a three-way split:

#### Classical Pentecostals: Pre-Tribulation (gathering before antichrist)

**Assemblies of God** — The Statement of Fundamental Truths (Article 14) describes a two-phase return:
> "The second coming of Christ includes the rapture of the saints, which is our blessed hope, followed by the visible return of Christ with His saints to reign on earth for one thousand years."
> --- AG Statement of Fundamental Truths, Article 14

A 1979 position paper, "The Rapture of the Church" (adopted by the General Presbytery, August 14, 1979), clarifies: "The weight of Scripture supports a pre-Tribulation Rapture. Wherever teaching about the Second Coming occurs in the New Testament, imminence is underscored."

**Church of God (Cleveland, TN)** similarly describes a two-phase coming: "First, to resurrect the righteous dead and to catch away the living saints to Him in the air. Second, to reign on the earth a thousand years."

**Church of God in Christ (COGIC)** affirms the rapture and the Great Tribulation but notably does *not* specify their order in official doctrine, leaving the timing question open.

**Word of Faith** teachers (Kenneth Hagin, Kenneth Copeland) default to standard dispensational pre-trib teaching but do not develop a distinctive eschatology. Kenneth Copeland Ministries states: "Kenneth Copeland has not taught extensively on the subject of the Rapture of the Church. However, through his personal study of God's Word, he adheres to the doctrine of Jesus coming to catch away His Church before the Great Tribulation" (kcm.org, "When will Jesus return?").

**Historical note:** Early AG leaders were *not* uniformly pre-trib. E.N. Bell (first General Superintendent) leaned mid-tribulation (*Pentecostal Evangel*, 1919). In 1924 the Executive Presbytery stopped Gospel Publishing House sales of the Scofield Reference Bible --- over Scofield's teaching on the Holy Spirit, not its dispensationalism --- and later reinstated it with a corrective pamphlet (per AG historian Daniel Isgrigg). The pre-trib consensus solidified later.

#### New Apostolic Reformation (NAR): No Rapture (church takes dominion)

The NAR represents a significant departure. Key figures (C. Peter Wagner, Bill Johnson of Bethel Church) reject the rapture framework entirely in favor of "Victorious Eschatology" --- the church is called to take dominion over all spheres of society *before* Christ returns.

Bill Johnson, *When Heaven Invades Earth* (2003), ch. 2:
> "Many, if not most theologians, make the mistake of taking all the good stuff contained in the prophets and sweeping it under that mysterious rug called, the Millennium... The mistaken idea is this: if it is good, it can't be for now."

The NAR framework is functionally postmillennial: the church wins, transforms the world, and Christ returns to a victorious bride --- not a rescued one. The antichrist is not a central concern.

#### IHOPKC / Mike Bickle: Post-Tribulation (church goes through tribulation)

Mike Bickle taught what he called "Apostolic Premillennialism" --- explicitly post-tribulational:

> "We believe in the post-tribulation rapture. That means the rapture at the end of the Tribulation, not at the beginning."
> --- Mike Bickle, "The Varying Importance of End-Time Beliefs," *Studies in the Book of Revelation*, Session 2 (IHOPKC, February 14, 2014)

This is the opposite of both the classical Pentecostal and NAR positions: the tribulation is real, it is coming, and the church must be prepared to endure it. The gathering occurs only *after* the antichrist's work.

**The ordering question across the Pentecostal/Charismatic world:**

| Stream | Gathering before antichrist? |
|--------|------------------------------|
| Classical Pentecostal (AG, CoG, COGIC) | Yes (pre-trib) |
| Word of Faith | Yes (default pre-trib) |
| NAR / Dominionist | N/A (no rapture framework) |
| IHOPKC / Bickle | No (post-trib) |

### Charles Haddon Spurgeon (1834-1892)

Spurgeon deserves special mention because he is claimed by many traditions but fits neatly into none of the modern camps. He was a **historic premillennialist** --- premillennial but *not* dispensational, and *not* pre-tribulational. The dedicated scholarly treatment confirms this characterization against rival claims (Masters claims him for amillennialism; Hulse and Iain Murray for postmillennialism): Swanson concludes his position "was most closely identifiable with that of historic premillennialism in teaching the church would experience the tribulation" (TMSJ 7 [1996]: 183; full study 183-212). Whitcomb adds that Spurgeon signed the 1891 Evangelical Baptist statement, "Our hope is the Personal Pre-millennial Return of the Lord Jesus in glory" (GTJ 7 [1986]: 233, citing *The Sword and Trowel* [1891] 446).

**On the antichrist (already present):**
> "It is the bounden duty of every Christian to pray against Antichrist, and as to what Antichrist is no sane man ought to raise a question. If it be not the Popery in the Church of Rome and in the Church of England, there is nothing in the world that can be called by that name."
> --- Spurgeon, "Pray for Jesus," Sermon No. 717, October 21, 1866 (Metropolitan Tabernacle Pulpit, Vol. 12)

**On Christ's premillennial return:**
> "We expect a reigning Christ on earth; that seems to us to be very plain, and to be put so literally that we dare not spiritualise it."
> --- Spurgeon, "Things to Come," Sermon 875, June 13, 1869

**On constant watchfulness over speculation:**
> "He may not come for a thousand years; he may come to-night."
> --- Spurgeon, "Watching for Christ's Coming," Sermon 2302, April 7, 1889

**The ordering question:** For Spurgeon, the antichrist (the papal system) was already present --- as it was for Luther, Calvin, and Wesley. Christ's return would destroy it and gather the saints. The gathering comes *at* Christ's return, not before a future tribulation.

### Amillennial / Catholic View

**Held by:** The Roman Catholic Church (officially) and many mainline Protestant and Reformed traditions. Notable advocates: Augustine of Hippo, Thomas Aquinas, the Catechism of the Catholic Church; among Protestant exegetes, Anthony A. Hoekema (*The Bible and the Future*, 1979) and Kim Riddlebarger (*A Case for Amillennialism*, 2003). (Eastern Orthodoxy concurs on the ordering and has its own section above.)

**The Protestant exegetical case** (so the view is not mistaken for a merely magisterial position): (1) the New Testament's two-age structure --- "this age" and "the age to come" (Matthew 12:32; Ephesians 1:21) --- leaves no room for an intermediate millennial age between them; (2) redemptive-historical typology --- the Old Testament land and kingdom promises find their fulfillment in Christ and the new-covenant people (Galatians 3:29; Hebrews 12:22-24), so Revelation 20's thousand years is the present reign of Christ, not a future earthly interregnum.

**Core claim:** The millennium of Revelation 20 is the present church age, not a future period. Satan is "bound" in the sense that the gospel can spread to all nations. At the end of the age, the antichrist will appear, the church will endure a final trial, and Christ will return in a single, visible event to judge the living and the dead. There is no separate rapture.

**The ordering question:** **Antichrist first, then gathering.** This is stated explicitly in both Augustine and the modern Catechism.

**Key quotations:**

Augustine, *City of God* 20.19:
> "That which there is no doubt he said is this, that Christ will not come to judge quick and dead unless Antichrist, His adversary, first come to seduce those who are dead in soul."

Thomas Aquinas (quoting the Gloss on 2 Thessalonians 2:4), *Summa Theologiae* III, Q.8, A.8:
> "As in Christ dwells the fullness of the Godhead, so in Antichrist the fullness of all wickedness... the devil by suggestion infuses his wickedness more copiously into him than into all others."

Catechism of the Catholic Church 675:
> "Before Christ's second coming the Church must pass through a final trial that will shake the faith of many believers. The persecution that accompanies her pilgrimage on earth will unveil the 'mystery of iniquity' in the form of a religious deception offering men an apparent solution to their problems at the price of apostasy from the truth. The supreme religious deception is that of the Antichrist, a pseudo-messianism by which man glorifies himself in place of God and of his Messiah come in the flesh."

Catechism of the Catholic Church 676:
> "The Church has rejected even modified forms of this falsification of the kingdom to come under the name of millenarianism, especially the 'intrinsically perverse' political form of a secular messianism."

Catechism of the Catholic Church 677:
> "The Church will enter the glory of the kingdom only through this final Passover, when she will follow her Lord in his death and Resurrection."

The Holy Office Decree of July 21, 1944 formally declared: "The system of mitigated Millenarianism cannot be taught safely" --- rejecting even modified forms of a future earthly millennium before the final judgment.

**Pastoral fruit:**

- **Deep historical roots:** This is the oldest continuous eschatological tradition in Christianity
- **Sobriety about the future:** Avoids speculative timelines and date-setting
- **Present kingdom focus:** The Church is living in the "already" of Christ's kingdom
- **Realistic about suffering:** Expects the church to endure trial, not escape it

**Honest challenges:**

- **Does amillennialism flatten Revelation?** Critics argue it spiritualizes too much, making Revelation's vivid imagery merely symbolic
- **The "thousand years":** Is it legitimate to read a specific number as purely symbolic?
- **Catholic-Protestant tension:** Protestant readers may resist a position so closely identified with Rome, even though many Reformed theologians (like Anthony Hoekema and Kim Riddlebarger) hold essentially the same amillennial framework

## Pastoral Considerations

Regardless of which view one holds, all Christians share these pastoral realities:

### Facing Persecution

Christians worldwide face persecution today — in some places severe, in others mild. Every view acknowledges believers will face tribulation of some kind. The comfort is not in escaping suffering but in being preserved through it.

### The Importance of Readiness

All views agree: be ready. Whether rapture comes in 1 year or 1,000 years, the call is the same — watch, pray, be faithful.

### Unity Among Believers

This is where pastoral sensitivity matters most. Christians who hold different eschatological views should:

- **Not judge each other's salvation** based on eschatology
- **Not divide over timelines** when the core is the same
- **Focus on what unites:** Christ's return, resurrection, redemption

### Evangelism and Mission

The urgency of the gospel is NOT dependent on whether the rapture is pre, mid, or post-tribulation. The world needs Christ regardless.

## Summary

Nine major traditions address the question of whether the gathering or the antichrist comes first. Only one --- the pre-tribulation view --- places the gathering *before* the antichrist. Every other tradition, from the early church fathers through the Reformers to the modern Catholic Catechism, places the antichrist's work *before* the gathering of believers at Christ's return (or treats the question differently, as with the Anabaptist and NAR traditions).

| Answer to "Which comes first?" | Views that hold this |
|-------------------------------|---------------------|
| **Gathering first** | Pre-tribulation only |
| **Antichrist first** | Post-tribulation, Mid-tribulation, Historicist, Amillennial/Catholic, Eastern Orthodox |
| **Antichrist already past** | Preterist (gathering is future, antichrist is not) |

All of these views have faithful, scholarly advocates. All are represented in Christian history and tradition. All pass the basic test: could a thoughtful Christian hold this view faithfully?

What matters most is not which view we hold, but that we hold our faith with humility and love for those who differ.
