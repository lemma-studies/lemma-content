## Areas of Agreement

Despite the debate over the reading, there is more agreement than the controversy suggests:

1. **The vision's theological message is not in dispute.** Whether the threat is locusts after the king's mowing or an invasion by Gog the king, the structure is the same: God reveals coming judgment, the prophet intercedes out of compassion, and God mercifully relents. The character of God --- just yet merciful --- is affirmed by both readings.

2. **Amos's intercession is remarkable regardless of the reading.** "By whom shall Jacob arise? for he is small" is a plea grounded in Israel's vulnerability, not Israel's merit. Both readings preserve this beautiful picture of prophetic compassion.

3. **The vision cycle's progression is clear.** Visions 1 and 2 feature intercession and divine relenting; visions 3-5 do not. The first vision is the most hopeful moment in a sequence that moves inexorably toward judgment. This structure is unchanged by the textual variant.

4. **The LXX Minor Prophets translator was not careless.** Whether one argues for a different Vorlage, a deliberate reinterpretation, or a scribal error, scholars on all sides agree that the "Gog" reading is not random noise --- something in the text or the tradition prompted it.

5. **Textual plurality was a reality in the Second Temple period.** The Dead Sea Scrolls have confirmed that multiple text forms of biblical books circulated simultaneously. The existence of variant readings does not undermine the reliability of Scripture --- it reflects the richness of its transmission history.

6. **Both readings are theologically fruitful.** The MT emphasizes God's concern for the economically oppressed; the LXX situates the vision within the grand prophetic narrative of God's ultimate victory over cosmic enemies. Neither reading is theologically deficient.

## Areas of Tension

1. **External evidence strongly favors the MT, but the LXX cannot be dismissed.** Every ancient version except the LXX reads "the king's mowings." This is the strongest argument for MT priority. But the LXX is the oldest translation, and Tov's work has shown that the LXX sometimes preserves older readings even against unanimous versional evidence.

2. **The nature of the LXX divergence is debated.** Was the "Gog" reading:
   - A scribal error (graphic confusion of consonants)?
   - A different Hebrew Vorlage?
   - A deliberate apocalyptic reinterpretation?
   - Some combination of these?
   Each explanation has different implications for how we evaluate the reading. A genuine Vorlage variant carries more weight than a scribal error; a deliberate reinterpretation is significant even if secondary.

3. **The chronological problem with Gog.** If the LXX reading is original and Amos (c. 760 BCE) referenced Gog, this predates the most common identification of Gog with Gyges of Lydia (c. 680-644 BCE). This means either: (a) Gog had a different, earlier referent unknown to us; (b) the name was secondarily inserted into the Amos text after Gyges became known; or (c) the Gyges identification is wrong and Gog has a different origin.

4. **How much weight should the LXX carry when it stands alone?** Some scholars (following Jerome) give strong priority to the Hebrew text. Others (following the early church's practice) argue the LXX deserves equal consideration as a witness to the text. This is a methodological question that reasonable scholars answer differently.

5. **The singularization problem.** The LXX does not merely swap "mowings" for "Gog" --- it also singularizes the locust swarm into "one locust" (*brouchos heis*) and adds the title "the king." This coordinated set of changes suggests something more than a simple misreading. But does it suggest a coherent variant Vorlage or a translator's interpretive intervention?

## Questions for the Reader

These questions are not leading --- they are designed to help you weigh the evidence and seek the Spirit's guidance.

### From Scripture:
- Does the consonantal Hebrew text (g-z-y vs. g-w-g) support one reading more naturally than the other?
- How does the immediate context of Amos's vision cycle --- the progression from concrete to cosmic --- inform which reading you find more compelling?
- What weight should the ancient versions (Targum, Peshitta, Vulgate) carry when they unanimously agree with the MT against the LXX?
- Does the LXX's singularization of the locust swarm ("one locust, Gog the king") suggest a coherent variant text or a translator's theological intervention?

### From Christocentrism:
- The apostles used the LXX of Amos as authoritative Scripture at the Jerusalem Council (Acts 15). Does this give the LXX readings of Amos greater weight, or is each variant an independent question?
- Jesus read Scripture for its deepest meaning, with humility before ambiguity. How does his example shape your approach to this textual question?
- Does it matter for your faith whether the original reading was "king's mowings" or "Gog the king"? What does your answer reveal about what you most value in Scripture?

### From Antiquity:
- The early church lived for centuries with both readings --- "Gog the king" in the East, "the king's mowings" in the West --- without doctrinal crisis. What does this precedent teach about living with textual ambiguity?
- The Gog tradition was well known in Jewish circles (the LXX reads "Gog" in Numbers 24:7; Targum Pseudo-Jonathan references "armies of Gog" in Numbers 24:17), yet the Targum does not read Gog in Amos 7:1. Does this suggest the MT reading was firmly established in the Jewish tradition, or does it simply reflect the Targumists' particular Vorlage?
- Jerome chose the Hebrew over the Greek. Was this the right priority? Or did something important get lost when the Western church abandoned the LXX?

### From Reality:
- Does either reading change what Amos 7:1-3 teaches about God's character --- his willingness to relent when a prophet intercedes?
- How should believers approach genuine textual uncertainty? Is it possible to hold a firm conviction about a reading while remaining charitable toward those who disagree?
- Which reading do you find more compelling, and why? What evidence most influences your conclusion?

## A Final Word

The textual question in Amos 7:1 is genuinely open. Responsible scholars disagree, and the evidence does not permit certainty. This study has attempted to present both readings at their strongest, with their best advocates' arguments and their honest challenges. No editorial verdict has been rendered because none is warranted by the evidence.

What is not open is the theological heart of the passage: God shows his prophet the coming devastation, the prophet pleads for the helpless, and God --- in his mercy --- relents. Whether the devastation is locusts after the king's mowing or the army of Gog the king, the God who hears the intercessor's prayer is the same God, and his mercy is the same mercy.

> "The LORD repented for this: It shall not be, saith the LORD."
> --- Amos 7:3 (KJV)
