*Wine, Jesus, and 1,900 Years of Christian Practice*

## The question

**Did Jesus drink alcoholic wine, and does Scripture teach that wine as a substance — distinct from drunkenness — is sinful?**

## The verdict

**Yes, Jesus drank fermented wine. No, Scripture does not teach that wine as a substance is sinful. Yes, drunkenness is universally condemned. Yes, personal abstention is honored as a vocation, never universalized as rule.**

## The SCAR summary

| Component | Verdict |
|---|---|
| **Scripture** | Wine is God's gift (Ps 104:15; Deut 14:26; Isa 25:6). Drunkenness is universally condemned (Prov 20:1; Eph 5:18; Gal 5:21). Abstention is honored as vocation (Num 6 Nazirite; Luke 1:15 John the Baptist), never as universal rule. The two-wine theory's lexical claim — that biblical vocabulary distinguishes fermented from unfermented substance — is not supported by Hebrew (Hos 4:11 pairs *yayin* and *tirosh* in a single warning that both "take away the heart" — the two-wine defense of the verse is given in Ch4.5 and answered in Ch5) or by Greek (*gleukos* at Acts 2:13 is what intoxicates, per Peter's response). |
| **Christocentrism** | Jesus made ~120-180 gallons of fermented wine at Cana (John 2). He self-reported at Matthew 11:19 that the crowd called him a "winebibber" — the accusation only lands because he drank wine. He took the Passover cup at the Last Supper and blessed it as "the fruit of the vine" (Matt 26:29 par.) — Passover cups were fermented wine in unanimous Jewish witness. He warned against drunkenness (Luke 21:34); he taught nothing prohibitive about wine as substance. |
| **Antiquity** | Every pre-Nicene source that names the eucharistic cup names it as wine: Didache 9-10; Ignatius (Rom 7; Phld 4); Justin 1 Apol 65-67 ("bread and a cup of wine mixed with water"); Irenaeus (AH V.2.3, "the mingled cup"); Cyprian Ep 62 (the anchor letter against water-only communion). Clement of Alexandria's *Paedagogus* II.2 treats wine as gift, permits moderate use, warns against drunkenness, honors ascetic abstention as vocation. The Encratite move to universalize abstention was condemned as heretical. |
| **Reality** | The two-wine theory is a 19th-c. American Protestant construction (Nott, lectures 1838-9, printed 1846; Stuart 1848; Lees & Burns 1868; Patton 1874; Bacchiocchi 1989). Thomas Welch invented pasteurized grape juice in Vineland NJ, 1869; the Methodist Discipline made grape juice standard in 1880; Prohibition (1920-1933) locked in the American Protestant practice. The doctrinal split runs along doctrine of the sacraments — sacramental traditions (Catholic, Orthodox, Anglican, Lutheran LCMS/WELS) never adopted grape juice; symbolic-memorial traditions (Methodist, Baptist, Restorationist, Holiness, Pentecostal) did. Modern academic scholarship (McGovern's biomolecular archaeology; Broshi's economic archaeology; Wenham, Wilkins, Meadors, Gentry) uniformly supports fermented wine as the biblical writers' referent. |

## The pattern

**Use with thanksgiving. Moderation always. Honor for those whose vocation calls for something different. Love and unity above every substance-question.**

- Wine is a gift of God (1 Tim 4:4-5).
- Drunkenness is sin (Eph 5:18).
- Vocational abstention is honored (Num 6; Rom 14).
- Substance is prudential and pastoral, not first-order in Jesus's priority-structure.

## The two-wine theory in brief

**Claim**: Biblical vocabulary for wine is generic; Scripture's approved "wine" is unfermented, its condemned "wine" is fermented.

**Origin**: American temperance movement (the movement from 1826, the exegesis 1838-1890).

**Modern academic status**: not received sustained defense in the major refereed evangelical journals since roughly 1980; it continues to be advanced in earnest inside Seventh-day Adventist, Independent Fundamental Baptist, and Wesleyan Holiness circles.

**Where the theory fails**: (a) Hosea 4:11 pairs *yayin* and *tirosh* as both intoxicating; (b) Acts 2:13's *gleukos* accusation only lands because *gleukos* intoxicates (Peter corrects the timing, not the vocabulary); (c) Cana's master-of-the-feast comment presupposes fermented wine on both sides of the comparison; (d) every pre-Nicene source the church received as authoritative read the same texts as fermented wine; (e) archaeological evidence (McGovern) shows fermented wine was the ordinary ancient table drink and unfermented juice a marginal preservation category.

**What the theory got right**: the pastoral concern for the addiction-vulnerable is real and deserves serious response. The mature sacramental accommodations (Catholic *mustum*, Lutheran and Anglican individual accommodation, ELCA whole-assembly flexibility) meet the concern without rewriting the lexicon.

## Practical guidelines

1. **Take Scripture's drunkenness warnings seriously.** Moderation is a real standard, not a temperance-era exaggeration.
2. **Read your own vocation honestly.** If drinking is a snare for you, abstain — with the church's blessing, in the Nazirite tradition.
3. **Read your community's vocation honestly.** Pastoral wisdom may direct a congregation's corporate practice; that is legitimate.
4. **Do not universalize your particular practice.** Whether wine or grape juice at communion, do not judge the other Christian community as sub-Christian or compromised. And do not treat the substance question as a test of orthodoxy or of personal holiness. Communities that adopt corporate abstention for pastoral reasons — high addiction burden, recovery culture, care for the weaker brother — are exercising legitimate prudence; the counsel here is only against elevating that local practice into a test of another community's fidelity to Christ.
5. **Test every practice by love and unity.** Where the substance-question divides Christ's body, it has been mis-scaled.
