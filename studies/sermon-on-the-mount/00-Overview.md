# The Sermon on the Mount: For Us Today?

*"Therefore whosoever heareth these sayings of mine, and doeth them, I will liken him unto a wise man, which built his house upon a rock."* --- Matthew 7:24 (KJV)

## The Question

Is the Sermon on the Mount for believers today to follow? Christians have given strikingly different answers. At one end, Anabaptist communities have practiced its commands literally for five centuries --- turning the other cheek, refusing oaths, loving enemies at real cost. At the other end, some dispensational teachers have argued that the Sermon was addressed to Jewish disciples about a future kingdom and is not the church's direct marching orders. In between, most Christians have affirmed the Sermon as binding but debated what "binding" looks like in daily life.

The question matters because the Sermon on the Mount (Matthew 5--7) is the longest recorded block of Jesus's teaching in all four Gospels. It contains the Beatitudes, the Lord's Prayer, the Golden Rule, and some of the most demanding ethical commands ever spoken: love your enemies, turn the other cheek, do not worry about tomorrow, be perfect as your Father in heaven is perfect. If these words apply to us, they reshape everything. If they don't, we need to know why.

This study examined the question through four lenses --- Scripture, Christocentrism, Antiquity, and Reality --- and what follows is what we found. Each section points to a deeper chapter for those who want the full evidence.

## What Scripture Says

The place to begin is the Sermon's own words. Two passages are especially important.

**First, the hinge passage --- Matthew 5:17--18:**

> "Think not that I am come to destroy the law, or the prophets: I am not come to destroy, but to fulfil. For verily I say unto you, Till heaven and earth pass, one jot or one tittle shall in no wise pass from the law, till all be fulfilled."
> --- Matthew 5:17--18 (KJV)

The word translated "fulfil" is the Greek *plēroō*, and it carries more than one meaning. Matthew uses it throughout his Gospel for prophetic fulfillment --- "that it might be fulfilled which was spoken by the prophet" (1:22; 2:15; 4:14). But in the immediate context, Jesus goes on to *deepen* the law through six antitheses: "Ye have heard that it was said... but I say unto you." Murder is extended to anger. Adultery is extended to lust. Retaliation is replaced by non-resistance. Love of neighbor is extended to love of enemies. The pattern is intensification, not abolition. Jesus fills the law with its fullest meaning.

The six antitheses are the structural core of the Sermon, and they are worth pausing over. In each case, Jesus claims personal authority over the Torah itself --- "but *I* say unto you" --- something no rabbi of his time would have dared. He does not weaken a single command. He deepens every one. The Sermon is the law at its most demanding, not at its most relaxed.

**Second, the Sermon's own conclusion --- Matthew 7:24--27:**

> "Therefore whosoever heareth these sayings of mine, and doeth them, I will liken him unto a wise man, which built his house upon a rock... And every one that heareth these sayings of mine, and doeth them not, shall be likened unto a foolish man, which built his house upon the sand."
> --- Matthew 7:24, 26 (KJV)

Jesus does not say "whosoever understands these sayings" or "whosoever admires these sayings." He says "whosoever *doeth* them." The Greek verb is *poieō* --- to do, to perform, to put into practice. And the consequences are stark: the house on the rock stands; the house on the sand falls, "and great was the fall of it." The Sermon presents itself as instruction to be practiced, not theory to be discussed.

**Third, the Great Commission --- Matthew 28:18--20:**

> "Go ye therefore, and teach all nations, baptizing them in the name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Ghost: Teaching them to observe all things whatsoever I have commanded you: and, lo, I am with you alway, even unto the end of the world."
> --- Matthew 28:19--20 (KJV)

"All things whatsoever I have commanded you" --- spoken to the apostles, directed to "all nations," extending "unto the end of the world." The Sermon on the Mount is the largest block of Jesus's commands in the Gospel of Matthew. To exclude it from "all things" requires an argument the text itself does not provide.

One more feature of the Sermon deserves mention. The command "Be ye therefore perfect" (Matt 5:48) sounds impossible --- but the Greek word *teleios* does not mean "sinless." It means "complete, whole, having reached its purpose." And the context tells us exactly what kind of completeness is in view: the verse concludes the section on loving enemies. Luke's parallel makes it explicit: "Be ye therefore merciful, as your Father also is merciful" (Luke 6:36). The "perfection" Jesus demands is wholeness of love --- a love that does not discriminate between friend and enemy, because the Father's love does not discriminate. That is still breathtakingly demanding. But it is a different kind of demand than sinless moral flawlessness.

Finally, the rest of the New Testament echoes the Sermon extensively. James parallels it throughout --- one recent study documents forty-five parallel statements (Porter, *BSac* 162 [2005]: 347--52) --- "be ye doers of the word, and not hearers only" (James 1:22), "swear not" (James 5:12), "judge not" (James 4:11). Paul applies Sermon ethics directly to Gentile churches: "Bless them which persecute you: bless, and curse not... Recompense to no man evil for evil... overcome evil with good" (Romans 12:14, 17, 21). These are not faint echoes. They are sustained, deliberate applications of the Sermon to church life --- without any suggestion that these teachings belong to a different dispensation.

## What Jesus Taught --- and Lived

The Sermon on the Mount was not spoken by an anonymous legislator. It was spoken by someone who then lived every word of it.

Jesus commanded: "Turn the other cheek" (Matt 5:39). Before the high priest, an officer struck him, and Jesus did not strike back (John 18:22--23). At the cross, he endured scourging, mockery, and crucifixion without resistance. Peter, who witnessed it, recognized the pattern: "Who, when he was reviled, reviled not again; when he suffered, he threatened not" (1 Peter 2:23).

Jesus commanded: "Love your enemies, ... and pray for them which despitefully use you" (Matt 5:44). From the cross, while his enemies were killing him: "Father, forgive them; for they know not what they do" (Luke 23:34). This was not a general wish for humanity. It was a specific prayer for specific enemies --- the soldiers driving the nails.

Jesus commanded: "Lay not up for yourselves treasures upon earth" (Matt 6:19). He owned nothing: "The foxes have holes, and the birds of the air have nests; but the Son of man hath not where to lay his head" (Matt 8:20). Born in a borrowed manger, buried in a borrowed tomb, the one who preached simplicity lived it.

Jesus commanded: "Pray in secret" (Matt 6:6). Mark records: "And in the morning, rising up a great while before day, he went out, and departed into a solitary place, and there prayed" (Mark 1:35). His prayer life was not performed for an audience.

If the Sermon is not for today, then Jesus lived by a standard he did not intend for his followers --- a conclusion that sits uneasily with the rest of his teaching. The Great Commission extends the Sermon to all nations, all time. The Holy Spirit, promised in John 14:15--17, was given to enable what the flesh cannot achieve on its own. Paul confirms: "the righteousness of the law might be fulfilled in us, who walk not after the flesh, but after the Spirit" (Romans 8:4). The Sermon's demands are staggering, but the provision for their practice is equally real.

And the Sermon is organized around Jesus's deepest priorities. His summary of the law --- "love God, love neighbor" (Matt 22:37--40) --- is exactly what the Sermon expounds. His new commandment --- "love one another, as I have loved you" (John 13:34) --- finds its most sustained illustration in the Sermon's teaching on reconciliation, generosity, and enemy-love. Remove the Sermon, and the supporting architecture of Jesus's entire ethical vision is weakened.

## What the Earliest Christians Practiced

The Christians of the first three centuries did not debate whether the Sermon applied to them. They simply followed it --- and paid for it.

The most striking early witness is the *Didache* ("Teaching of the Twelve Apostles"), a church manual written perhaps as early as 70--100 AD. Its opening section is baptismal instruction for new converts, and it draws directly from the Sermon:

> "Bless them that curse you, and pray for your enemies and fast for them that persecute you; for what thank is it, if ye love them that love you? Do not even the Gentiles the same? But do ye love them that hate you, and ye shall not have an enemy... If any man give thee a blow on thy right cheek, turn to him the other also, and thou shalt be perfect; if a man impress thee to go with him one mile, go with him twain."
> --- *Didache* 1:2--4 (Lightfoot translation)

This was not advanced instruction for the spiritually elite. It was the basic content of Christian formation --- what every convert learned before baptism.

Justin Martyr (c. 155 AD) went further. Writing to the Roman Emperor, he presented the Sermon's commands as a description of what Christians actually do:

> "...we who formerly delighted in fornication, but now embrace chastity alone... we who valued above all things the acquisition of wealth and possessions, now bring what we have into a common stock, and communicate to every one in need; we who hated and destroyed one another... now, since the coming of Christ, live familiarly with them, and pray for our enemies."
> --- Justin Martyr, *First Apology* 14 (Marcus Dods and George Reith translation, ANF Vol. 1)

Justin was not preaching to Christians. He was explaining Christianity to an emperor --- and he staked his case on the claim that Christians really live this way.

Tertullian (c. 200 AD), a trained lawyer, applied the Sermon with legal precision. He argued that Christians cannot serve in the military because Christ forbade violence and retaliation: "Shall it be held lawful to make an occupation of the sword, when the Lord proclaims that he who uses the sword shall perish by the sword?" (*De Corona* 11). He was not alone. The question of Christian military service was a live controversy in the pre-Nicene church, and the Sermon on the Mount was the primary text on one side.

The pattern across three centuries is unmistakable: from the Didache through Clement of Rome, Polycarp, Irenaeus, Origen, and on to Chrysostom and Augustine, not a single writer treats the Sermon as intended for a future dispensation or a different audience. Every one of them applies it to believers in their own time. The unanimity is remarkable.

## The Major Views Today

Four principal traditions have shaped how Christians read the Sermon. Each deserves its strongest case.

### View 1: Literal Obedience (Anabaptist)

The Sermon contains direct commands from Christ that all Christians are to obey without qualification. Non-resistance, refusal of oaths, simplicity of life, and enemy-love are requirements, not ideals. Jesus said "doeth them," not "admires them." The Great Commission universalizes the Sermon to all nations. The pre-Nicene church took the Sermon literally. And Anabaptist communities --- Mennonites, Hutterites, Amish --- have practiced it for five hundred years, demonstrating that literal obedience is costly but not impossible. The Amish forgiveness extended to the family of the gunman who killed five schoolgirls at Nickel Mines, Pennsylvania (2006) was not a theological abstraction. It was the Sermon on the Mount in practice.

**Honest challenge:** If Matthew 5:29 ("pluck out your eye") is almost universally recognized as hyperbole, on what principle does one distinguish hyperbolic commands from literal ones? (Chapter 4 examines a published ten-criterion answer --- and its double edge.)

### View 2: Kingdom Ethics for All Believers (Mainstream Evangelical)

The Sermon describes the ethical standard for all who enter the kingdom of God. Its commands are binding on every believer, to be obeyed in the power of the Holy Spirit within the "already/not-yet" tension of kingdom life. Christians strive toward the Sermon's standard, empowered by grace, knowing that perfect fulfillment awaits the consummation. This is the broadest consensus position, held by John Stott, Dietrich Bonhoeffer, D.A. Carson, and the mainstream of Reformed, Catholic, and Orthodox theology.

**Honest challenge:** If the commands are binding but we acknowledge we will never perfectly obey them, at what point does "striving in the Spirit" become indistinguishable from comfortable disobedience?

### View 3: The Law's Mirror (Lutheran)

The Sermon's demands are intentionally impossible. They are designed to reveal the depth of human sin and drive the hearer to dependence on Christ. "Be ye therefore perfect" is not an achievable goal but a hammer that shatters every claim to moral sufficiency. The law convicts; grace saves. This view produces a profound appreciation for grace and protects against the crushing burden of perfectionism.

**Honest challenge:** Jesus used imperative verbs --- commands to be obeyed, not merely admired. If the Sermon's primary function is to expose failure, why did Jesus insist on *doing* as the mark of the wise builder (Matt 7:24)?

### View 4: Future Kingdom (Classical Dispensational)

The Sermon was addressed to Jewish hearers about the coming Messianic kingdom. Its commands are variously assigned within the tradition --- to the millennial kingdom (Scofield, Chafer), to the offer of the kingdom (Pentecost), or to the tribulation period (Rand) --- but not to the church as its direct rule of life. The church operates under the Pauline epistles and the dispensation of grace. This view is associated with C.I. Scofield, Lewis Sperry Chafer, and Charles Ryrie, though even within dispensationalism the strict kingdom reading is now "probably a minority position" (Martin, quoted in Wiley, *JODT* 23 [2019]: 36), and progressive dispensationalists (Craig Blaising, Darrell Bock) have largely moved toward View 2.

**Honest challenge:** The Great Commission (Matt 28:20) commands the teaching of "all things whatsoever I have commanded you" to "all nations." Paul echoes the Sermon throughout Romans 12. The entire pre-Nicene church applied the Sermon to all believers. No writer before John Nelson Darby (c. 1830) reads the Sermon this way.

## What All Views Agree On

The disagreements are real, but the common ground is wider than most expect. Across all four views:

- **Jesus speaks with supreme authority.** No Christian tradition denies that the Sermon is the word of the Son of God.
- **The Christian life requires transformation.** Even those who create the most distance between the Sermon and the church still expect Christians to live by Romans 12, which says substantially the same things.
- **Love is the governing principle.** Jesus himself said that all the law and the prophets hang on love of God and love of neighbor (Matt 22:37--40). The Sermon expounds exactly that.
- **Grace and obedience are not enemies.** No one is saved by obeying the Sermon. But the one who has been saved by grace is called to walk in newness of life --- and the Sermon describes what that walk looks like. Paul makes the sequence explicit: "For by grace are ye saved through faith... For we are his workmanship, created in Christ Jesus unto good works, which God hath before ordained that we should walk in them" (Ephesians 2:8, 10).
- **Hearing alone is not enough.** Every view agrees that the Sermon demands a response. The wise builder hears and does. The foolish builder hears and does not. Whatever one's theological framework, the wisest response to the words of Jesus is the simplest: do them.

The patristic evidence is unusually one-sided: from 70 AD to 430 AD, not a single writer treats the Sermon as intended for a future dispensation. The Great Commission creates a significant burden of proof for anyone who would exclude the Sermon from "all things whatsoever I have commanded you." And the New Testament echoes are too extensive to be coincidental --- James, Paul, Peter, and John all apply Sermon ethics to the church without qualification.

## For the Reader

If you have read this far, you already know that the evidence accumulated across five chapters of this study points in a clear direction: the vast majority of Christians across two thousand years have believed the Sermon on the Mount is for all believers in every age. The pre-Nicene church practiced it. The Great Commission extends it. Paul and James and Peter applied it. And Jesus himself --- the one who spoke it --- lived every word of it, then commissioned its teaching to all nations until the end of the world.

But this study does not render a verdict. The Sermon itself does. "Whosoever heareth these sayings of mine, and doeth them" --- that is the Sermon's own standard, and it belongs to the reader, not to the commentator.

What this study can do is clear the ground. If you feared that following the Sermon means returning to a law-keeping religion, Paul answers: grace saves, and the saved are created for good works. If you feared that the Sermon's demands are impossible, Jesus answers: "I will pray the Father, and he shall give you another Comforter, that he may abide with you for ever" (John 14:16). The Spirit who enables is the gift of the one who commands. If you feared that the Sermon belongs to another people and another age, the Great Commission answers: all nations, all things, until the end of the world.

The deeper chapters are there for those who want the full evidence. Chapter 1 takes you through the Sermon passage by passage in Greek. Chapter 2 shows you how Jesus himself lived and interpreted his own teaching. Chapter 3 lays out the early church testimony in complete quotations you can read for yourself. Chapter 4 presents every major view at its strongest, with the fairness its best advocates would recognize. Chapter 5 names the common ground, the honest tensions, and the questions that only you and the Spirit can answer.

But the Sermon was never meant to be an intellectual exercise. It was given by a Teacher who expected his students to do the homework. It was lived by a Savior who walked the path before asking anyone else to walk it. And it was sealed by a promise:

> "Lo, I am with you alway, even unto the end of the world."
> --- Matthew 28:20 (KJV)

You are not asked to obey the Sermon alone. You are asked to obey it in the company of the one who spoke it and the Spirit he sent. That is the rock on which the house is built.

> "A new commandment I give unto you, That ye love one another; as I have loved you, that ye also love one another. By this shall all men know that ye are my disciples, if ye have love one to another."
> --- John 13:34--35 (KJV)
