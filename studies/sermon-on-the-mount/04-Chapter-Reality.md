# Chapter 4: Reality --- Four Views and Their Fruit

## Overview

Four principal interpretive traditions have emerged around the question of whether the Sermon on the Mount (Matthew 5--7) is for believers today to follow. They are not equally distributed across church history, and they are not equally supported by the exegetical evidence --- but each deserves to be heard at its strongest before being evaluated.

The four views are:

1. **Literal Obedience** --- The Sermon contains direct commands from Christ that all Christians are to obey without qualification (Anabaptist / Radical Reformation).
2. **Kingdom Ethics for All Believers** --- The Sermon describes the ethical standard for all who enter the kingdom, to be obeyed in the power of the Holy Spirit while acknowledging the already/not-yet tension (Mainstream Evangelical / Reformed).
3. **The Law's Mirror / Impossible Ideal** --- The Sermon's demands are intentionally impossible, designed to crush self-righteousness and drive the hearer to faith in Christ (Lutheran Emphasis).
4. **Future Kingdom / Primarily for Israel** --- The Sermon was addressed to Jewish disciples about the coming kingdom and is not the church's direct marching orders (Classical Dispensational).

An important structural observation: Views 1 and 2 overlap significantly in their conclusion --- both insist the Sermon must be *obeyed* --- even though they differ in method and emphasis. Views 3 and 4 overlap in their practical effect: both create distance between the Sermon's commands and the daily ethical obligations of the believer, though for very different theological reasons. The real fault line in the church is not between adjacent views but between those who read the Sermon as a *direct ethical mandate* (Views 1 and 2) and those who read it as pointing *beyond itself* to something else --- grace (View 3) or a future dispensation (View 4).

Two additional minority positions deserve brief mention: the **medieval two-tier view**, which distinguished between precepts binding on all Christians and "counsels of perfection" for the spiritually advanced (monks, clergy); and Albert Schweitzer's **interim ethic**, which proposed that Jesus expected the world to end imminently and issued extreme commands suitable only for that brief window. The two-tier view dominated Catholic theology from Aquinas through Vatican II. The interim ethic is largely rejected by modern scholarship but occasionally resurfaces.

## View 1: Literal Obedience (Anabaptist / Radical Reformation)

### Held By

Mennonites, Hutterites, Amish, Church of the Brethren, many Quakers, and in recent decades a growing movement of "neo-Anabaptists" including theologians such as Stanley Hauerwas, ethicist Glen Stassen, pastor and author Shane Claiborne, and theologian Greg Boyd. Leo Tolstoy's late-life Christianity was built almost entirely on a literal reading of the Sermon, particularly the command to non-resistance. The Schleitheim Confession (1527), the earliest Anabaptist confession of faith, explicitly grounds its ethic of non-resistance in the words of Jesus in Matthew 5. John Howard Yoder's *The Politics of Jesus* (1972) is the most influential modern articulation. Harold Bender's 1943 presidential address to the American Society of Church History, "The Anabaptist Vision," articulated the movement's self-understanding: the center of Christianity is discipleship, and the Sermon on the Mount is its charter.

### Core Claim

The Sermon on the Mount contains direct commands from Christ that all Christians are to obey literally and without qualification. Non-resistance, refusal of oaths, simplicity of life, and enemy-love are not distant ideals, lofty aspirations, or hyperbolic illustrations --- they are requirements. To "spiritualize" these commands into attitudes rather than actions is to do exactly what the Pharisees did: honor God with the lips while the heart (and the hands) are far from obedience.

### Strongest Arguments

**1. Jesus said "doeth them," not "admires them."** The Sermon concludes with a parable that draws the line with unmistakable clarity:

> "Therefore whosoever heareth these sayings of mine, and doeth them, I will liken him unto a wise man, which built his house upon a rock."
> --- Matthew 7:24 (KJV)

> "And every one that heareth these sayings of mine, and doeth them not, shall be likened unto a foolish man, which built his house upon the sand."
> --- Matthew 7:26 (KJV)

The word *ποιέω* ("to do, to make, to perform") is concrete, active, and practical. Jesus does not say "whosoever understands," "whosoever appreciates," or "whosoever aspires to" these sayings. The Sermon is framed as instruction to be *practiced*. Its final word is a warning to those who hear without doing.

**2. The Great Commission universalizes the Sermon.** The risen Christ commands:

> "Go ye therefore, and teach all nations, baptizing them in the name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Ghost: Teaching them to observe all things whatsoever I have commanded you."
> --- Matthew 28:19-20 (KJV)

"All things whatsoever I have commanded you" --- spoken by the same Jesus who spoke the Sermon, in the same Gospel of Matthew. If the Sermon is not to be observed by all nations, what did Jesus mean by "all things"? The Great Commission makes no distinction between commands for Israel and commands for the church, between hard sayings and comfortable ones.

**3. The early church took the Sermon literally.** Tertullian, writing around 200 AD, refused military service on the grounds that Christ had disarmed Peter and thus disarmed every Christian (*De Corona* 11; *De Idololatria* 19). The Didache, possibly the earliest post-apostolic document (c. 70--100 AD), opens by echoing the Sermon's commands almost verbatim: love your enemies, pray for those who persecute you, turn the other cheek. Justin Martyr, in his *First Apology* (c. 155 AD), presents the Sermon's teachings as proof that Christians actually live this way --- not as an ideal but as a description of practice. The pre-Constantinian church, when it was closest to the apostolic witness and farthest from political power, read the Sermon as a literal mandate.

**4. Spiritualizing the hard commands is exactly what the Pharisees did.** The Sermon itself is a sustained critique of finding ways around God's requirements. Jesus says the Pharisees taught that only murder is sin --- but he extends the command to anger (5:21-22). They taught that only adultery is sin --- but he extends the command to lust (5:27-28). The Pharisees narrowed God's requirements to make them manageable. To read "turn the other cheek" as a metaphor for inner patience, or "give to him that asketh" as a principle about generosity rather than a command about actual giving, is to play the same game.

**5. The Anabaptist witness has been remarkably consistent.** For five centuries, Anabaptist communities have practiced non-resistance, refusal of oaths, simplicity, mutual aid, and enemy-love --- not as monastic elites but as ordinary families and farming communities. The Amish forgiveness extended to the family of the gunman who killed five schoolgirls in Nickel Mines, Pennsylvania (2006) was not a theological abstraction. It was the Sermon on the Mount in practice. This consistency of witness across centuries suggests that literal obedience is not impossible but merely costly.

### Pastoral Fruit

- A radical peace witness that has challenged every Christian tradition to ask whether its compromises with violence are truly necessary
- Deep community life built on mutual aid, simplicity, and shared accountability
- Counter-cultural discipleship that is visibly distinct from the surrounding culture --- exactly what Jesus described as a "city set on a hill" (Matt 5:14)
- A refusal to separate faith from practice that honors the Sermon's own conclusion (Matt 7:24-27)

### Honest Challenges

**Does literal obedience to Matthew 5:29-30 show the Sermon uses hyperbole?** Jesus says:

> "And if thy right eye offend thee, pluck it out, and cast it from thee: for it is profitable for thee that one of thy members should perish, and not that thy whole body should be cast into hell."
> --- Matthew 5:29 (KJV)

No Anabaptist community practices literal self-mutilation. If this command is almost universally recognized as hyperbole (Rand is a rare published exception, reading 5:29-30 literally but as addressed to unbelievers --- *BSac* 112 [1955]: 133), on what principle does one distinguish hyperbolic commands from literal ones? The question has a published answer: Cruise systematized ten criteria --- four logical (following Stein: literally impossible; conflicts with what Jesus says or does elsewhere; conflicts with Scripture; would not achieve its goal if literally fulfilled), three linguistic, and three rhetorical (*JETS* 61 [2018]: 92--93). But the answer cuts both ways for View 1: applying his own criteria, Cruise concludes that all four positive prescriptions of Matt 5:38-42 --- including turn-the-other-cheek --- are hyperbolic, mitigated to a still-radical core of non-retaliation and forgiveness: "Once scaled down, the teaching may be seen as simply encouraging nonretaliation" (pp. 100--101). He notes that Jesus himself "modeled only the negative principle, that is, non-retaliation" --- when struck before the high priest, Jesus protested the blow rather than offering the other cheek (John 18:22-23; Cruise, p. 94). The Anabaptist can accept the criteria and still insist the mitigated core --- real non-retaliation at real cost --- is exactly what Anabaptist communities practice; but the view must concede that not every sentence in the Sermon is intended to be obeyed in its most wooden literal sense.

**The tension between non-resistance and the protection of the vulnerable.** If a man is beating a child, does "resist not evil" (Matt 5:39) require the Christian to stand by? Anabaptist ethicists have wrestled with this honestly. Yoder distinguished between non-resistance to personal affront and the obligation to protect the helpless. But the text itself does not make that distinction, and critics argue that the logic of non-resistance, pressed to its conclusion, produces passivity in the face of injustice.

**The "two kingdoms" critique.** By withdrawing from civic participation --- refusing to hold public office, serve on juries, or bear arms --- Anabaptist communities have been accused of abandoning responsibility for the common good. Hauerwas and the neo-Anabaptists have responded that the church's first political task is to *be the church*, a visible alternative community --- but the question remains whether this is faithfulness or withdrawal.

## View 2: Kingdom Ethics for All Believers (Mainstream Evangelical / Reformed)

### Held By

This is the dominant position in modern Protestant Christianity and in much Catholic and Orthodox theology. Its most influential advocates include John Stott, whose *The Message of the Sermon on the Mount* (1978) has shaped a generation of evangelical preaching; D.A. Carson, whose *The Sermon on the Mount: An Evangelical Exposition of Matthew 5--7* (1978) provides rigorous exegetical foundations; Dietrich Bonhoeffer, whose *The Cost of Discipleship* (1937) is one of the most important Christian books of the twentieth century; and Martyn Lloyd-Jones, whose *Studies in the Sermon on the Mount* (1959--60) remains a landmark of Reformed pastoral exposition. The position is also broadly held in mainline Protestantism, in Catholic moral theology since Vatican II, and in many Orthodox catechetical traditions.

### Core Claim

The Sermon on the Mount describes the ethical standard for all who enter the kingdom of God. Its commands are binding on every believer in every age. They are to be obeyed --- genuinely, earnestly, practically --- but in the power of the Holy Spirit and within the "already/not-yet" framework of kingdom theology. Christians *strive* toward the Sermon's standard, empowered by grace, knowing that perfect fulfillment awaits the consummation of the kingdom. Failure to obey is sin to be repented of, not a proof that the commands were never meant for us.

### Strongest Arguments

**1. Matthew 5:17-20 explicitly says the law continues --- and intensifies.**

> "Think not that I am come to destroy the law, or the prophets: I am not come to destroy, but to fulfil. For verily I say unto you, Till heaven and earth pass, one jot or one tittle shall in no wise pass from the law, till all be fulfilled."
> --- Matthew 5:17-18 (KJV)

The word *πληρόω* ("fulfil") does not mean "abolish" or "complete so as to set aside." It means to fill full, to bring to its intended meaning. Jesus fills the law with its deepest significance --- not anger merely but contempt, not adultery merely but lust, not oath-keeping merely but transparent honesty. The Sermon does not discard the Old Testament ethic; it deepens and extends it.

**2. The Great Commission universalizes the Sermon.** This argument applies here with the same force it carries for View 1. "Teaching them to observe all things whatsoever I have commanded you" (Matt 28:20) includes the Sermon. The Commission is given to the apostles for "all nations" --- Jew and Gentile, first century and twenty-first.

**3. Paul echoes Sermon teaching throughout his epistles, showing the church applied it.**

> "Bless them which persecute you: bless, and curse not... Recompense to no man evil for evil... If it be possible, as much as lieth in you, live peaceably with all men... Be not overcome of evil, but overcome evil with good."
> --- Romans 12:14, 17, 18, 21 (KJV)

Romans 12 reads like a systematic application of the Sermon on the Mount to Gentile church life. Paul does not say "this was for Israel" --- he says "do it." James similarly echoes the Sermon: not swearing oaths (James 5:12), the danger of anger (James 1:19-20), the call to be doers and not hearers only (James 1:22-25). The apostolic church universally applied the Sermon to all believers.

**4. The Beatitudes describe character, not just rules.**

> "Blessed are the poor in spirit: for theirs is the kingdom of heaven."
> --- Matthew 5:3 (KJV)

> "Blessed are the merciful: for they shall obtain mercy."
> --- Matthew 5:7 (KJV)

> "Blessed are the peacemakers: for they shall be called the children of God."
> --- Matthew 5:9 (KJV)

The Beatitudes are not a checklist of actions. They describe the kind of person who belongs to the kingdom --- poor in spirit, meek, hungry for righteousness, merciful, pure in heart, peacemaking. This is character shaped by the Spirit, not mere rule-following. The Kingdom Ethics view holds that the Sermon addresses the whole person --- heart, habit, and practice --- and that the Spirit makes this transformation possible, even if not yet complete.

**5. "Be ye therefore perfect" is an eschatological trajectory, not an impossible demand.**

> "Be ye therefore perfect, even as your Father which is in heaven is perfect."
> --- Matthew 5:48 (KJV)

The word *τέλειος* means "complete," "mature," "having reached its end (*τέλος*)." The parallel in Luke's Sermon on the Plain reads:

> "Be ye therefore merciful, as your Father also is merciful."
> --- Luke 6:36 (KJV)

The call is to grow toward the Father's character --- a trajectory, not a threshold. Christians are being made perfect (Heb 10:14), are pressing toward the mark (Phil 3:14), and will one day be complete (1 John 3:2). The Sermon sets the direction; grace sustains the journey.

### Pastoral Fruit

- Balanced discipleship that holds together grace and obedience without collapsing into either legalism or license
- Active engagement with the world: Christians as salt and light in every sphere of life, not withdrawing from culture but transforming it
- Honest acknowledgment of failure without excusing disobedience --- the Sermon remains the standard even when we fall short
- A strong emphasis on the work of the Holy Spirit as the power for ethical living, not mere willpower

### Honest Challenges

**The danger of "cheap grace."** Bonhoeffer's famous critique applies here with particular force. If the Kingdom Ethics view affirms the Sermon as binding while acknowledging that we will never perfectly obey it, the practical result can be comfortable disobedience dressed in theological sophistication. "Of course the Sermon is for us --- but of course we can't fully obey it" can become an excuse for not trying. The Anabaptist challenge stands: at what point does "striving in the Spirit" become indistinguishable from not obeying?

**Where is the line between Spirit-empowered striving and excuse-making?** A man who loses his temper may say, "I'm growing in grace." A church that never practices enemy-love may say, "We're on a trajectory." The Sermon demands concrete action --- turning the cheek, giving the cloak, going the second mile. When these remain perpetually aspirational and never actual, something has gone wrong.

**The "already/not-yet" framework can defer obedience indefinitely.** If the Sermon will only be fully obeyed in the consummated kingdom, and if we are still in the "not yet," there is always a theological reason why we have not yet arrived. The Anabaptist tradition asks a pointed question: how many centuries of "not yet" before we admit that the problem is not eschatological timing but simple disobedience?

## View 3: The Law's Mirror / Impossible Ideal (Lutheran Emphasis)

### Held By

Confessional Lutherans are the primary advocates of this position. It is rooted in Martin Luther's own treatment of the Sermon on the Mount, particularly in his 1530--32 commentary and in his distinction between the "two kingdoms" --- the spiritual kingdom (where the Sermon governs the Christian's inner disposition before God) and the civil kingdom (where the magistrate's sword, the law court, and civic duty operate by different principles). Gerhard Forde, a 20th-century Lutheran theologian, articulated the law-gospel distinction with particular force. Dietrich Bonhoeffer straddles Views 2 and 3 --- he took the Sermon as a genuine command (*The Cost of Discipleship*) but also affirmed the Lutheran insight that the law reveals our inability and drives us to grace.

### Core Claim

The Sermon's demands are intentionally impossible. They are not impossible because of bad translation or misunderstanding --- they are impossible because they are designed to do what the law always does: reveal the depth of human sin, crush the pretension of self-righteousness, and drive the hearer to absolute dependence on Christ. The Sermon is the law at its most intense. "Be ye therefore perfect" is not an achievable goal but a hammer that shatters every human claim to moral sufficiency. The one who truly hears the Sermon should not walk away thinking "I can do this" but rather "God, have mercy on me, a sinner."

### Strongest Arguments

**1. "Be ye therefore perfect" --- who can do this?**

> "Be ye therefore perfect, even as your Father which is in heaven is perfect."
> --- Matthew 5:48 (KJV)

If the command is to be as perfect as God, then by definition no human being can obey it. The Sermon does not set a high bar --- it sets an infinite one. If anger is murder (5:21-22), lust is adultery (5:27-28), and God-like perfection is the standard (5:48), then every human being who has ever lived stands utterly condemned by the Sermon's own terms. This is precisely the point: the law's purpose is to convict.

**2. The Sermon must be read alongside Paul's teaching on the law.**

> "Therefore by the deeds of the law there shall no flesh be justified in his sight: for by the law is the knowledge of sin."
> --- Romans 3:20 (KJV)

> "Wherefore the law was our schoolmaster to bring us unto Christ, that we might be justified by faith."
> --- Galatians 3:24 (KJV)

Paul is clear: the law's function is not to provide a path to righteousness but to reveal sin and point toward grace. The Sermon is the law *par excellence*. If the law cannot justify, and if the Sermon is the most demanding expression of the law ever spoken, then the Sermon's primary function is not behavioral instruction but theological revelation --- it shows us who God is and how far short we fall.

**3. Luther's "two kingdoms" theology provides a practical framework.** Luther argued that the Christian lives simultaneously in two realms. In the spiritual realm --- the Christian's relationship with God --- the Sermon governs completely: we forgive, we do not resist evil, we love our enemies. In the civil realm --- the Christian's responsibilities in society --- different principles apply. A Christian judge does not "turn the other cheek" when sentencing a criminal; a Christian soldier does not refuse to defend the helpless. The Sermon governs the *heart's disposition* before God; it does not dictate the mechanics of every civic role.

**4. The Sermon itself assumes human inability.** The Beatitudes begin with "poor in spirit" --- those who have nothing to offer, who come to God empty-handed. The Lord's Prayer includes "forgive us our debts" --- an admission that even those who pray this prayer will sin against God. The Sermon's own internal logic points to human dependence on grace, not to human moral capability.

**5. Historical continuity.** The Lutheran emphasis on law and gospel has produced centuries of Christian preaching that takes sin seriously, exalts grace magnificently, and protects the conscience of believers who know they have failed. This is not a tradition of lax ethics but of profound theological seriousness about the gap between God's holiness and human capacity.

### Pastoral Fruit

- A profound appreciation for grace that protects against legalism, spiritual pride, and the crushing burden of perfectionism
- Honesty about human failure --- the Sermon exposes what we are, not just what we should be
- A robust doctrine of justification by faith alone that keeps Christ (not human effort) at the center of the Christian life
- Protection of the conscience: the believer who has failed does not conclude that Christianity has failed, but that Christ's grace is sufficient

### Honest Challenges

**Can this view lead to functional antinomianism?** If the Sermon is primarily a mirror of human inability rather than a mandate for human action, the practical result can be that Christians do not actually try to obey it. "The Sermon wasn't really meant to be obeyed" --- even if no Lutheran theologian puts it that way --- can become the lived reality. James confronts this directly:

> "But be ye doers of the word, and not hearers only, deceiving your own selves."
> --- James 1:22 (KJV)

> "Even so faith, if it hath not works, is dead, being alone."
> --- James 2:17 (KJV)

**Jesus's own language is imperative, not merely diagnostic.** "Love your enemies" (5:44) is a command, not a mirror. "Pray for them which despitefully use you" (5:44) is an instruction to be followed, not merely a standard to be admired. Treating the Sermon primarily as law's mirror requires explaining why Jesus used imperative verbs if he did not intend obedience.

**The "two kingdoms" distinction can become a license for double ethics.** If the Christian is non-resistant in the spiritual realm but participates in violence in the civil realm, the Anabaptist critique applies: which kingdom does Jesus actually rule? Luther himself acknowledged the tension; his followers have not always navigated it well. The history of Lutheran state churches cooperating with unjust political power --- most devastatingly in the German church's accommodation of National Socialism --- raises painful questions about where the two-kingdoms framework leads in practice. (It must be noted, however, that Bonhoeffer --- himself a Lutheran --- recognized this failure and paid for his resistance with his life.)

**Does treating the Sermon as "impossible ideal" undercut Jesus's authority as a teacher?** If a teacher gives assignments that no student can complete, the purpose of the assignment may be to teach humility --- but the students are not wrong to ask whether the teacher actually wants the work done. Jesus presents himself in the Sermon as a teacher who expects to be obeyed (Matt 7:24-27). To reframe his commands as primarily diagnostic rather than prescriptive is to answer a question Jesus did not ask.

## View 4: Future Kingdom / Primarily for Israel (Classical Dispensational)

### Held By

This view is associated with classical dispensationalism, particularly the tradition flowing from John Nelson Darby through C.I. Scofield (whose *Scofield Reference Bible* notes were enormously influential), Lewis Sperry Chafer (*Systematic Theology*, 1948), and Charles Ryrie (*Dispensationalism Today*, 1965). The Dallas Theological Seminary tradition historically leaned this direction. Darby himself stated the position plainly: "I affirm then that the sermon on the mount was addressed to the disciples in their then state; and I should think it very natural that it should be so. But their then state was not that of the church, but very far indeed from it" --- while immediately adding, "Now I fully admit that the directions in the sermon on the mount are a guide to us" (*Collected Writings* 8:53, 51, quoted in Wiley, *JODT* 23 [2019]: 11--12).

Two historical qualifications keep this section honest. First, the tradition was never monolithic: Wiley's survey documents that "at least four major dispensational theologians in the early days of dispensational thinking --- Gray, Gaebelein, Ironside, and Walvoord... did not hold to a stereotyped 'Kingdom View' of the Sermon" (*JODT* 23 [2019]: 36) --- Gaebelein called the exclusively-Jewish reading "a false interpretation" as early as 1903 (p. 18). Second, among modern dispensationalists the strict reading has receded: "Actually, the kingdom view is probably a minority position among modern dispensationalists" (John Martin, quoted in Wiley, p. 36), and **progressive dispensationalists** --- including Craig Blaising, Darrell Bock, and Robert Saucy --- align closer to View 2, holding that the Sermon has direct ethical relevance for the church ("there are few who would argue for a strict kingdom interpretation" --- Saucy, quoted in Wiley, p. 36).

### Core Claim

The Sermon on the Mount was addressed to Jewish hearers in a Jewish context about the coming Messianic kingdom. It is not the church's direct rule of life; the church operates under the Pauline epistles and the "dispensation of grace." To place the church under the Sermon on the Mount is to confuse Israel and the church, law and grace, the kingdom program and the church program. Chafer stated the framework in its classic form:

> "The Bible provides three complete and wholly independent rules for human conduct---one for the past age... which is known as the Mosaic Law and is crystallized in the Decalogue; one for the future age of the kingdom which is crystallized in the Sermon on the Mount; and one for the present age which appears in the Gospel by John, the Acts, and the Epistles of the New Testament."
> --- Lewis Sperry Chafer, "The Teachings of Christ Incarnate," *Bibliotheca Sacra* 108 (1951), pp. 392--93

The key distinction is between *primary* and *secondary* application: "To give this discourse a primary application to the Church means that it is made to be, word for word, the rule of life prescribed for the child of God under grace. A secondary application to the Church means that lessons and principles may be drawn from it" (Chafer, p. 392). Scofield granted the secondary application warmly --- "there is a beautiful moral application to the Christian" (*Scofield Reference Bible*, quoted in Chafer, pp. 399--400) --- while denying the primary one.

But it is important to present this view accurately: classical dispensationalism is a **family** of readings, not one claim. The wing represented by Scofield, Chafer, and Rand assigns the Sermon primarily to the millennial kingdom --- "The Sermon on the Mount presents God's standards for Israel's entrance into the long anticipated Messianic kingdom and the rules for conduct of life which will obtain during that age" (Rand, *BSac* 112 [1955]: 28), with Rand extending it also to the tribulation period (p. 38). A second wing rejects the millennial-constitution reading outright --- from inside the tradition. J. Dwight Pentecost, writing in Dallas Seminary's own journal:

> "We thus conclude that the Sermon on the Mount cannot be made to apply to conditions on the earth after the establishment of the kingdom... this Sermon on the Mount is to be connected with the offer of the kingdom, rather than with the description of the kingdom or the kingdom age itself."
> --- J. Dwight Pentecost, "The Purpose of the Sermon on the Mount," *Bibliotheca Sacra* 115 (1958), pp. 134--35

Toussaint likewise: "This view has some very serious difficulties. The greatest is seen in the many references throughout the discourse to conditions which are incongruous to the millennial kingdom" (quoted in Wiley, *JODT* 23 [2019]: 33). What unites the family is only the negative claim --- the Sermon's *primary* address is not the church --- while its positive assignment (millennial constitution, kingdom offer, tribulation ethics) is disputed among its own advocates. As Wiley's historical survey concludes, "to present a 'dispensational view' on the Sermon in one blanket statement is a misrepresentation of the complexities of the development of dispensational thought" (*JODT* 23 [2019]: 36--37).

### Strongest Arguments

**1. The original audience was Jewish.** Matthew's Gospel is the most Jewish of the four Gospels. The Sermon is addressed to disciples who were ethnically Jewish, living under the Mosaic law, in a Jewish theocratic context. Jesus says:

> "Think not that I am come to destroy the law, or the prophets."
> --- Matthew 5:17 (KJV)

This presupposes hearers who live under the law and the prophets --- an Israelite audience. The references to the Sanhedrin (5:22), the temple altar (5:23-24), Jewish courts (5:25), and the swearing of oaths by Jerusalem and God's throne (5:34-35) all presuppose a Jewish social and religious context that does not directly apply to the Gentile church.

**2. "Kingdom of heaven" is a Jewish concept.** The phrase *basileia tōn ouranōn* ("kingdom of heaven") appears only in Matthew and is widely recognized as a Jewish circumlocution --- "heaven" standing for "God" --- reflecting Jewish reverence for the divine name. The dispensationalist argues that "kingdom of heaven" refers specifically to the Messianic kingdom promised to Israel in the Old Testament prophets (Daniel 2:44, Isaiah 9:6-7, Jeremiah 23:5-6), which will be established at Christ's second coming. The Sermon describes the ethics of that future kingdom.

**3. The Sermon presupposes a theocratic context.** Commands about bringing gifts to the altar (5:23-24), about disputes settled by Jewish courts (5:25-26), and about the tradition of the elders (5:21, 27, 33, 38, 43) assume a functioning Jewish social order. The Gentile church does not bring gifts to a temple altar or appear before the Sanhedrin. The dispensationalist reads these as evidence that the Sermon's primary address is to Israel. McClain worked this out in detail, finding all three traditional elements of the Mosaic law present in the Sermon --- moral, ceremonial (the altar-gift, 5:23-24), and civil (the judgment, the council, 5:21-22) --- and concluding that "the so-called Sermon on the Mount is an interpretation of the same one Mosaic law, in part, and with special reference to its original inner meaning... He is reaffirming in the strongest kind of language the unity and inviolability of the Mosaic law" (*BSac* 110 [1953]: 337--39). Note that McClain's version of the argument corroborates this study's Chapter 1 reading of Matt 5:17-19 (intensification, not abolition) --- from the strictest dispensationalist quoted in this chapter.

**4. Paul, not Jesus, is the apostle to the Gentiles.**

> "For I speak to you Gentiles, inasmuch as I am the apostle of the Gentiles, I magnify mine office."
> --- Romans 11:13 (KJV)

If Paul is specifically commissioned as the apostle to the Gentile church, then his epistles --- not the Sermon on the Mount --- are the church's direct ethical instruction. The Sermon is "for us" in the sense that all Scripture is profitable (2 Tim 3:16), but it is not "to us" in the way that Romans, Ephesians, and Colossians are.

**5. There is no mention of the cross, resurrection, or Holy Spirit in the Sermon.** The Sermon contains no reference to Christ's atoning death, no call to faith in his resurrection, and no promise of the indwelling Holy Spirit as the power for obedience. These are the defining realities of the church age. Their complete absence from the Sermon, the dispensationalist argues, suggests that the Sermon belongs to a different dispensational context. Chafer's own formulation: "There is in the Sermon on the Mount a recognition of the Father and the Messiah-Son, but no reference will be found to the Holy Spirit... There is no reference to the death of Christ with its redemption, reconciliation, and propitiation values... No enabling power whereby these great demands both in character and conduct may be realized is intimated. It represents a human responsibility" (*BSac* 108 [1951]: 411).

**6. The Sermon's forgiveness is conditional --- the epistles' forgiveness is not.** This was Scofield's and Chafer's lead exhibit, and it deserves to be stated in their own words, because it is the tradition's sharpest single argument:

> "Under the law of the kingdom, for example, no one may hope for forgiveness who has not first forgiven (Mt. 6:12, 14, 15). Under grace the Christian is exhorted to forgive because he is already forgiven (Eph 4:30--32)."
> --- C.I. Scofield, quoted in Chafer, *Bibliotheca Sacra* 108 (1951), pp. 399--400

Matthew 6:14--15 makes God's forgiveness contingent on ours; Ephesians 4:32 grounds our forgiving in God's already-given forgiveness. Chafer presses the point: "It cannot but be recognized that this one portion --- meaning what Christ insists it means --- is directly opposed in principle to the grace ideal as set forth in Ephesians 4:32" (p. 407). He pairs it with the Sermon's three-fold hell-fire warnings (Matt 5:22, 29--30): the believer secure in grace "has no relation to those warnings" (p. 392). And he forecloses selective use: "this entire discourse presents a complete rule of conduct and is not subject to that destructive method of interpretation which accepts one portion of it while rejecting another portion of it" (p. 392) --- if the church takes the Beatitudes, it must also take the conditional forgiveness and the hell-fire; better, on his reading, to assign the whole to its own dispensation.

### Pastoral Fruit

- A strong emphasis on grace and the distinctiveness of the church age, protecting believers from the burden of law-keeping as a means of standing before God
- Clarity about the differences between Israel and the church, which illuminates many otherwise confusing passages of Scripture
- Protection against legalism: the believer's relationship with God is grounded in the finished work of Christ, not in adherence to the Sermon's demands
- An eschatological hope for the kingdom that takes the Old Testament promises to Israel seriously

### Honest Challenges

**The Great Commission is devastating to this view.** Matthew 28:18-20 commands the apostles to teach "all nations" to "observe all things whatsoever I have commanded you." This is the risen Christ speaking, after the cross and resurrection, commissioning Gentile-inclusive ministry. "All things whatsoever I have commanded you" necessarily includes the Sermon on the Mount --- the largest block of Jesus's teaching in Matthew. If the Sermon is not for "all nations," the Great Commission loses a significant portion of its content.

**Paul echoes the Sermon throughout his epistles.** Romans 12:14 ("Bless them which persecute you") echoes Matthew 5:44. Romans 12:17 ("Recompense to no man evil for evil") echoes Matthew 5:39. Romans 12:21 ("overcome evil with good") echoes the entire logic of Matthew 5:38-48. If the Sermon is for Israel and the epistles are for the church, why does Paul apply the Sermon's teaching directly to Gentile believers?

**James echoes the Sermon extensively.** James 5:12 ("But above all things, my brethren, swear not") directly echoes Matthew 5:34-37. James 1:22 ("Be ye doers of the word") echoes Matthew 7:24-27. James 4:11-12 echoes Matthew 7:1-5. Porter documents forty-five parallel statements between James and the Sermon (*BSac* 162 [2005]: 347--52) --- and the irony is worth noting: the fullest case that James built church instruction out of the Sermon appears in *Bibliotheca Sacra*, the flagship journal of the tradition that assigns the Sermon to another dispensation. James, writing to scattered Jewish-Christian congregations, treats the Sermon as directly authoritative for church life.

**The early church applied the Sermon universally.** The Didache, Justin Martyr, Tertullian, Origen, and every known pre-Nicene writer who discusses the Sermon treats its commands as binding on all Christians without any distinction between Israelite and Gentile application. No early church writer --- not one --- reads the Sermon as reserved for a future dispensation. This history is conceded from inside the tradition: Sturz builds his case on the Ante-Nicene citation data ("This attitude carried right on, without a break, into the period of the Ante Nicene Fathers... There was no question in their minds but that the words of the Lord were to be received and acted upon" --- *Grace Journal* 4 [1963]: 13), and Chafer's response to the historical record is not to contest it but to discount tradition as such: "Pure doctrine cannot be guaranteed by following tradition whether it be of Protestantism or of Rome" (*BSac* 108 [1951]: 402).

**The strongest challenges come from inside the tradition itself.** Harry Sturz --- a dispensational scholar at Biola, writing in Grace Theological Seminary's journal --- argued that the kingdom-constitution reading "will not stand under examination. In the first place, it lacks proof," and that "dispensational lines would be easier to defend, have fewer inconsistencies, and might make more converts if this untenable position would be abandoned" (*Grace Journal* 4 [1963]: 7--8). His rebuttal of the offer/tribulation restriction asks how "the unregenerate Jew" could "ever hope to manifest such righteousness in his own strength... This would be sheer legalism" (pp. 9--10) --- turning the law-grace argument back on the view itself. Toussaint pressed the millennium-incongruity problem ("This is indeed a strange portrayal of that kingdom which was pictured by the Old Testament prophets!" --- quoted in Wiley, *JODT* 23 [2019]: 33), and the Hogg--Watson catalog of the Sermon's age-markers (persecution, evil men, false prophets, the Lord absent) is cited by dispensational authors themselves as proof the Sermon does not describe the millennium (Rand, *BSac* 112 [1955]: 35--36; Sturz, pp. 7--8).

**The dispensational tradition has moved --- and not only recently.** Even in the Scofield era the tradition was divided (Gray, Gaebelein, Ironside; Wiley, *JODT* 23 [2019]: 15--23), and among contemporary dispensationalists the kingdom view "is probably a minority position" (Martin, quoted in Wiley, p. 36). The most sophisticated contemporary dispensational scholars (Bock, Blaising, Saucy) hold that the Sermon has direct ethical relevance for the church, even while maintaining other distinctively dispensational convictions. This century-long internal erosion, driven by the tradition's own exegetes, suggests that the "Sermon is for Israel" reading does not hold up under sustained scrutiny.

**On the conditional-forgiveness argument (argument 6):** the mainstream response reads Matthew 6:14--15 not as a legal condition for justification but as a description of the forgiven heart --- the person who will not forgive shows that they have not received forgiveness (cf. the unmerciful servant, Matt 18:21--35, where the servant's unforgiveness proves he never grasped his own release). On this reading the Sermon and Ephesians 4:32 describe the same reality from two directions: grace produces forgiveness, and the absence of forgiveness betrays the absence of grace. The tension Scofield identified is real, but it runs *through the Gospel of Matthew itself* --- the same evangelist who records Matthew 6:14--15 records the Great Commission extending "all things whatsoever I have commanded" to all nations. Whatever solution one adopts must work inside Matthew, not merely between Matthew and Paul.

**The absence of the cross is also true of large portions of the Old Testament.** The Psalms do not mention the cross. Proverbs does not mention the resurrection. The Song of Solomon does not mention the Holy Spirit. Yet no one argues that these books are therefore irrelevant to the church. The argument from silence proves too much.

## Brief Note: Two Minority Positions

### The Medieval Two-Tier View (Counsels of Perfection)

Thomas Aquinas and the broader medieval Catholic tradition distinguished between "precepts" (commands binding on all Christians) and "counsels of perfection" (voluntary renunciations --- poverty, celibacy, obedience --- for those pursuing a higher spiritual vocation). Under this framework, the Sermon's harder commands --- turning the other cheek, selling possessions, not swearing oaths --- were "counsels" directed at monks, friars, and clergy, while ordinary laypeople were bound only to the general moral law.

This view has largely been set aside since Vatican II, which emphasized the "universal call to holiness" --- all believers, not just religious professionals, are called to the fullness of Christian life. The Reformation also rejected the two-tier framework: Luther, Calvin, and the Anabaptists all insisted (for different reasons) that the Sermon addresses every believer, not a spiritual elite.

### The Interim Ethic (Schweitzer)

Albert Schweitzer, in *The Quest of the Historical Jesus* (1906), proposed that Jesus expected the end of the world within his own generation and issued extreme ethical demands appropriate only for that brief interval. The Sermon was not meant to be a permanent moral code but an emergency ethic for the last days. Since the world did not end, the Sermon's commands stand as artifacts of an unrealized expectation.

This view is almost universally rejected by modern scholarship. It presupposes that Jesus was fundamentally mistaken about the future --- a position that most Christian theology cannot accept --- and it fails to account for the fact that the early church continued to apply the Sermon for centuries after Jesus's death without any sense that its commands had expired.

## The Key Pastoral Questions

Two sincere pastoral questions define the shape of this debate, and they deserve to be taken seriously rather than dismissed.

**The dispensationalist asks:** "If the Sermon is for today, does that mean we're under law?"

This is not a frivolous question. The dispensationalist has read Galatians, heard Paul's fierce defense of grace against the Judaizers, and knows that the law cannot justify:

> "Christ is become of no effect unto you, whosoever of you are justified by the law; ye are fallen from grace."
> --- Galatians 5:4 (KJV)

The fear is genuine: if the Sermon is binding on the church, does the church return to a law-keeping religion? The answer from Views 1, 2, and 3 is that obedience to Jesus's commands is not the same as justification by the law. No one is saved by obeying the Sermon on the Mount. But the one who has been saved --- by grace, through faith, not of works --- is called to *walk in newness of life* (Rom 6:4), and the Sermon describes what that walk looks like. Jesus does not give the Sermon as a path to salvation but as the shape of kingdom life for those who have already been welcomed in. Grace and obedience are not enemies.

**The Anabaptist asks:** "If the Sermon is NOT for today, what authority does Jesus's direct teaching have?"

This too is not a frivolous question. If the church can set aside Jesus's longest recorded teaching as "not for us," what remains of Jesus's authority as a teacher? Can the church claim to follow Christ while treating his most sustained ethical instruction as belonging to another people and another age? The Anabaptist recognizes that if the Sermon is optional, the Jesus who spoke it is optional --- and that is a conclusion no Christian can accept.

These two questions --- one about grace, one about authority --- are not contradictory. They are complementary. The answer to both is that the Sermon is the word of the living Christ, spoken in grace and demanding obedience, addressed to all who call him Lord.

## A Parallel with "What Is the Perfect?"

The careful reader will notice a structural parallel with the question of 1 Corinthians 13:10, examined in the companion study "What Is the Perfect?" Just as the cessationist-continuationist debate *can be separated* from the exegesis of what *τὸ τέλειον* means, so the law-grace debate *can be separated* from the question of whether the Sermon on the Mount applies to believers today.

One can affirm justification by grace alone through faith alone --- fully, without qualification --- and still hold that the Sermon's commands are binding on all believers. Stott does this. Bonhoeffer does this. The entire Reformed and evangelical mainstream does this. "The Sermon applies to us" does not mean "the Sermon is how we are justified." These are different questions, and conflating them has caused unnecessary division.

Conversely, one can hold that the Sermon is the church's ethical charter and still respect the dispensationalist's insistence that the church age is distinct from Israel's history. The question is not whether there are differences between dispensations but whether those differences require setting aside the direct commands of Jesus.

## Pastoral Considerations

Across all four views, several things remain constant --- and these are more important for the life of the church than the interpretive question that divides them.

**Every view agrees that Jesus's teaching carries supreme authority.** The Anabaptist, the evangelical, the Lutheran, and the dispensationalist all confess Jesus as Lord. None would say his words can be ignored. They disagree about *how* his words apply, not about *whether* they matter. This shared confession is the ground of unity.

**Every view agrees that the Christian life demands transformation.** Even the Lutheran who reads the Sermon as law's mirror expects that faith in Christ will produce the fruits of the Spirit --- love, joy, peace, patience, kindness, goodness, faithfulness, gentleness, self-control (Gal 5:22-23). Even the dispensationalist who reads the Sermon as kingdom law expects the church to live by Romans 12, which says substantially the same things. The disagreement is about the Sermon's *genre and address*, not about whether Christians should love their enemies, forgive, and pursue righteousness.

**The Sermon itself settles the question of its own importance.** The wise man builds on Jesus's words; the foolish man does not. The Sermon does not present itself as an ideal for a distant age, a mirror for theological reflection, or a constitution for a future kingdom. It presents itself as teaching to be *done*, by those who *hear* it, *now*:

> "Therefore whosoever heareth these sayings of mine, and doeth them, I will liken him unto a wise man, which built his house upon a rock: And the rain descended, and the floods came, and the winds blew, and beat upon that house; and it fell not: for it was founded upon a rock."
> --- Matthew 7:24-25 (KJV)

The reader who loves the Sermon and strives to obey it --- whether through View 1's literal practice, View 2's Spirit-empowered kingdom ethic, View 3's grace-driven repentance and renewal, or even View 4's analogical application --- is closer to the Sermon's own intent than the reader who admires it from a theological distance.

> "Beloved, let us love one another: for love is of God; and every one that loveth is born of God, and knoweth God."
> --- 1 John 4:7 (KJV)

## Summary

The four views examined in this chapter represent genuinely different ways of reading the Sermon on the Mount, and each carries real consequences for how Christians live. The Anabaptist tradition (View 1) takes the Sermon at its most direct and produces communities whose radical obedience has challenged the entire church. The Kingdom Ethics view (View 2) holds the broadest consensus and balances grace with genuine moral demand. The Lutheran emphasis (View 3) protects against the legalism that can result from turning the Sermon into a code of conduct, even as it risks reducing the Sermon to a diagnostic tool. The classical dispensational view (View 4) raises the genuine question of dispensational context but faces formidable challenges from the Great Commission, Paul's echoes of the Sermon, and the unanimous testimony of the early church.

A thoughtful reader who weighs these four positions should notice that the weight of evidence --- exegetical, historical, and pastoral --- falls decisively on the conviction that the Sermon is for all believers in every age. Views 1 and 2 share this conviction. Views 3 and 4 create varying degrees of distance from it. But even the most honest advocates of Views 3 and 4 do not say the Sermon is *irrelevant* --- they say it functions differently than Views 1 and 2 suppose. The question is not *whether* the Sermon matters but *how*.

The one thing all four views share, and the one thing the Sermon itself insists upon, is that hearing alone is not enough. The Sermon demands a response. Whatever one's theological framework, the wisest response to the words of Jesus is the simplest: *do them*.
