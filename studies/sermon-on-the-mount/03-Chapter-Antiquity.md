# Chapter 3: Antiquity --- How the Earliest Christians Read the Sermon

## Why the Early Church Matters

The Christians of the first three centuries read the Sermon on the Mount not as ancient literature but as a living word addressed to them. Their reading of it carries special weight for a simple reason: they were closer to the source. Many of them knew the apostles or knew teachers who had. They practiced their faith without state protection and often under direct threat of persecution. When they read "Blessed are ye, when men shall revile you, and persecute you" (Matt 5:11), they were not reading theory --- they were reading biography.

**The pre-Nicene cutoff (325 AD)** marks the Council of Nicaea and the beginning of the church-state alliance under Constantine. Before that date, Christians were a minority under suspicion, sometimes under sentence of death. This matters enormously for our question. The Sermon's hardest commands --- love your enemies, do not resist evil, do not swear oaths, give to those who ask --- cost something real when you have no political power. The pre-Nicene church lived the Sermon under conditions remarkably similar to those of the original audience on the mountainside.

**A critical observation:** The Sermon on the Mount is the most-quoted New Testament text in the ante-Nicene period. Matthew is the most frequently cited book in the Ante-Nicene Fathers, and "the most frequently used portion of Matthew is that portion (chapters 5--7) now called 'The Sermon on the Mount'" --- a datum reported not by a critic of dispensationalism but by the dispensational scholar Harry Sturz (*Grace Journal* 4 [1963]: 3). Unlike some passages where early evidence is sparse, here the patristic testimony is abundant. The Didache, the earliest surviving church manual, opens with Sermon material. Justin Martyr presented the Sermon to the Roman emperor as what Christians actually practice. Tertullian refused military service on the basis of its commands. We are not reconstructing a faint signal --- we are listening to a chorus.

**The fathers are witnesses, not authorities.** When they agree, their agreement is significant. When they conflict with Scripture clearly understood, Scripture takes priority. This chapter presents their testimony; the reader weighs it.

---

## Part One: The Apostolic Fathers (c. 70--150 AD)

### The Didache (c. 70--100 AD)

**Context:** The *Didache* ("Teaching of the Twelve Apostles") is one of the earliest surviving Christian documents outside the New Testament, probably originating in Syria. It functions as a church manual covering moral instruction, baptism, fasting, prayer, and the Eucharist. Its opening section --- the "Two Ways" teaching (chapters 1--6) --- echoes the Sermon on the Mount so extensively that some scholars consider it the earliest surviving catechism built on Jesus's teaching in Matthew 5--7.

Didache 1:2--4 reads:

> "The way of life is this. First of all, thou shalt love the God that made thee; secondly, thy neighbour as thyself. And all things whatsoever thou wouldest not have befal thyself neither do thou unto another. Now of these words the doctrine is this. Bless them that curse you, and pray for your enemies and fast for them that persecute you; for what thank is it, if ye love them that love you? Do not even the Gentiles the same? But do ye love them that hate you, and ye shall not have an enemy. Abstain thou from fleshly and bodily lusts. If any man give thee a blow on thy right cheek, turn to him the other also, and thou shalt be perfect; if a man impress thee to go with him one mile, go with him twain; if a man take away thy cloak, give him thy coat also; if a man take away from thee that which is thine own, ask it not back, for neither art thou able."
> --- *Didache* 1:2--4 (Lightfoot translation)

The parallels are unmistakable. "Bless them that curse you" echoes Matthew 5:44. "If any man give thee a blow on thy right cheek, turn to him the other also" is nearly verbatim from Matthew 5:39. "If a man impress thee to go with him one mile, go with him twain" reproduces Matthew 5:41. The phrase "and thou shalt be perfect" (*kai esē teleios*) echoes Matthew 5:48: "Be ye therefore perfect, even as your Father which is in heaven is perfect."

**Significance:** The Didache uses the Sermon on the Mount as catechetical instruction --- teaching given to new converts as preparation for baptism. This is not homiletical exhortation to an advanced spiritual elite. It is basic Christian formation. The earliest church manual we possess treats the Sermon's most difficult commands --- non-retaliation, love of enemies, generosity under compulsion --- as the standard expected of every baptized Christian. The commands are presented as imperative, not advisory; as present obligation, not future ideal.

---

### Clement of Rome (c. 96 AD), 1 Clement

**Context:** Clement of Rome wrote to the Corinthian church around 96 AD, making this one of the earliest post-apostolic documents we possess. In chapter 13, Clement exhorts the Corinthians to humility and cites a cluster of Jesus's sayings that draw heavily on Sermon on the Mount material.

1 Clement 13:1--2 reads:

> "Let us therefore, brethren, be of humble mind, laying aside all haughtiness, and pride, and foolishness, and angry feelings; and let us act according to that which is written (for the Holy Spirit saith, 'Let not the wise man glory in his wisdom, neither let the mighty man glory in his might, neither let the rich man glory in his riches; but let him that glorieth glory in the Lord, in diligently seeking Him, and doing judgment and righteousness'), being especially mindful of the words of the Lord Jesus which He spake, teaching us meekness and long-suffering. For thus He spoke: 'Be ye merciful, that ye may obtain mercy; forgive, that it may be forgiven to you; as ye do, so shall it be done unto you; as ye judge, so shall ye be judged; as ye are kind, so shall kindness be shown to you; with what measure ye mete, with the same it shall be measured to you.'"
> --- 1 Clement 13:1-2 (Roberts-Donaldson translation, ANF Vol. 1)

The echoes are clear: "Be ye merciful, that ye may obtain mercy" parallels Matthew 5:7 ("Blessed are the merciful: for they shall obtain mercy"). "Forgive, that it may be forgiven to you" echoes Matthew 6:14. "As ye judge, so shall ye be judged; with what measure ye mete, with the same it shall be measured to you" is a close paraphrase of Matthew 7:1--2.

**Significance:** Clement introduces this collection of Jesus's sayings as "the words of the Lord Jesus which He spake, teaching us meekness and long-suffering." The pronoun *us* is crucial. Clement does not present the Sermon as teaching addressed to a past audience, a future dispensation, or a spiritual elite. He presents it as teaching addressed to the church in his own day. He applies it to a pastoral crisis --- division in the Corinthian church --- as a present, binding standard of behavior.

---

### Polycarp, To the Philippians (c. 110--130 AD)

**Context:** Polycarp was bishop of Smyrna and, according to Irenaeus, a disciple of the Apostle John. His letter to the Philippians is one of the few surviving documents from a man with direct apostolic connection.

Polycarp 2:3 reads:

> "Being mindful of what the Lord said in His teaching: 'Judge not, that ye be not judged; forgive, and it shall be forgiven unto you; be merciful, that ye may obtain mercy; with what measure ye mete, it shall be measured to you again'; and once more, 'Blessed are the poor, and those that are persecuted for righteousness' sake, for theirs is the kingdom of God.'"
> --- Polycarp, *To the Philippians* 2:3 (Roberts-Donaldson translation, ANF Vol. 1)

**Significance:** Polycarp, a man within one generation of the apostles, quotes the Beatitudes and the Sermon's teaching on judgment and mercy as "what the Lord said in His teaching" and applies it to his Christian audience as normative instruction. Like Clement, he treats the Sermon as present-tense direction for believers, not as a description of a future age or an impossible ideal.

---

### Justin Martyr, First Apology (c. 155 AD)

**Context:** Justin Martyr's *First Apology* is addressed to the Roman Emperor Antoninus Pius and his court. It is an apologetic work --- a defense of Christian faith and practice before the highest civil authority. In chapters 15--17, Justin quotes extensively from the Sermon on the Mount, presenting it to the emperor as what Christians actually do. This is a uniquely important witness: Justin is not preaching to Christians but explaining Christianity to outsiders, and he does so by citing the Sermon as current practice.

From *First Apology* 15--16:

> "Concerning chastity, He uttered such sentiments as these: 'Whosoever looketh upon a woman to lust after her, hath committed adultery with her already in his heart before God.' And, 'If thy right eye offend thee, cut it out; for it is better for thee to enter into the kingdom of heaven with one eye, than, having two eyes, to be cast into everlasting fire.' ... And, 'Whosoever shall marry her that is divorced from another husband, committeth adultery.' ... And, 'Swear not at all; but let your yea be yea, and your nay, nay; for whatsoever is more than these cometh of evil.' And, 'If ye love them that love you, what new thing do ye? for even fornicators do this. But I say unto you, Pray for your enemies, and love them that hate you, and bless them that curse you, and pray for them that despitefully use you.'"
> --- Justin Martyr, *First Apology* 15--16 (Marcus Dods and George Reith translation, ANF Vol. 1; quotations condensed from chs. 15--16, see full text for complete sequence)

Justin then explains to the emperor:

> "...we who formerly delighted in fornication, but now embrace chastity alone; we who formerly used magical arts, dedicate ourselves to the good and unbegotten God; we who valued above all things the acquisition of wealth and possessions, now bring what we have into a common stock, and communicate to every one in need; we who hated and destroyed one another, and on account of their different manners would not live with men of a different tribe, now, since the coming of Christ, live familiarly with them, and pray for our enemies, and endeavour to persuade those who hate us unjustly to live conformably to the good precepts of Christ, to the end that they may become partakers with us of the same joyful hope of a reward from God the ruler of all."
> --- Justin Martyr, *First Apology* 14 (Marcus Dods and George Reith translation, ANF Vol. 1)

**Significance:** Justin's testimony is extraordinary for two reasons. First, he presents the Sermon on the Mount to an emperor as a description of actual Christian behavior --- "we who formerly hated ... now pray for our enemies." This is not aspirational language; it is apologetic: Justin is claiming that Christians really do these things, and he is staking his case for Christianity's legitimacy on that claim. Second, he does not qualify the Sermon's commands as intended for a different audience or a future age. He presents them as the "commands of Christ" that Christians currently live by. Writing only about a century after Jesus spoke the Sermon, Justin treats it as binding on all believers in his own time.

## Part Two: The Ante-Nicene Fathers (c. 150--325 AD)

### Irenaeus of Lyon (c. 130--202 AD), Against Heresies

**Context:** Irenaeus was bishop of Lyon, a disciple of Polycarp (who was a disciple of John), making his theological genealogy as direct as any post-apostolic writer's. In *Against Heresies* 4.13, Irenaeus addresses the relationship between the Mosaic law and Christ's teaching. His argument is that Christ did not abolish but "extended and fulfilled" the law --- and the Sermon on the Mount is his primary evidence.

In AH 4.13.1, Irenaeus argues (author's summary of the section's argument, ANF Vol. 1, pp. 477--478): Christ's commands in the Sermon do not relax the law's demands but intensify them. Where the law prohibited murder, Christ prohibited anger. Where the law prohibited adultery, Christ prohibited lust. Where the law required oaths be kept, Christ prohibited oaths entirely. This "extension" proves that the Sermon belongs to the new covenant and is binding on all who receive it. The law was a preparation; the Sermon is the fulfillment. To claim these commands are not for present believers would, in Irenaeus's framework, be to claim that Christ's covenant is less demanding than Moses's --- an absurdity.

**Significance:** Irenaeus reads the Sermon on the Mount as the definitive expression of Christ's new-covenant ethic --- not as an impossible standard, not as a future kingdom program, but as the law of life for Christians now. His argument that the Sermon "extends" the law rules out any reading that would set it aside as impractical or inapplicable. For Irenaeus, the Sermon is the proof that Christ fulfills the law by raising its demands, and the Christian is the one who lives under those raised demands.

---

### Tertullian of Carthage (c. 155--240 AD)

**Context:** Tertullian is the most vigorous ante-Nicene voice for the literal obedience reading of the Sermon. A North African lawyer turned theologian, he applies the Sermon's commands to specific practical questions --- military service, oaths, non-retaliation, adornment --- with a precision that reveals how seriously the early church took these texts as binding.

**On oaths and military service (*De Corona* 11, *De Idololatria* 19):**

In *De Corona* ("On the Chaplet"), Tertullian argues that Christians cannot serve in the military. His reasoning draws directly on the Sermon:

> "Shall it be held lawful to make an occupation of the sword, when the Lord proclaims that he who uses the sword shall perish by the sword? And shall the son of peace take part in the battle when it does not become him even to sue at law? And shall he apply the chain, and the prison, and the torture, and the punishment, who is not the avenger even of his own wrongs?"
> --- Tertullian, *De Corona* 11 (S. Thelwall translation, ANF Vol. 3)

In *De Idololatria* 19, Tertullian addresses the question directly:

> "But now inquiry is made about this point, whether a believer may turn himself unto military service, and whether the military may be admitted unto the faith. ... the Lord afterward, in disarming Peter, unbelted every soldier."
> --- Tertullian, *De Idololatria* 19 (S. Thelwall translation, ANF Vol. 3)

**On non-retaliation (*De Patientia* 8):**

> "If one attempt to provoke you by manual violence, the monition of the Lord is at hand: 'To him,' He saith, 'who smiteth thee on the face, turn the other cheek likewise.' Let outrageousness be wearied out by your patience. Whatever that blow may be, conjoined with pain and contumely, it shall receive a heavier one from the Lord."
> --- Tertullian, *De Patientia* 8 (S. Thelwall translation, ANF Vol. 3)

**Significance:** Tertullian reads the Sermon on the Mount as literal, binding law for Christians. His application is thorough and specific: no military service (because the Sermon forbids violence and retaliation), no oaths (because Christ said "swear not at all"), no reprisal even when physically assaulted. He does not treat these commands as counsel for the unusually devout. He treats them as the basic obligations of every baptized believer. Tertullian is particularly important because he was a trained lawyer --- his readings are precise and deliberate, not naive or sentimental. He understood the social cost of these positions and accepted it.

---

### Clement of Alexandria (c. 150--215 AD), Stromata and Paedagogus

**Context:** Clement of Alexandria was head of the Catechetical School of Alexandria. His works --- especially the *Paedagogus* ("The Instructor") and the *Stromata* ("Miscellanies") --- present the ethical teaching of Jesus, including the Sermon on the Mount, as moral instruction for the educated Christian.

In *Paedagogus* 3.12, Clement applies Sermon teaching on simplicity and detachment (author's summary of Clement's argument in this section; the ANF Vol. 2 translation of Clement is often paraphrase rather than strict rendering): Christ's instructions about not storing up treasure on earth, about the lilies of the field, and about simplicity in dress and food are binding moral guidance for Christians. Wealth is not evil in itself, but the Christian must hold it loosely and use it generously --- this is what the Sermon requires.

In *Stromata* 4.6, Clement discusses the Beatitudes as a progression of Christian virtue, culminating in the peacemakers:

> "They also are peacemakers, who teach those who war against the stratagems of sin to have recourse to faith and peace."
> --- Clement, *Stromata* 4.6 (William Wilson translation, ANF Vol. 2)

**Significance:** Clement is the most "philosophical" of the ante-Nicene readers of the Sermon. He does not reject the literal application but tends to interpret the Sermon's commands at a moral and spiritual level appropriate to his educated Alexandrian audience. His reading is gentler than Tertullian's --- less legal, more pedagogical --- but the conclusion is the same: the Sermon is addressed to Christians now, and its commands are to be followed. Clement does not suggest the Sermon belongs to a future dispensation or a different audience.

---

### Origen of Alexandria (c. 185--254 AD)

**Context:** Origen is the most prolific and most philosophically sophisticated of the ante-Nicene fathers. His *Commentary on Matthew* is the most sustained ante-Nicene exegesis of the Sermon on the Mount. The commentary survives partly in Greek (for the later chapters of Matthew) and partly in an anonymous Latin translation; for the Sermon itself, we depend primarily on fragments preserved in catena manuscripts and on Origen's discussions of Sermon material in other works such as *De Principiis*, *Contra Celsum*, and the *Commentary on Romans*. (Note: the fragmentary state of Origen's Matthean commentary means we are reconstructing his views on the Sermon from multiple sources, not reading a continuous text.)

**On the love of enemies (*Contra Celsum* 8.73):**

In his apology against the pagan philosopher Celsus, Origen addresses the charge that Christians are unpatriotic because they refuse military service. His defense is grounded in the Sermon:

> "To those enemies of our faith who require us to bear arms for the commonwealth, and to slay men, we can reply: 'Do not those who are priests at certain shrines ... keep their hands free from blood, that they may with hands unstained and free from human blood offer the appointed sacrifices to your gods?' ... And we do take our part in public affairs, when along with righteous prayers we join self-denying exercises and meditations, which teach us to despise pleasures, and not to be led away by them."
> --- Origen, *Contra Celsum* 8.73 (Crombie translation, ANF Vol. 4)

Though this passage comes from Book 8 rather than the Sermon commentary directly, it reveals Origen's hermeneutical posture: the Sermon's commands on non-violence are not a future ideal but the basis for present Christian refusal of military service. In his commentary fragments on Matthew 5:39 (the "turn the other cheek" passage), Origen allows for a deeper, spiritual layer of meaning --- the struggle against spiritual enemies --- but he does not use this allegorical reading to cancel the literal one. The Christian is to practice non-retaliation in both senses.

**Significance:** Origen is the father who most consistently holds together the literal and the spiritual senses of the Sermon. He reads the Beatitudes as describing real Christian life and real blessedness, even while discerning in them a deeper spiritual progression. He does not make the allegorical reading a substitute for obedience. In practice, his conclusions align with Tertullian's: Christians do not serve in the military, do not retaliate, and do not take oaths. The Sermon is for Christians now.

## Part Three: The Post-Nicene Witness (for reference, noting the pre-325 boundary)

The SCAR framework places primary weight on pre-Nicene evidence. The following post-Nicene fathers are included because they produced the most extensive surviving commentaries on the Sermon on the Mount, and their readings illuminate the trajectory of the tradition.

---

### John Chrysostom (c. 347--407 AD), Homilies on the Gospel of Matthew

**Context:** Chrysostom's *Homilies on Matthew* (NPNF Series 1, Vol. 10, Prevost translation, revised by Riddle) contain the most detailed verse-by-verse patristic commentary on the Sermon on the Mount. Homilies 15--24 cover Matthew 5--7 comprehensively. Chrysostom was a pastor-preacher, and his homilies apply every command of the Sermon directly to the lives of his congregation.

On Matthew 5:39 ("whosoever shall smite thee on thy right cheek, turn to him the other also"), Chrysostom preaches:

> "'What then?' it is said, 'ought we not to resist the evil one?' Indeed we ought, but not in this way, but as He hath commanded, by giving one's self up to suffer wrongfully; for thus shalt thou prevail over him. For one fire is not quenched by another, but fire by water."
> --- Chrysostom, *Homilies on Matthew* 18.1 (George Prevost translation, revised by M.B. Riddle, NPNF 1.10)

On Matthew 5:44 ("Love your enemies"), Chrysostom writes:

> "Seest thou how many steps He hath ascended, and how He hath set us on the very summit of virtue? Nay, mark it, numbering from the beginning. A first step is, not to begin with injustice: a second, after he hath begun, to vindicate one's self by equal retaliation; a third, not to do unto him that is vexing us the same that one hath suffered, but to be quiet; a fourth, even to give one's self up to suffer wrongfully; a fifth, to give up yet more than the other, who did the wrong, wishes; a sixth, not to hate him who hath done so; a seventh, even to love him; an eighth, to do him good also; a ninth, to entreat God Himself on his behalf."
> --- Chrysostom, *Homilies on Matthew* 18.4 (George Prevost translation, revised by M.B. Riddle, NPNF 1.10)

And on whether such commands are possible at all:

> "Let us not therefore suppose His injunctions impossible. Nay, for besides their expediency, they are very easy, if we are sober-minded; and the profit of them is so great as to be an exceeding help, not to ourselves only, but to those also who are using us despitefully."
> --- Chrysostom, *Homilies on Matthew* 18.2 (George Prevost translation, revised by M.B. Riddle, NPNF 1.10)

On the question of whether the Sermon's commands are achievable, Chrysostom insists that Christ does not command the impossible: "Let us not therefore suppose His injunctions impossible... they are very easy, if we are sober-minded" (18.2). The commands are demanding, but their difficulty is the point: they produce the character Christ intends.

**Significance:** Chrysostom treats the Sermon as universally binding on all believers. He does not carve out exceptions for difficult circumstances, does not relegate the commands to a future age, and does not limit them to a spiritual elite. His pastoral application is relentlessly practical: this is how you must live, and the Christ who commands it gives the grace to do it. That he preaches this to ordinary congregants in Antioch and Constantinople --- not to monks or ascetics --- is significant.

---

### Augustine of Hippo (c. 354--430 AD), De Sermone Domini in Monte

**Context:** Augustine's *De Sermone Domini in Monte* ("On the Lord's Sermon on the Mount," c. 394 AD) is the first full-length treatise devoted exclusively to the Sermon on the Mount. It survives in its entirety, covering every verse of Matthew 5--7 in two books. The NPNF translation is Series 1, Vol. 6 (Findlay translation). Augustine wrote this treatise relatively early in his career; in his later *Retractationes* (2.21), he noted that he would have treated some points differently with greater maturity.

Augustine reads the Beatitudes as ascending stages of the spiritual life:

> "For this reason the number of sentences before us is to be carefully considered. For the beatitudes begin with humility: 'Blessed are the poor in spirit,' i.e. those not puffed up, while the soul submits itself to divine authority, fearing lest after this life it go away to punishment, although perhaps in this life it might seem to itself to be happy."
> --- Augustine, *De Sermone Domini in Monte* 1.3.10 (William Findlay translation, NPNF 1.6)

On the second beatitude:

> "Then, the meek are those who yield to acts of wickedness, and do not resist evil, but overcome evil with good."
> --- Augustine, *De Sermone Domini in Monte* 1.2.4 (William Findlay translation, NPNF 1.6)

Augustine opens the treatise with his estimate of the Sermon's place in Christian life:

> "If any one will piously and soberly consider the sermon which our Lord Jesus Christ spoke on the mount, as we read it in the Gospel according to Matthew, I think that he will find in it, so far as regards the highest morals, a perfect standard of the Christian life... these sayings which He uttered on the mount so perfectly guide the life of those who may be willing to live according to them, that they may justly be compared to one building upon a rock."
> --- Augustine, *De Sermone Domini in Monte* 1.1.1 (William Findlay translation, NPNF 1.6)

(Augustine's insistence that obedience requires divine grace rather than merely human effort is genuinely his --- but it belongs to his wider theology of grace, e.g. *De Natura et Gratia* 43.50, "God does not command impossibilities" --- not to this treatise's opening section.)

**Significance:** Augustine's reading is important for two reasons. First, he treats every verse of the Sermon as addressed to Christians and as requiring obedience. He does not suggest that any portion is intended for a future age or a different audience. Second, he introduces a nuance that would be influential: the Sermon's commands require grace. The Christian does not obey the Sermon by willpower alone but by the power of the Spirit. This becomes the standard Augustinian (and later Protestant) framework: the Sermon is binding, but its demands drive the believer to dependence on Christ. Notably, Augustine later revised some of his readings in the *Retractationes*, making this early work a snapshot rather than his final word --- but even in revision, he did not retract the basic claim that the Sermon addresses all believers.

## Part Four: Patterns and Observations

Having surveyed the evidence from c. 70 AD to c. 430 AD, several clear patterns emerge.

**1. Unanimous application to present believers.**

Not a single writer surveyed in this chapter --- from the Didache to Augustine --- suggests that the Sermon on the Mount is intended for a future dispensation, a past audience, or a different covenant community. Every writer treats the Sermon as addressed to Christians in the present age and as requiring obedience. This unanimity is remarkable and spans the apostolic, ante-Nicene, and post-Nicene periods without exception.

**2. The Sermon was used in catechesis from the very beginning.**

The Didache embeds the Sermon's commands into baptismal instruction. This means that within a generation of the apostles, the church was teaching new converts to follow the Sermon's most difficult commands --- non-retaliation, love of enemies, refusal to swear oaths --- as the basic content of Christian formation, not as an advanced curriculum for spiritual athletes.

**3. The literal reading was the dominant reading before Nicaea.**

Tertullian, Origen, and the Didache all apply the Sermon's commands about non-violence and oaths literally. This had direct practical consequences: the question of Christian military service was a live and contentious issue in the pre-Nicene church, and the Sermon on the Mount was the primary text cited by those who argued Christians could not serve. The literal reading was not a minority position before Constantine; it was the mainstream.

**4. The Constantinian shift changed the conversation.**

After the Edict of Milan (313 AD) and the rise of Christianity as the imperial religion, the practical implications of the literal reading became vastly more complicated. A religion that had taught non-retaliation from a position of powerlessness now had to govern an empire. The post-Nicene shift toward more nuanced and spiritualized readings of the Sermon's hard commands --- while never abandoning the claim that the Sermon addresses all believers --- is difficult to separate from this political transformation.

**5. Allegorical readings supplemented but did not replace literal ones.**

Origen is often cited as the great allegorizer, but even Origen's allegorical readings of the Sermon coexist with his literal application. He reads "turn the other cheek" both as a spiritual struggle against demonic powers and as an instruction for actual non-violence --- and he refused military service. Clement of Alexandria reads the Sermon's teaching on wealth both as inner detachment and as practical generosity. The allegorical and the literal were not in competition in the pre-Nicene period; they were complementary layers.

**6. No writer treats the Sermon as an impossible ideal designed to expose sin.**

The later Protestant reading of the Sermon as "law's mirror" --- the Sermon shows us how far we fall short, driving us to grace --- is not present in the ante-Nicene period. The early fathers believed the Sermon could be obeyed, even if imperfectly and only with divine help. Augustine comes closest to the later Protestant nuance by emphasizing grace as the condition of obedience, but even Augustine does not suggest the commands are given merely to expose failure.

**7. The pre-Nicene church put the Sermon into practice at great cost.**

This is not abstract theology. Early Christians were expelled from the military, refused to testify under oath in court, accepted physical assault without retaliation, and shared their goods with the poor --- all based on their reading of the Sermon. The evidence from Tertullian, Justin, and the Didache is not merely literary; it reflects a community that lived the Sermon's commands and accepted the social consequences.

---

## Summary

The patristic evidence on the Sermon on the Mount is unusually abundant and unusually unified. From the Didache (c. 70--100 AD) through Augustine (c. 394 AD), the testimony is consistent: the Sermon on the Mount is addressed to all Christians. Its commands are to be obeyed in the present age. The literal reading of the hardest commands --- non-retaliation, refusal of oaths, love of enemies --- was the dominant reading in the pre-Nicene church and shaped actual practice, including the refusal of military service.

No pre-Nicene writer treats the Sermon as intended for a future dispensation or a different covenant community. No pre-Nicene writer treats it as an impossible ideal designed merely to reveal human sinfulness. Every early writer who addresses the Sermon applies it to believers in his own time.

The post-Nicene writers (Chrysostom, Augustine) continue this reading but begin to develop the nuances --- grace as the condition of obedience (Augustine), the difficulty of the commands as pedagogically intended (Chrysostom) --- that would shape later theological developments. These nuances do not overturn the basic conviction: the Sermon is for Christians now.

**For the earliest church, the Sermon on the Mount was not a problem to be explained away. It was the way of life to which they had been called.**
