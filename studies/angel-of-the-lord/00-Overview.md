# The Angel of the Lord

*"And the angel of the LORD appeared unto him in a flame of fire out of the midst of a bush: and he looked, and, behold, the bush burned with fire, and the bush was not consumed... And when the LORD saw that he turned aside to see, God called unto him out of the midst of the bush, and said, Moses, Moses. And he said, Here am I."* --- Exodus 3:2, 4 (KJV)

---

## The Question

Something strange happens in the Hebrew Bible. A figure called "the Angel of the LORD" appears to people in crisis --- to a pregnant slave fleeing into the desert, to an old man grieving his childlessness, to a shepherd tending sheep in the wilderness. This figure speaks not as a messenger relaying someone else's words, but as God himself: "I will multiply thy seed." "I have seen the affliction of my people." "I will be with thee." The biblical writers call him an angel --- the Hebrew word *mal'akh* simply means "messenger" --- and yet they also call him LORD, and God, and the Redeemer. He is sent by God and yet speaks as God. He is distinguished from God and yet identified with God. This is not a contradiction the text tries to resolve. It is a mystery the text presents, and it runs from Genesis to Malachi.

Who is this figure? A created angelic being serving as God's authorized representative? God the Father appearing in visible form? The pre-incarnate Son of God, walking the earth centuries before Bethlehem? Or is "the Angel of the LORD" simply a literary convention --- a way the ancient writers described encounters with the divine?

Christians have debated this question since the earliest centuries of the church, and thoughtful believers hold each of these views today. This study examines the question through four lenses --- Scripture, the witness of Christ and the apostles, the testimony of the earliest church, and the lived reality of the believing community --- not to deliver a verdict, but to lay the evidence before you and let you weigh it for yourself. What we find may surprise you: the early church was far more unified on this question than the modern church, and the points of agreement across all views are more significant than the points of disagreement.

## What Scripture Says

The Angel of the LORD appears in more than fifty passages across the Hebrew Bible, and the pattern that emerges is remarkably consistent. Four categories of encounter reveal a figure who is both messenger and the One who sends messengers, both angel and God.

### Hagar: The God Who Sees

The first appearance comes in Genesis 16, and it sets the pattern for everything that follows. Hagar, pregnant and desperate, has fled from Sarah's cruelty into the wilderness. She is alone, without resources, heading toward death.

*"And the angel of the LORD found her by a fountain of water in the wilderness, by the fountain in the way to Shur. And he said, Hagar, Sarai's maid, whence camest thou? and whither wilt thou go? And she said, I flee from the face of my mistress Sarai. And the angel of the LORD said unto her, Return to thy mistress, and submit thyself under her hands. And the angel of the LORD said unto her, I will multiply thy seed exceedingly, that it shall not be numbered for multitude."* --- Genesis 16:7-10 (KJV)

Notice what happens. The Angel of the LORD makes a promise that only God can make: "I will multiply thy seed exceedingly." Not "God will multiply" --- "*I* will multiply." And Hagar's response is extraordinary:

*"And she called the name of the LORD that spake unto her, Thou God seest me: for she said, Have I also here looked after him that seeth me?"* --- Genesis 16:13 (KJV)

Hagar does not call him an angel. She calls him LORD --- and she gives him a name: *El Roi*, "the God who sees me." The narrator confirms her identification: it is "the LORD that spake unto her." From the very first appearance, the paradox is in place. He is called the Angel of the LORD, yet the woman who meets him calls him God, and the text agrees with her.

### The Burning Bush: Angel and God in the Same Breath

The encounter at the burning bush in Exodus 3 is perhaps the most striking example of the identification. The passage opens with the Angel appearing, and then, without explanation, shifts to God himself speaking:

*"And the angel of the LORD appeared unto him in a flame of fire out of the midst of a bush: and he looked, and, behold, the bush burned with fire, and the bush was not consumed... And when the LORD saw that he turned aside to see, God called unto him out of the midst of the bush, and said, Moses, Moses."* --- Exodus 3:2, 4 (KJV)

Verse 2: the Angel of the LORD appeared. Verse 4: the LORD saw, and God called. The text makes no effort to explain the transition. The Angel *is* the LORD speaking from the bush. And what does he say?

*"Moreover he said, I am the God of thy father, the God of Abraham, the God of Isaac, and the God of Jacob. And Moses hid his face; for he was afraid to look upon God."* --- Exodus 3:6 (KJV)

Moses does not treat this as a meeting with an ambassador. He hides his face because he is afraid to look upon God.

### Jacob's Blessing: The Angel as Redeemer

One of the most theologically rich passages comes at the end of Jacob's life. Blessing his grandsons Ephraim and Manasseh, the old patriarch speaks three lines of blessing that reveal how he understood the Angel:

*"And he blessed Joseph, and said, God, before whom my fathers Abraham and Isaac did walk, the God which fed me all my life long unto this day, The Angel which redeemed me from all evil, bless the lads."* --- Genesis 48:15-16 (KJV)

The structure is remarkable. Jacob invokes three figures --- "God, before whom my fathers walked," "the God which fed me," and "the Angel which redeemed me" --- yet asks them to "bless the lads" using a singular Hebrew verb. Three descriptions, one act of blessing. And the word Jacob uses for "redeemed" is *go'el* --- the kinsman-redeemer, the one who has the right and the obligation to rescue a family member. Jacob calls the Angel his *go'el*, his personal Redeemer, and places him in direct parallel with God.

### Manoah's Terror: "We Shall Surely Die"

In Judges 13, the Angel of the LORD appears to Manoah and his wife to announce the birth of Samson. After the Angel ascends in the flame of their sacrifice, Manoah is terrified:

*"And Manoah said unto his wife, We shall surely die, because we have seen God."* --- Judges 13:22 (KJV)

Manoah does not say, "We have seen an angel." He says, "We have seen God." His wife's calm response is equally telling: if God meant to kill them, she reasons, he would not have accepted their offering or shown them these things. Both of them understand that they have encountered God himself.

### "My Name Is in Him"

Perhaps the most provocative statement comes in Exodus 23, where God speaks of the Angel he is sending before Israel:

*"Behold, I send an Angel before thee, to keep thee in the way, and to bring thee into the place which I have prepared. Beware of him, and obey his voice, provoke him not; for he will not pardon your transgressions: for my name is in him."* --- Exodus 23:20-21 (KJV)

This Angel has the authority to pardon or withhold pardon for sins --- a prerogative that belongs to God alone. And the reason given is stunning: "my name is in him." In Hebrew thought, the *name* (*shem*) of God is not a label but the fullness of God's character and authority. To say "my name is in him" is to say something far more than "he represents me." It is to say that the divine nature itself dwells in this figure.

### The Central Paradox

Across all these passages, the same pattern holds. The Angel is sent by God --- yet speaks as God. He is called the Angel --- yet accepts the worship and the terror that belong to God alone. He is described as a messenger --- yet exercises the divine prerogatives of forgiveness, redemption, and the multiplication of descendants. He is distinguished from God and identified with God in the same breath, sometimes in the same verse.

This is the mystery at the heart of the Angel of the LORD passages, and no reading of Scripture can honestly flatten it in either direction. The identification is real. The distinction is also real. Any interpretation must account for both.

## What Jesus and the Apostles Said

The New Testament never contains a verse that says, "The Angel of the LORD was Christ." But it contains something more interesting: a pattern of passages that identify Christ with the visible God of the Old Testament --- the one who appeared, who was seen, who walked among his people.

### The Invisible Father and the Visible Son

The Gospel of John opens with a sweeping theological claim:

*"No man hath seen God at any time; the only begotten Son, which is in the bosom of the Father, he hath declared him."* --- John 1:18 (KJV)

If no one has ever seen the Father, then who appeared to Abraham at Mamre? Who spoke to Moses from the bush? Who wrestled with Jacob at Peniel? Who stood before Joshua with a drawn sword? The New Testament writers seem to have an answer, and they give it in several passages.

### "That Rock Was Christ"

Paul, writing to the Corinthians about Israel's wilderness journey, makes a remarkable identification:

*"And did all drink the same spiritual drink: for they drank of that spiritual Rock that followed them: and that Rock was Christ."* --- 1 Corinthians 10:4 (KJV)

Paul does not say the rock *symbolized* Christ or *prefigured* Christ. He says the Rock *was* Christ --- present with Israel in the wilderness, sustaining them, journeying with them. This is the same wilderness where the Angel of the LORD led and guided the people.

### Isaiah Saw His Glory

The Gospel of John contains another striking identification. After quoting Isaiah's vision of the LORD high and lifted up in the temple (Isaiah 6), John writes:

*"These things said Esaias, when he saw his glory, and spake of him."* --- John 12:41 (KJV)

The "him" is Christ. Isaiah saw the glory of the LORD in the temple --- and John says he was seeing Christ's glory. The pre-incarnate Son was the visible manifestation of God that Isaiah encountered.

### Stephen's Testimony

In his speech before the Sanhedrin, Stephen explicitly connects the Angel at the burning bush with the divine figure who delivered Israel:

*"And when forty years were expired, there appeared to him in the wilderness of mount Sina an angel of the Lord in a flame of fire in a bush. When Moses saw it, he wondered at the sight: and as he drew near to behold it, the voice of the Lord came unto him, Saying, I am the God of thy fathers, the God of Abraham, and the God of Isaac, and the God of Jacob."* --- Acts 7:30--32 (KJV)

Stephen calls the figure "an angel of the Lord" and then, without pause, records the same figure declaring himself to be "the God of Abraham, and the God of Isaac, and the God of Jacob." For Stephen, there was no contradiction in this.

### The Hebrews Counterargument

Honesty requires noting a significant counterargument. The letter to the Hebrews opens by arguing that Christ is superior to the angels:

*"Being made so much better than the angels, as he hath by inheritance obtained a more excellent name than they."* --- Hebrews 1:4 (KJV)

If Christ is superior to angels, can he properly be called "the Angel of the LORD"? Those who identify the Angel with Christ argue that the title "angel" (messenger) describes a role, not a nature --- just as Christ is called "servant" and "lamb" without being ontologically a servant or a sheep. Those who reject the identification argue that Hebrews draws a sharp line between Christ and angels that cannot be crossed. Both readings have merit, and this study presents both at full strength.

## What the Earliest Christians Believed

When we turn to the Christians of the second and third centuries --- the believers closest in time to the apostles --- we find something remarkable: a near-unanimous consensus that the Angel of the LORD was the pre-incarnate Son of God. This was not a fringe view. It was the dominant reading, shared by theologians as diverse as Justin Martyr in Rome, Irenaeus in Gaul, Tertullian in North Africa, and Eusebius in Caesarea.

### Justin Martyr: "Another God and Lord"

Justin Martyr (c. 100-165 AD), in his *Dialogue with Trypho* --- a debate with a Jewish interlocutor --- devotes chapters 56 through 62 to the Angel of the LORD passages. His argument is systematic: the figure who appeared to Abraham, to Jacob, to Moses at the bush, is "another God and Lord under the Creator of all things," distinct from the Father in number but not in will. Justin writes that the Father, who is above the heavens and has never been seen by anyone, could not have been the one who appeared visibly and spoke audibly to the patriarchs. That visible presence, Justin insists, was the Logos --- the Word --- the Son.

### Irenaeus: Always Present with Humanity

Irenaeus of Lyon (c. 130-202 AD), the great defender of apostolic tradition, taught that the Son had always been present with the human race, revealing the Father long before the incarnation. For Irenaeus, the Old Testament appearances of God were the Son's work --- not because the Father was absent, but because the Son is the one through whom the Father makes himself known.

### The Jewish Parallels

The early Christians were not alone in seeing a divine mediator in these texts. Philo of Alexandria (c. 20 BC - 50 AD), the great Jewish philosopher, described the Logos as the "archangel of many names," the "second God," and the one through whom God created and governs the world. The Aramaic Targums --- the synagogue translations of the Hebrew Bible --- routinely substituted "the Memra (Word) of the LORD" where the Hebrew text had God appearing or speaking directly. The Talmud itself preserves the warning against believing in "two powers in heaven" (b. Sanhedrin 38b) --- evidence that some Jewish readers of the Hebrew Bible were drawing exactly the conclusions that the rabbis sought to prevent.

### The Post-Nicene Shift

After the Council of Nicaea in 325 AD, the landscape changed. The Arian heresy --- which taught that the Son was a created being --- had used the Angel of the LORD identification as evidence that the Son was a creature. In response, Augustine of Hippo broke decisively with the earlier consensus. Augustine argued that any person of the Trinity could have appeared in the Old Testament, and that the Angel of the LORD was not specifically the Son but simply God manifesting through created angelic intermediaries.

Augustine's shift was pastoral and strategic --- he was closing a door the Arians had used. But it meant that a reading held with near-unanimity for three centuries was abandoned for reasons that had everything to do with a contemporary controversy and little to do with the texts themselves. The Eastern Orthodox tradition and, later, Oneness Pentecostalism would follow Augustine in various ways. The Reformed tradition, by contrast, largely returned to the pre-Nicene consensus.

## The Major Views Today

Four major interpretations are held by thoughtful Christians today. Each deserves to be understood at its strongest.

**Christophany: The Pre-incarnate Christ.** This view, held by most evangelical and Reformed scholars and rooted in the near-unanimous pre-Nicene consensus, reads the Angel of the LORD as the Son of God appearing in visible form before his incarnation. The identification is supported by the Angel's exercise of divine prerogatives, his acceptance of worship, and the New Testament passages connecting Christ with Old Testament theophanies. The strongest form of this argument holds that the Angel's simultaneous identification with and distinction from God reflects the same relationship the Son has with the Father.

**Created Angel: A Supreme Angelic Being.** This view holds that the Angel of the LORD is a created being --- perhaps the highest angel --- authorized to speak in God's name and on God's behalf. It draws on the Jewish principle of *shaliah* (agency), in which an authorized representative can speak as if he were the one who sent him. The strongest form of this argument notes that the word *mal'akh* always means "messenger" elsewhere in Scripture and that Hebrews 1-2 draws a firm line between Christ and all angels.

**Theophany of the Father: God Himself Manifesting Directly.** Associated with Augustine, the Eastern Orthodox tradition, and Oneness Pentecostals, this view holds that the Angel of the LORD is God the Father (or, in Trinitarian versions, any person of the Trinity) appearing through a created medium. It preserves the deity of the figure without requiring the identification with the Son specifically. The strongest form of this argument holds that the pre-Nicene identification with the Son was theologically naive and that a more mature Trinitarianism does not require it.

**Literary Device: A Narrative Convention.** Some critical scholars view "the Angel of the LORD" as a literary technique the biblical writers used to describe encounters with the divine while maintaining God's transcendence. The Angel is not a distinct being but a way of speaking about God's direct involvement in the world. The strongest form of this argument treats the identification-and-distinction pattern as evidence of the text's composite origins rather than of a real theological paradox.

## What All Views Agree On

The debate over the Angel of the LORD is genuine and important, but the common ground is more significant than the disagreements. All serious interpreters agree on the following:

The Angel of the LORD is not an ordinary figure. No other angel in Scripture speaks as God in the first person, accepts worship without correction, exercises the divine prerogative of forgiving sins, or is explicitly identified as God by the people who encounter him and by the biblical text itself.

The Angel reveals God's intimate, personal care for his people. He comes to Hagar in her rejection, to Moses in his obscurity, to Gideon in his fear, to Israel in their bondage. Whatever his identity, the Angel demonstrates that the God of Israel is not distant or abstract but draws near to human suffering.

The Angel exercises divine prerogatives. He creates, he redeems, he forgives, he multiplies descendants, he receives worship. These are not the actions of a mere messenger, and all views must account for them.

The passages resist being flattened. They cannot be read as simple angelic visitations, nor can the distinction between the Angel and the LORD be erased. The tension between identification and distinction is a feature of the text, not a problem to be solved.

And perhaps most importantly: the Angel of the LORD passages are an invitation to worship, not a weapon for division. They reveal a God who comes down --- who meets people in deserts and at wells and in fields and on mountains --- and that truth is held by every believer, regardless of which view they hold.

## For the Reader

If you have read this far, you have encountered a question that the brightest minds in the church have pondered for two thousand years. You have seen that the evidence is rich, that the views are more nuanced than popular summaries suggest, and that the common ground is surprisingly deep.

The Angel of the LORD passages are, at their heart, stories of encounter. God drew near to Hagar when she had been cast out and had nowhere to go. He drew near to Moses when he was a forgotten shepherd in the back of the wilderness. He drew near to Gideon when Gideon was hiding from his enemies in a winepress. He drew near to Manoah and his wife in their long years of childlessness.

Whatever you conclude about the identity of the Angel, do not miss what every passage shouts: God comes to people. He comes to the rejected, the obscure, the frightened, and the barren. He speaks their names. He makes them promises. He does not forget.

The chapters that follow this overview will take you deeper --- into the Hebrew text, into the New Testament witness, into the testimony of the earliest Christians, into the best arguments for each view, and into the synthesis of agreement and tension. Read them at whatever pace serves you. Return to the passages themselves. Sit with the mystery.

And remember that on the other side of every question stands the God who draws near.

> *"For now we see through a glass, darkly; but then face to face: now I know in part; but then shall I know even as also I am known."*
> --- 1 Corinthians 13:12 (KJV)

May the God who appeared in the bush, who wrestled at the river, who stood with the drawn sword, and who ascended in the flame --- may he draw near to you as you read. And may the grace of our Lord Jesus Christ, and the love of God, and the communion of the Holy Ghost, be with you all. Amen.
