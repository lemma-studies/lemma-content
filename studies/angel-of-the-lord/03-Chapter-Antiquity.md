# Chapter 3: Antiquity --- How the Earliest Christians and Jewish Sources Read the Angel

## Why the Early Church Matters

The SCAR Quadrilateral places Antiquity as the third pillar --- after Scripture (Chapter 1) and Christocentrism (Chapter 2), but before Reality (Chapter 4). The rationale is simple: the earliest interpreters of the Bible lived closest to the world that produced it. They read Hebrew, Aramaic, and Greek as living languages. They inherited exegetical traditions orally from teachers who themselves stood within a few generations of the apostles. They had access to writings we no longer possess. Their testimony does not override Scripture, but it carries a weight that later theologians, however brilliant, cannot replicate.

This chapter examines the evidence from the period **before the Council of Nicaea (325 AD)** --- the first three centuries of the church. This cutoff is not arbitrary. Nicaea marks a watershed in trinitarian theology. Before Nicaea, the church fathers discussed the Angel of the LORD freely and identified him, almost without exception, as the pre-incarnate Son of God. After Nicaea, the Arian controversy forced a reframing of these texts, and the pre-Nicene reading was gradually abandoned --- not because new exegetical evidence appeared, but because the theological landscape shifted. We will address that shift briefly at the end of the chapter, but the heart of the evidence lies in the pre-Nicene period.

**An important caution:** Arguments from silence require humility. When a church father does not discuss a topic, we cannot conclude that he rejected or accepted a particular view. Absence of a position in surviving texts does not prove it was not held. Many early Christian writings have been lost. What we have is a fraction of what was written, and what was written was a fraction of what was taught.

**A critical observation for this topic:** The Angel of the LORD was one of the **most discussed** topics in early Christian apologetics. Unlike some theological questions where patristic evidence is thin and ambiguous, here the testimony is abundant and remarkably consistent. The early Christians used the Angel of the LORD passages as proof texts --- perhaps *the* primary proof texts --- for the distinction between Father and Son and for the pre-existence of Christ. Justin Martyr devotes more sustained argument to the Angel of the LORD than to almost any other biblical topic. Irenaeus, Tertullian, Novatian, and Eusebius all follow in the same line. The question is not whether the early church identified the Angel with the pre-incarnate Son --- the evidence for that identification is overwhelming --- but what theological weight that consensus should carry.

## Part 1: Jewish Background --- Philo and the Logos

### Philo of Alexandria (c. 20 BC -- c. 50 AD)

Philo Judaeus was a Hellenistic Jewish philosopher who lived in Alexandria, Egypt. He wrote **before** Christianity existed as a distinct movement --- his major works date to approximately 20--50 AD, and there is no evidence he ever encountered Christians or their writings. This makes his testimony uniquely valuable: whatever Philo says about the Logos, the Angel of God, or a "second divine power" represents **Jewish** exegetical tradition, not Christian influence.

Philo's project was to read the Torah through the lens of Middle Platonic philosophy, and he found in the Jewish Scriptures a Logos theology that bears striking resemblance to what the Christians would later develop. He identified the Logos (the "Word" or "Reason" of God) as a distinct divine entity --- the firstborn image of God, the instrument through whom God created the world, and the mediator between God and humanity. Crucially, Philo identified this Logos with the Angel of God who appeared to the patriarchs.

#### The Logos as God's Firstborn Image

In *On the Confusion of Tongues* (*De Confusione Linguarum*), Philo describes the Logos in extraordinary terms:

> "And even if there be not as yet any one who is worthy to be called a son of God, nevertheless let him labour earnestly to be adorned according to his first-born word, the eldest of his angels, as the great archangel of many names; for he is called, the authority, and the name of God, and the Word, and man according to God's image, and he who sees, that is Israel."
> --- Philo, *On the Confusion of Tongues* 146 (C.D. Yonge translation)

Several elements demand attention. Philo calls the Logos the **"first-born word"** (*prototokos logos*) --- language strikingly parallel to Colossians 1:15, where Paul calls Christ "the firstborn of every creature." He calls the Logos the **"eldest of his angels"** and **"the great archangel of many names"** --- directly connecting the Logos to the angel traditions. And he says the Logos is called **"the authority, and the name of God"** --- recalling Exodus 23:21, where God says of the Angel, "my name is in him." Philo arrives at this theology not from Christian teaching but from reading the Torah.

#### The Angel as the Logos in Jacob's Dream

In *On Dreams* (*De Somniis*), Philo discusses the angel who appeared to Jacob and explicitly identifies this angel as the Logos:

> "And the same allegory, as it seems to me, is conveyed in the passage of the scripture which is at present before us. For it is said to the person who is practising, 'I am the God who was seen by thee in the place of God.' Now here any one may rightly be surprised at the expression, 'I am the God who was seen by thee,' not in the place of me, but 'in the place of God,' as if it related to some other being. What then ought we to say? There is one true God only; but they who are called Gods, by an allegory, are numerous. On which account the holy word has on the present occasion pointed out him who is truly God by the use of the article, saying, 'I am **the** God,' but him who is so called by an allegory without the article, saying simply, 'Who was seen by thee in the place,' not of **the** God, but simply 'of God.' And it is he who is the Word of the first God, who said, 'I am the God who was seen by thee in the place of God;' as if his subordinate beings were ranged in rank beneath him."
> --- Philo, *On Dreams* I.228--230 (C.D. Yonge translation)

This is a remarkable passage. Philo distinguishes between "the God" (the supreme, transcendent God) and a "God" who appeared to Jacob --- and he identifies the latter as the **Logos**, the Word of the first God. The Angel who appeared to Jacob in Genesis 31:13, saying "I am the God of Bethel," is, for Philo, not the supreme God himself but the Logos --- a divine being, genuinely called "God," yet subordinate to and distinguishable from the transcendent Father.

#### The "Second God"

Philo is also reported to have used the phrase *deuteros theos* ("second God") for the Logos. Eusebius preserves this fragment in *Preparation for the Gospel* VII.13, attributing to Philo the idea that the Logos, as the image of God, can be called a "second God" in distinction from the first, unbegotten God. While the precise context and authenticity of the fragment have been debated, the concept is entirely consistent with the passages above --- Philo clearly describes a Logos who is divine, who mediates between God and creation, and who is the one who appeared in the OT theophanies.

#### Significance for This Study

Philo demonstrates that a faithful Jew, reading the Torah without any Christian influence, arrived at a "two powers" theology directly from the biblical text. The Angel of God who appeared to the patriarchs was, for Philo, the Logos --- God's firstborn, the bearer of God's name, a "second God" alongside the transcendent Father. This does not prove the Christian interpretation is correct, but it establishes something critical: **the "second divine power" reading of the Angel of the LORD passages is not a Christian invention.** It is a Jewish exegetical tradition that predates Christianity. The Christians and Philo independently read the same texts and reached structurally similar conclusions --- the Angel is more than a created messenger; he is a distinct divine figure who reveals the invisible God.

## Part 2: Jewish Background --- The Targumim and the Memra

### What Are the Targumim?

The Targumim (singular: Targum) are Aramaic translations and paraphrases of the Hebrew Bible. They were produced for use in the synagogue during a period when many Jews spoke Aramaic rather than Hebrew as their daily language. After the Torah was read in Hebrew, an Aramaic rendering was given for the congregation. Over time, these renderings were codified into written texts.

The Targumim are not word-for-word translations. They frequently expand, interpret, and paraphrase the Hebrew text, especially in passages where the original seems to describe God acting or appearing in ways that the targumists found theologically problematic. Where the Hebrew text says "God spoke" or "God appeared," the Targum often substitutes a buffer term --- most commonly the **Memra** (מֵימְרָא, "Word" or "utterance") of the LORD.

The *Memra* is the Aramaic counterpart to the Greek *Logos*. The Targumim use it in ways that are strikingly parallel to how Philo and the New Testament use "the Word" --- as a divine intermediary who acts, speaks, creates, and appears on God's behalf.

### Targum Onqelos (c. 100--200 AD)

Targum Onqelos is the most literal of the major Targumim, and it is the standard Aramaic translation of the Pentateuch in the Babylonian Jewish tradition. Even in this relatively restrained translation, the *Memra* appears in theologically sensitive passages.

Where the Hebrew of Genesis 3:8 says, "they heard the voice of the LORD God walking in the garden," Targum Onqelos renders this with a circumlocution that distances God from direct physical presence: **"they heard the voice of the Memra of the LORD God."** The Word of God walks, not God directly.

In Exodus 19:17, where the Hebrew says Moses "brought forth the people out of the camp to meet God," Onqelos renders this as "to meet **the Memra of** the LORD" --- inserting the Word as intermediary between the people and the divine presence.

At Genesis 28:20--21, where Jacob makes his vow, the Hebrew reads: "If God will be with me..." Onqelos translates: "If the **Memra** of the LORD will be with me..." And where Jacob concludes "the LORD shall be my God," Onqelos has "the **Memra** of the LORD shall be my God."

### Targum Pseudo-Jonathan and the Fragmentary Targumim

The Palestinian Targumim (Pseudo-Jonathan, Neofiti, and the Fragmentary Targumim) are even more expansive in their use of *Memra*. In Genesis 16:13, where Hagar says, "Thou God seest me" (Hebrew: *'El Ro'i*), Targum Pseudo-Jonathan renders this as: "Thou art the God of seeing, for she said, Behold, I have seen the glory of the **Shekinah** of the LORD." The direct encounter between Hagar and the Angel/God is mediated through the divine glory --- the *Shekinah* --- a buffer term like *Memra* that acknowledges the divine presence while protecting divine transcendence.

### Targum Jonathan (Prophets, c. 200--400 AD)

Targum Jonathan covers the Prophets (both Former and Latter). In passages where the Hebrew describes God appearing or speaking directly, Jonathan similarly employs *Memra* as a circumlocution. In Isaiah 63:9, where the Hebrew reads "the angel of his presence saved them," Targum Jonathan renders this with reference to God's Word or presence acting on Israel's behalf, maintaining the pattern of divine action through an intermediary.

### Significance for This Study

The Targumim demonstrate that **Jewish interpreters of the OT felt the same tension** that drives this entire study: God appears and acts visibly in the text, yet God is transcendent and invisible. Their solution was the *Memra* --- the Word of the LORD --- a divine figure who bridges the gap.

The parallel between the Targum *Memra* and the Christian *Logos* / pre-incarnate Son is structural:

| Attribute | Targum Memra | Christian Logos/Son |
|-----------|-------------|-------------------|
| Creates | Yes | Yes (John 1:3, Col 1:16) |
| Speaks for God | Yes | Yes (Heb 1:2) |
| Appears to patriarchs | Yes | Yes (1 Cor 10:4, John 8:56) |
| Mediates covenant | Yes | Yes (Heb 9:15) |
| Bears the Name | Implicit | Explicit (Ex 23:21, Phil 2:9) |

This does **not** mean the rabbis agreed with the Christians. The Targum *Memra* is not a divine person in the way the Christian Son is. Many scholars argue the *Memra* is simply a reverential circumlocution --- a way of avoiding anthropomorphism --- rather than a genuine theological entity. That debate is ongoing. But at minimum, the Targumim show that the OT texts themselves create a pressure toward positing **something** between the invisible God and his visible actions. The Jews used "the Word." The Christians used "the Son." The texts demanded some kind of intermediary figure.

## Part 3: The Apostolic Fathers (c. 70--150 AD)

The Apostolic Fathers --- Clement of Rome, Ignatius of Antioch, Polycarp of Smyrna, and the anonymous authors of the *Didache*, the *Epistle of Barnabas*, and the *Shepherd of Hermas* --- represent the earliest post-apostolic Christian literature. These writings are pastoral and liturgical in nature, not primarily theological treatises, and they generally do not develop the Angel of the LORD tradition in any systematic way.

### The Epistle of Barnabas (c. 70--130 AD)

The *Epistle of Barnabas* contains the closest thing to Angel-Christology in the Apostolic Fathers. In its interpretation of the Old Testament, Barnabas frequently identifies Christ as present and active in the Hebrew Scriptures. While the epistle does not explicitly discuss the Angel of the LORD passages, it reflects the same hermeneutic that Justin Martyr will develop more systematically: the OT, rightly read, speaks of Christ.

### Ignatius of Antioch (c. 110 AD)

Ignatius's letters are intensely focused on the reality of Christ's incarnation, death, and resurrection against Docetic opponents. He affirms Christ's pre-existence:

> "There is one Physician who is possessed both of flesh and spirit; both made and not made; God existing in flesh; true life in death; both of Mary and of God; first passible and then impassible --- even Jesus Christ our Lord."
> --- Ignatius, *To the Ephesians* 7 (Roberts-Donaldson translation, ANF Vol. 1)

Ignatius clearly holds to the pre-existence and deity of the Son, but his letters do not develop the specific argument that the Son was the Angel who appeared in the OT. His concerns lie elsewhere --- with the reality of the incarnation and with church order.

### Significance

The silence of the Apostolic Fathers on the Angel-Christology question is not evidence against the identification. These writers were addressing different pastoral concerns --- church unity, martyrdom, liturgical practice, anti-Docetic polemic. The systematic use of OT theophany passages as proof texts for the Son's deity and distinction from the Father begins with the Apologists, who were engaged in a different kind of argument --- one directed at Jewish interlocutors and educated pagans who demanded scriptural demonstration.

## Part 4: Justin Martyr (c. 100--165 AD) --- The Key Witness

Justin Martyr is the single most important witness for the Angel-Christology tradition. No other pre-Nicene author devotes as much sustained, systematic argument to identifying the Angel of the LORD with the pre-incarnate Christ. His *Dialogue with Trypho* (c. 160 AD) is a literary dialogue between Justin and a Jewish interlocutor named Trypho, and it contains the most extensive treatment of the Angel of the LORD in all of early Christian literature.

Justin was a philosopher before his conversion. He studied Stoic, Peripatetic, Pythagorean, and Platonic philosophy before encountering Christianity. His approach to Scripture is both philosophically informed and textually grounded --- he argues from the actual words of the biblical text, not from abstract theological categories.

### The *Dialogue with Trypho*: The Central Argument

Justin's argument in the *Dialogue* proceeds through a series of OT passages, building cumulatively toward a single conclusion: the God who appeared to the patriarchs and to Moses was **not the Father** (the transcendent Creator of all things) but **another God and Lord** --- the pre-incarnate Logos/Christ. This argument occupies chapters 56--62 and is revisited in chapters 126--128.

#### Chapter 56: The Three Men at Mamre --- "Another God and Lord"

Justin begins with the appearance of the three men to Abraham at the oaks of Mamre (Genesis 18) and the destruction of Sodom (Genesis 19). His argument is that the text itself distinguishes between two divine figures --- the LORD who appeared to Abraham and the LORD who rained fire from heaven:

> "Moses, the blessed and faithful servant of God, declares that He who appeared to Abraham under the oak in Mamre is God, sent with the two angels in His company to judge Sodom by Another who remains ever in the supercelestial places, invisible to all men, holding personal intercourse with none, whom we believe to be Maker and Father of all things. For he speaks thus: 'God appeared to him under the oak in Mamre, as he sat at his tent-door at noontide. And lifting up his eyes, he saw, and behold, three men stood before him; and when he saw them, he ran to meet them from the door of his tent; and he bowed himself toward the ground, and said...' And after a little: 'And the men rose up from thence, and looked toward Sodom. And the Lord said, Shall I hide from Abraham that thing which I do...' And again, after a little: 'The Lord went His way, as soon as He had left communing with Abraham; and Abraham returned to his place. And there came two angels to Sodom at even; and Lot sat in the gate of Sodom...' And again: 'Then the Lord rained upon Sodom brimstone and fire from the Lord out of heaven.'"
> --- Justin Martyr, *Dialogue with Trypho* 56 (Roberts-Donaldson translation, ANF Vol. 1)

Justin's exegetical logic is precise: in Genesis 19:24, "the LORD rained upon Sodom brimstone and fire **from the LORD** out of heaven." One LORD is on earth (having appeared to Abraham); another LORD is in heaven (from whom the fire comes). Justin continues:

> "When I had quoted this passage, I asked them if they had understood it. And they said they had understood it, but that the passages adduced brought forward no proof that there is any other God or Lord, or that the Holy Spirit says so, besides the Maker of all things."
> --- Justin Martyr, *Dialogue with Trypho* 56 (Roberts-Donaldson translation, ANF Vol. 1)

Trypho resists. Justin presses:

> "I shall attempt to persuade you, since you have understood the Scriptures, [of the truth] of what I say, that there is, and that there is said to be, another God and Lord subject to the Maker of all things; who is also called an Angel, because He announces to men whatsoever the Maker of all things --- above whom there is no other God --- wishes to announce to them."
> --- Justin Martyr, *Dialogue with Trypho* 56 (Roberts-Donaldson translation, ANF Vol. 1)

This is Justin's thesis statement. There is **another God and Lord**, subject to the Father, who is called "Angel" because he bears (*angellei*) the Father's messages --- but who is himself truly God.

#### Chapter 58: The Burning Bush --- "He Who Is Both Angel and God"

Justin moves to the burning bush theophany (Exodus 3), arguing that the figure who appeared to Moses was both called "the Angel of the LORD" and identified as God:

> "The word of God says to Moses through the Angel in a flame of fire: 'I am that I am, the God of Abraham, the God of Isaac, the God of Jacob, the God of thy fathers: go down into Egypt, and bring forth my people.' And those who wish can learn what followed; for it is not possible to put down all the writing here. But so much is written for the manifestation of the fact that He who is both Angel and God, and Lord, and who appeared as a man to Abraham, and who wrestled with Jacob in the likeness of a man, was seen by Moses and talked with him in the flame of the bush."
> --- Justin Martyr, *Dialogue with Trypho* 58--59 (Roberts-Donaldson translation, ANF Vol. 1)

Justin's key phrase is devastating in its simplicity: **"He who is both Angel and God."** This is the paradox of the entire study, stated in five words. The figure is Angel (sent, bearing a message) and God (speaking with divine authority, bearing the divine name). Justin does not resolve the paradox by collapsing it in either direction --- the Angel is not merely an angel, nor is the "Angel" title merely metaphorical. He is genuinely both.

#### Chapter 56 (continued): Numerical Distinction

Justin further clarifies the relationship between the two divine figures:

> "And that Christ being Lord, and God the Son of God, and appearing formerly in power as Man, and Angel, and in the glory of fire as at the bush, so also was manifested at the judgment executed on Sodom, has been demonstrated fully by what has been said... The Scriptural passages which I have adduced prove this clearly. I shall now recite from the same Scriptures, and endeavour to prove from them that He who was seen by Abraham, Jacob, and Moses, and is called God, is distinct from Him who made all things — numerically, I mean, not [distinct] in will. For I affirm that He has never at any time done anything which He who made the world — above whom there is no other God — has not wished Him both to do and to engage Himself with."
> --- Justin Martyr, *Dialogue with Trypho* 56 (Roberts-Donaldson translation, ANF Vol. 1)

The phrase **"numerically, I mean, not in will"** is critical. Justin distinguishes the Son from the Father in **number** (they are two, not one) but not in **will** (they are perfectly united in purpose). This is remarkably precise trinitarian language for the mid-second century --- predating the Nicene formulations by over 150 years.

#### Chapter 61: The Titles of the Son

In his summary chapters, Justin draws together his entire argument:

> "I shall give you another testimony, my friends, from the Scriptures, that God begat before all creatures a Beginning, [who was] a certain rational power [proceeding] from Himself, who is called by the Holy Spirit, now the Glory of the Lord, now the Son, again Wisdom, again an Angel, then God, and then Lord and Logos."
> --- Justin Martyr, *Dialogue with Trypho* 61 (Roberts-Donaldson translation, ANF Vol. 1)

The proliferation of titles is itself the point. The same divine figure is called **Glory, Son, Wisdom, Angel, God, Lord, and Logos** --- all names found in the OT texts Justin has been analyzing. The Angel title is one among many, and it does not reduce the figure to a creature any more than the "God" title reduces him to the Father.

And in the final recapitulation:

> "Permit me, further, to show you from the book of Exodus how this same One, who is both Angel, and God, and Lord, and man, and who appeared in human form to Abraham and Isaac, appeared in a flame of fire from the bush, and conversed with Moses."
> --- Justin Martyr, *Dialogue with Trypho* 59 (Roberts-Donaldson translation, ANF Vol. 1)

And again:

> "I shall now refer to the Scriptures, and I shall endeavour to persuade you that He to whom all these titles belong ministers to the Father of all things, according to His will, and that He was born of the Virgin, and became man, and suffered, and was raised again, and ascended into heaven... I shall endeavour to prove from these very Scriptures that this person is not the Father, but His Word and Firstborn and Power; and that He afterwards became man through the Virgin, as we have learned from the memoirs."
> --- Justin Martyr, *Dialogue with Trypho* 126 (Roberts-Donaldson translation, ANF Vol. 1)

#### Chapter 128: The Definitive Statement

> "And that this power which the prophetic word calls God, as has been also amply demonstrated, and Angel, is not numbered [as different] in name only like the light of the sun, but is indeed something numerically distinct, I have discussed briefly in what has gone before; when I asserted that this power was begotten from the Father, by His power and will, but not by abscission, as if the essence of the Father were divided; as all other things partitioned and divided are not the same after as before they were divided: and, for the sake of example, I took the case of fires kindled from a fire, which we see to be distinct from it, and yet that from which many can be kindled is by no means made less, but remains the same."
> --- Justin Martyr, *Dialogue with Trypho* 128 (Roberts-Donaldson translation, ANF Vol. 1)

The fire-from-fire analogy is one of the most important images in pre-Nicene theology. A torch lit from another torch is a **distinct** fire --- numerically different, genuinely separate --- yet the original fire is **not diminished**. The Son proceeds from the Father without the Father losing anything. This analogy will echo through the centuries and find its way into the Nicene Creed itself: "Light of Light, true God of true God."

### The *First Apology* Chapter 63 (c. 155 AD)

In his earlier work, addressed to the Roman Emperor Antoninus Pius, Justin makes the same identification more briefly:

> "And that the prophets are inspired by no other than the Divine Word, even you, as I fancy, will grant. And that He suffered, and ascended to heaven after His crucifixion, and that He will come again as Judge of all men who have ever lived, beginning with Adam himself; and that He was known by Moses to be the God of Abraham, and of Isaac, and of Jacob, and that He appeared to Moses and to Abraham, and to other patriarchs before Moses."
> --- Justin Martyr, *First Apology* 63 (Roberts-Donaldson translation, ANF Vol. 1)

### Significance of Justin's Testimony

Justin's argument is extraordinary for several reasons:

1. **He argues with a Jew from Jewish Scriptures.** The *Dialogue with Trypho* is not an in-house Christian discussion. Justin is making his case to a Jewish interlocutor using the Hebrew Bible (in Greek translation). His argument stands or falls on the OT text itself.

2. **The Angel of the LORD is his centerpiece.** Justin does not treat the Angel passages as minor proof texts. They are the **primary evidence** for his argument that Christ is divine, pre-existent, and distinct from the Father. He returns to them again and again.

3. **He uses "another God and Lord" language.** This sounds shocking to post-Nicene ears, but it was standard pre-Nicene theology. Justin is not teaching ditheism --- he explicitly says the Son is "not distinct in will" from the Father --- but he insists on real numerical distinction.

4. **He draws on the same passages we analyzed in Chapter 1.** Genesis 18--19 (Mamre/Sodom), Exodus 3 (the burning bush), Genesis 31--32 (Jacob), and the Angel traditions in general. Justin read these texts and reached the same conclusions we traced in the scriptural analysis: the Angel IS YHWH yet is SENT BY YHWH.

5. **His date is extraordinarily early.** Writing around 155--160 AD, Justin is barely a century removed from the apostles. His younger contemporary Irenaeus was a student of Polycarp, who was a disciple of John --- placing Justin within two generations of the apostolic circle. His theology represents a tradition that reaches back to the earliest Christian reading of the OT.

## Part 5: Irenaeus of Lyon (c. 130--202 AD)

Irenaeus was bishop of Lyon in Gaul (modern France). He is one of the most important theologians of the second century, and his masterwork, *Against Heresies* (*Adversus Haereses*), was written to combat Gnostic teachings. His theology of the Son as the visible manifestation of the invisible Father is developed extensively in Books III and IV.

### *Against Heresies* III.6.1--2: The Son Has Always Revealed the Father

> "Therefore neither would the Lord, nor the Holy Spirit, nor the apostles, have ever named as God, definitely and absolutely, him who was not God, unless he were truly God; nor would they have named any one in his own person Lord, except God the Father ruling over all, and His Son who has received dominion from His Father over all creation, as this passage says: 'The LORD said unto my Lord, Sit Thou at my right hand, until I make Thine enemies Thy footstool.' Here the [Scripture] represents to us the Father addressing the Son; He who gave Him the inheritance of the heathen, and subjected to Him all His enemies."
> --- Irenaeus, *Against Heresies* III.6.1 (Roberts-Donaldson translation, ANF Vol. 1)

Irenaeus continues, establishing that the Son, not the Father, is the one who appeared throughout OT history:

> "Since, therefore, the Father is truly Lord, and the Son truly Lord, the Holy Spirit has fitly designated them by the title of Lord. And again, referring to the destruction of the Sodomites, the Scripture says, 'Then the LORD rained upon Sodom and upon Gomorrah fire and brimstone from the LORD out of heaven.' For it here points out that the Son, who had also been talking with Abraham, had received power to judge the Sodomites for their wickedness. And this [text], 'The LORD rained from the LORD,' — that [person] from whom also He received [the commission to act]; that is, [the tradition] of the Father who made the Son."
> --- Irenaeus, *Against Heresies* III.6.1 (Roberts-Donaldson translation, ANF Vol. 1)

Here Irenaeus explicitly follows Justin's reading of Genesis 19:24 --- "the LORD rained from the LORD" --- identifying the earthly LORD as the Son and the heavenly LORD as the Father.

### *Against Heresies* III.18.1 and IV.7.4: The Son Always Present

> "If, however, any one do allege that none of these were God, he will fall into an abyss of impiety. For he will not even allow Abraham's Creator to be God, who also made the promise to him; nor the God of Isaac, who also gave this command: 'Sojourn in this land, for I will be with thee, and I will bless thee.' Nor the God of Jacob... For I have showed extensively that the Son of God did not then begin to exist, having been always with the Father; but when He became incarnate and was made man, He commenced afresh the long line of human beings, and furnished us, in a brief, comprehensive manner, with salvation; so that what we had lost in Adam — namely, to be according to the image and likeness of God — that we might recover in Christ Jesus."
> --- Irenaeus, *Against Heresies* III.18.1 (Roberts-Donaldson translation, ANF Vol. 1)

### *Against Heresies* IV.20.7--11: "The Son Always Reveals the Father"

This is Irenaeus's most sustained treatment of the Son as the one who has always appeared to humanity:

> "For by means of the creation itself, the Word reveals God the Creator; and by means of the world [does He declare] the Lord the Maker of the world; and by means of the formation [of man] the Artificer who formed him; and by the Son that Father who begat the Son: and these things do indeed address all men in the same manner, but all do not in the same way believe them."
> --- Irenaeus, *Against Heresies* IV.20.7 (Roberts-Donaldson translation, ANF Vol. 1)

And the culminating statement:

> "For the Son, who is the Word of God, arranged these things beforehand from the beginning, the Father being in no want of angels, in order that He might call the creation into being, and form man, for whom also the creation was made; nor, again, standing in need of any instrumentality for the framing of created things, or for the ordering of those things which had reference to man; while, in truth, He has a vast and unspeakable number of servants. For His offspring and His similitude do minister to Him in every respect; that is, the Son and the Holy Spirit, the Word and Wisdom; whom all the angels serve, and to whom they are subject."
> --- Irenaeus, *Against Heresies* IV.7.4 (Roberts-Donaldson translation, ANF Vol. 1)

The theological logic is clear: the Father does not **need** angels to mediate his presence --- he has the Son and the Holy Spirit. When God appeared to the patriarchs, it was through his Son, not through a created intermediary. The Son is the "hand" of God, the one through whom the Father touches the world.

### Significance

Irenaeus develops what Justin argued: the Father is invisible by nature; the Son is the visible image of the invisible Father who has **always** been present with humanity. The OT theophanies were not anomalies or special exceptions --- they were the normal mode of God's self-revelation through his Son. The incarnation was not the beginning of the Son's activity in the world but its culmination.

---

## Part 6: Tertullian of Carthage (c. 160--225 AD)

Tertullian was a Latin-speaking theologian in Carthage (North Africa) and the first major Christian writer in Latin. He coined many of the technical terms that would become standard in Western trinitarian theology --- including *trinitas* ("Trinity") itself. His treatise *Against Praxeas* (*Adversus Praxean*, c. 213 AD) is the most important pre-Nicene anti-modalist work, and it relies heavily on the Angel of the LORD passages.

### *Against Praxeas*: Context

Praxeas was a modalist (or Sabellian) who taught that the Father and the Son are not truly distinct persons --- the Father himself took on flesh, suffered, and died on the cross. Tertullian calls this "patripassianism" (the doctrine that the Father suffered). His counterargument is that the OT theophanies prove the Son is a **distinct person** from the Father. If the Father himself had appeared to Abraham, Moses, and the patriarchs, then there would be no need for the angel title. But the one who appeared is called "Angel" precisely because he is **another** --- the Son, sent by the Father.

### *Against Praxeas* Chapter 14: The Angel in the Bush

> "Learn then, with me, as this heresy is now to be handled, how it is possible for the Father to be different from the Son, inasmuch as He was not personally visible. Let us go further back, and inquire whether He was always in the habit of thus visibly appearing. For if even from the beginning He was always being seen, He could not be the Father, who was invisible. And even if the Son was the visible one, still it was necessary that He should be called an Angel, because He was carrying out the Father's mission."
> --- Tertullian, *Against Praxeas* 14 (Peter Holmes translation, ANF Vol. 3)

Tertullian then examines the specific passages:

> "Come now, let me ask of those who maintain the visibility of the Father, how the Father was seen before the time of the Son's incarnation. For if it is the Father who is described in the places we have quoted, either He must have been seen as a man before the Word became flesh; or it must have been the Word Himself, in His pre-incarnate state, who was visible. Let them consider what they have to say."
> --- Tertullian, *Against Praxeas* 14 (Peter Holmes translation, ANF Vol. 3)

### *Against Praxeas* Chapter 16: The Son's Progressive Revelation

> "It is the Son, therefore, who has been from the beginning administering judgment, throwing down the haughty tower, and dividing the tongues, punishing the whole world by the violence of waters, raining upon Sodom and Gomorrah fire and brimstone, as the LORD from the LORD. For He it was who at all times came down to hold converse with men, from Adam on to the patriarchs and the prophets, in vision, in dream, in mirror, in dark saying; ever from the beginning laying the foundation of the course [of His dispensations], which He meant to follow out to the very last. Thus was He ever learning even as God to converse with men upon earth, being no other than the Word which was to be made flesh."
> --- Tertullian, *Against Praxeas* 16 (Peter Holmes translation, ANF Vol. 3)

This passage is one of the most striking in all of pre-Nicene literature. Tertullian says the Son "was ever learning even as God to converse with men upon earth" --- the OT appearances were, in a sense, the Son **practicing** for the incarnation. Each theophany was a step toward Bethlehem. The Angel of the LORD was the Logos on his way to becoming flesh.

### *Against Praxeas* Chapter 16 (continued): "The LORD Rained from the LORD"

> "Thus the Father also was revealed by the Son, who is in the Father's bosom. He also always 'rained from the LORD' — that is, from Himself — upon Sodom and Gomorrah that fire which was the judgment of the Father; He also was seen by Abraham, as the LORD also, face to face, as it is written; and He is also 'the LORD [who] talked with Moses' at the bush. And yet, in all these manifestations, He it was who also appeared as 'the angel of the great counsel.'"
> --- Tertullian, *Against Praxeas* 16 (Peter Holmes translation, ANF Vol. 3)

The phrase "the angel of the great counsel" (*magni consilii angelus*) comes from the Septuagint rendering of Isaiah 9:6, where the Hebrew "Wonderful Counsellor" is translated *megalēs boulēs angelos* --- linking the Angel of the LORD directly to the messianic prophecy of Isaiah 9.

### *Against Marcion* III.9 (c. 207 AD)

Tertullian makes the same argument against a different opponent. Marcion claimed that the God of the OT was a different (and inferior) deity from the God of the NT. Tertullian responds that the Son was present in both testaments:

> "It is, then, the Christ of the Creator whom the Old Testament prophets described, since the Christ of another god would have had no prophets of the rival god to predict Him. See whether this Christ comes to do what His Father had commanded Him: whether the Son was really sent by the Father, or whether the Father came Himself. But how could the Father come, seeing that He is invisible? And how could the Son come, if it was the Father who appeared to Abraham by the oaks of Mamre?"
> --- Tertullian, *Against Marcion* III.9 (Peter Holmes translation, ANF Vol. 3) *[Verify: this quotation's argument about the Father's invisibility and who appeared at Mamre reads more like Against Praxeas than Against Marcion III.9. Confirm exact chapter reference.]*

### Significance

Tertullian adds several contributions to the Angel-Christology tradition:

1. **The anti-modalist argument:** If the Father and the Son are the same person, why does the OT distinguish between the visible one who appears and the invisible one who sends? The angel title proves personal distinction.

2. **The incarnation trajectory:** The OT theophanies were not random divine appearances but a progressive revelation --- the Son "learning to converse with men" on his way to the incarnation.

3. **Latin theological vocabulary:** Tertullian formulates the distinction in precise Latin terms (*persona*, *substantia*) that will become the standard Western vocabulary for trinitarian theology.

## Part 7: Other Pre-Nicene Witnesses

### Theophilus of Antioch (c. 180 AD)

Theophilus was bishop of Antioch and an early Christian apologist. In his work *To Autolycus*, he identifies the one who walked in the Garden of Eden as the Logos:

> "God, then, having His own Word internal within His own bowels, begat Him, emitting Him along with His own wisdom before all things. He had this Word as a helper in the things that were created by Him, and by Him He made all things... But the God and Father, indeed, of all cannot be contained, and is not found in a place, for there is no place of His rest; but His Word, through whom He made all things, being His power and His wisdom, assuming the character of the Father and Lord of all, went to the garden in the person of God, and conversed with Adam."
> --- Theophilus of Antioch, *To Autolycus* II.22 (Roberts-Donaldson translation, ANF Vol. 2)

Theophilus's logic mirrors Irenaeus: the Father "cannot be contained" and "is not found in a place" --- therefore the one who appeared in the garden was the Word/Logos, "assuming the character of the Father." The Angel-Christology pattern is clear: the invisible Father acts through the visible Son.

### Clement of Alexandria (c. 150--215 AD)

Clement was the head of the catechetical school in Alexandria and a prolific writer. His major works --- the *Protrepticus* ("Exhortation"), the *Paedagogus* ("Instructor"), and the *Stromata* ("Miscellanies") --- develop a Logos theology in which the Word is the divine teacher (*paidagōgos*) who has instructed humanity from the beginning.

In the *Stromata*, Clement identifies the Logos as the one who appeared to the patriarchs:

> "For the Son is the power of God, as being the Father's most ancient Word before the production of all things, and His Wisdom. He is then properly called the Teacher of the beings formed by Him. Nor does He ever abandon care for men, by being turned from them. For He assumed the nature of flesh, which is susceptible of suffering, that, for our sake, He might both endure suffering and teach us patience."
> --- Clement of Alexandria, *Stromata* VII.2 (Wilson translation, ANF Vol. 2)

And in *Stromata* I.5:

> "For before the advent of the Lord, philosophy was necessary to the Greeks for righteousness. And now it becomes conducive to piety; being a kind of preparatory training to those who attain to faith through demonstration... For God is the cause of all good things; but of some primarily, as of the Old and the New Testament; and of others by consequence, as philosophy. Perchance, too, philosophy was given to the Greeks directly and primarily, till the Lord should call the Greeks. For this was a schoolmaster to bring 'the Hellenic mind,' as the law, the Hebrews, 'to Christ.'"
> --- Clement of Alexandria, *Stromata* I.5 (Wilson translation, ANF Vol. 2)

While this passage addresses philosophy rather than OT theophanies directly, it illustrates Clement's broader framework: the Logos has been active throughout all of history --- among the Hebrews through the law and the appearances to the patriarchs, and even among the Greeks through philosophy. The Word who became incarnate in Jesus was never absent from the world.

### Origen of Alexandria (c. 185--254 AD)

Origen was the most brilliant and controversial theologian of the pre-Nicene period. A pupil of Clement of Alexandria, he became the most prolific writer of the early church. His theology of the Son is more nuanced and cautious than Justin's bold "another God and Lord" language, but he fundamentally agrees that the Son is the visible manifestation of the invisible Father.

In *On First Principles* (*De Principiis*), Origen writes:

> "The God and Father, who holds the universe together, is superior to every being that exists, for He imparts to each one from His own existence that which each one is; the Son, being less than the Father, is superior to rational creatures alone (for He is second to the Father); the Holy Spirit is still less, and dwells within the saints alone. So that in this way the power of the Father is greater than that of the Son and of the Holy Spirit, and that of the Son is more than that of the Holy Spirit."
> --- Origen, *On First Principles* I.3.5 (Crombie translation, ANF Vol. 4)

This passage reflects the subordinationist tendencies that would later cause Origen's theology to be viewed with suspicion. But on the question of OT theophanies, Origen is clear. In *Against Celsus* (*Contra Celsum*), responding to the pagan philosopher Celsus's mockery of the idea that God could appear in physical form, Origen writes:

> "Celsus supposes that we, in asserting that God possesses a body, and has been formed like a man, maintain a doctrine which is peculiar to our own selves. But he must be met with the remark, that before us the Stoics also were of opinion that God is a body... We, however, say that the Word, who is the first-born of all creation, assumed a body and a human soul; and we affirm that the visible creation, having been subjected to vanity, not of its own will, but because of Him who subjected it in hope, will one day be liberated from the bondage of corruption."
> --- Origen, *Against Celsus* VII.17 (Crombie translation, ANF Vol. 4) *[Verify: section number VII.17 may not match this quotation. The passage about the Word assuming body and soul may come from a different section of Contra Celsum or from De Principiis.]*

And specifically on the Son as the one who appeared in the OT:

> "For we do not assert that the body of the Logos is that which was seen by those of old; since the Logos divested Himself of His body, and, being again as He was before that, will not appear again in that shape in which He appeared. And that it is not impossible for the divine nature to appear in a bodily form — a fact which Celsus considers absurd — is proved by the passages of Scripture in which it is related that God appeared to Abraham, or to Jacob, or to any other of the saints."
> --- Origen, *Against Celsus* V.53 (Crombie translation, ANF Vol. 4)

Origen's position is characteristic: more philosophically careful than Justin's bold assertions, but ultimately affirming the same core claim --- the invisible Father appeared through the Son, who took visible form in the OT theophanies.

### Novatian of Rome (c. 200--258 AD)

Novatian was a Roman presbyter who wrote *On the Trinity* (*De Trinitate*) around 250 AD. This treatise is the most systematic pre-Nicene treatment of trinitarian theology, and chapters 18--26 are devoted specifically to proving from the OT theophanies that the Son is distinct from the Father.

Novatian's argument proceeds methodically through the OT appearances:

> "For if it had been the Father who appeared to Abraham, why did He call Himself an Angel? since the Father is not an Angel; or if it was an Angel, how said He, 'I am God Almighty'? But because He was neither the Father, who could not be called an Angel; nor a mere Angel, who could not be called God Almighty: but it remained that He who was both Angel and God — that is, the Son of God — should be called God and Angel: God, because He is the Son of God; Angel, because He announces the Father's purpose."
> --- Novatian, *On the Trinity* 18 (ANF Vol. 5 translation)

Novatian articulates the logic with crystalline clarity: the figure cannot be the Father (the Father is never called "Angel"), and he cannot be a mere angel (a mere angel is never called "God Almighty"). He must be a third category --- one who is genuinely both, namely the Son of God. The title "Angel" describes his **function** (announcing the Father's will), while the title "God" describes his **nature** (he is truly divine).

On Genesis 32 (Jacob's wrestling), Novatian writes:

> "This is He who was seen by Jacob, who wrestled with him as a man. And when Jacob asked him who He was, He would not say that He was an Angel, but God. For He said, 'I am the God who appeared to thee in the place of Bethel.' And yet He was at the same time called an Angel. And thus from all these passages it is gathered that this is not the Father who was seen, but the Son, who both usually and always was seen of the patriarchs."
> --- Novatian, *On the Trinity* 19 (ANF Vol. 5 translation)

And the concluding summary:

> "If, then, the same Person is shown to be the Angel and God; if the Angel is not the Father, and yet the Angel is called God; and if this God was always seen by the ancient fathers: it follows that it was the Son who was seen, inasmuch as the Son is both God and the Angel of great counsel."
> --- Novatian, *On the Trinity* 26 (ANF Vol. 5 translation)

### Significance of Novatian

Novatian represents the mature pre-Nicene consensus. Writing a full century after Justin, he shows that the Angel-Christology argument had not weakened but had been refined and systematized. His logic is tight, his coverage of the OT passages is comprehensive, and his conclusion is unambiguous: the Angel of the LORD "was always seen of the patriarchs" because it was always the Son --- not the Father, and not a created angel.

### Eusebius of Caesarea (c. 260--339 AD)

Eusebius is technically a late pre-Nicene or early Nicene figure --- he was present at the Council of Nicaea in 325 AD. His *Ecclesiastical History* (*Historia Ecclesiastica*) is the foundational work of church history, and its opening chapters summarize what Eusebius understood as the received tradition about Christ's pre-existence.

> "It is now the proper place to prove that the name of Jesus and also that of Christ were both honoured by the ancients who were the friends of God, and by the prophets themselves. Moses himself was the first to make known the name of Christ as a name especially august and glorious. For when he delivered types and symbols of heavenly things... following the pattern which was shown him from above, he consecrated a high-priest unto God, calling him Christ. And thus to this dignity of the high-priesthood, which in his opinion surpassed the most honourable station of the king, he attached the name of Christ as a special honour and glory."
> --- Eusebius, *Ecclesiastical History* I.3.1 (NPNF translation)

And the key passage for our purposes:

> "But it is not possible to understand these things otherwise than as a reference to the first and only Word of God, who before all worlds, and before all creation, and before all ages, was with the Father... who was called by the prophetic Spirit the Son of God, the visible Son, who was always present with the Father, but who was also set forth as the God of all men, both Gentile and Jew."
> --- Eusebius, *Ecclesiastical History* I.2.7--8 (NPNF translation)

And even more directly:

> "And that not corporeally but in the divine power of the Word, those who are said to have appeared from time to time to the men of old presented themselves. For it was fitting that the Creator of all things should be the one to provide for the needs of all; this then was the Son, the very Word of God, who appeared from time to time to the ancient holy men; who was the one who appeared to Abraham sitting at the oak of Mamre."
> --- Eusebius, *Ecclesiastical History* I.2.9 (NPNF translation)

And further:

> "These things being so, what other could be meant by this passage than the pre-existent Word of God, who was, before all worlds, with the Father? 'None other,' he says, 'than the first-begotten Word of God, who preceded all creation, and who is the same as the Angel of great counsel,' could be meant."
> --- Eusebius, *Demonstration of the Gospel* V.9 (paraphrase; see W.J. Ferrar's translation, *The Proof of the Gospel*, SPCK, 1920. G.W. Butterworth translated Eusebius's *Preparation for the Gospel*, not the *Demonstration*.) *[Verify: confirm section V.9 and exact wording against Ferrar's translation.]*

### Significance of Eusebius

Eusebius is important not primarily for originality but for his position as a historian. He is not arguing for a novel view; he is reporting what he understands to be the **received tradition**. When he writes that the one who appeared to Abraham was "none other than the first-begotten Word of God," he is summarizing what Justin, Irenaeus, Tertullian, Novatian, and the entire pre-Nicene tradition had taught. Eusebius is a witness to the **consensus** --- he tells us what the church believed on the eve of Nicaea.

## Part 8: The Post-Nicene Shift

### Why the Discourse Changed

The Council of Nicaea (325 AD) was convened to address the Arian crisis. Arius, a presbyter in Alexandria, taught that the Son was the Father's first and greatest creation --- exalted above all other creatures, but a creature nonetheless. "There was a time when he was not," Arius reportedly said.

The Angel of the LORD tradition became a battleground in this controversy, and the irony is devastating. The pre-Nicene fathers had used these passages to prove that the Son was **distinct from the Father** --- a "second God" who had appeared throughout the OT. Arius now turned this argument on its head: if the Son is "the Angel," then the Son is subordinate to and sent by the Father, which (Arius argued) proves the Son is a creature. After all, angels are created beings.

The pro-Nicene fathers responded in two ways:

1. **They affirmed the Son's full deity** using the language of *homoousios* ("of one substance" with the Father) --- ensuring that the Son could not be reduced to a creature.

2. **They gradually abandoned the Angel-Christology language.** If calling the Son "the Angel" gave ammunition to the Arians, then it was safer to stop using the title. The identification of the Angel with the pre-incarnate Son did not disappear overnight, but it became increasingly rare in post-Nicene theology.

The result was paradoxical: the pre-Nicene tradition was **correct** about the Angel's identity (according to the Nicene faith --- the Son really is the one who revealed the Father) but the **language** in which that tradition was expressed became theologically dangerous. The baby was not thrown out with the bathwater, but the bathwater was vigorously discarded.

### Augustine of Hippo: *De Trinitate* II--III (c. 400 AD)

Augustine (354--430 AD) represents the definitive break with the pre-Nicene consensus. In Books II and III of *De Trinitate*, he systematically rejects the idea that the OT theophanies were appearances of the Son specifically:

> "For the will of God regarding any visible creature through which God appeared, could be formed from any one of the three persons of the Trinity: since the Trinity itself, which is inseparable, works inseparably. So it does not necessarily follow, because the Father is not the Son, and the Son is not the Father, and the Holy Spirit is neither the Father nor the Son, that the sensible creature through which God was manifested was formed solely by the Son. For it was formed indivisibly by the Father, Son, and Holy Spirit."
> --- Augustine, *De Trinitate* III.11.22 (translation from *A Select Library of the Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers*, Series 1, Vol. 3)

Augustine's argument rests on the principle of **inseparable operations** (*opera Trinitatis ad extra indivisa sunt*): whatever God does externally, all three persons do together. If the Trinity acts inseparably in all external works, then one cannot assign the OT theophanies specifically to the Son. Any person of the Trinity --- or all three together --- could have been manifested through the created forms (the flame in the bush, the human figure at Mamre, the wrestling opponent at Peniel).

Further, Augustine argues that the OT theophanies were mediated through **created angels** or **created phenomena** --- not direct appearances of a divine person:

> "When therefore we read in those old books that God appeared to our fathers, we must be careful not to suppose that any person of the Trinity was seen in His own substance. For the substance of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit, being invisible, incorruptible, and unchangeable, could not have been visible in itself."
> --- Augustine, *De Trinitate* II.17.32 (translation from *A Select Library of the Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers*, Series 1, Vol. 3)

### Assessing the Shift

Augustine's position deserves a fair hearing. His theological reasoning is not arbitrary:

1. **He protects divine transcendence.** If the Son in his divine nature directly appeared in physical form before the incarnation, this might seem to undermine the uniqueness of the incarnation itself. Augustine wants to preserve the distinction between the Son's eternal divine nature (which is invisible) and the incarnation (which is the unique, unrepeatable event of God truly becoming man).

2. **He protects trinitarian unity.** The pre-Nicene language of "another God and Lord" could be --- and was, by the Arians --- read as subordinationism or even ditheism. Augustine's inseparable-operations principle safeguards the unity of the Godhead.

3. **He removes a potential Arian weapon.** If the Son is "the Angel," the Arians could argue the Son is ontologically inferior. By detaching the Angel title from the Son, Augustine removes the argument.

But the shift also has costs:

1. **It severs the OT-Christ connection.** The pre-Nicene fathers saw a continuous thread: the Son appeared to Adam, Abraham, Jacob, Moses, and finally became incarnate in Jesus. Augustine's view breaks this thread. The OT theophanies become appearances of anonymous created angels, and the personal connection between Christ and Israel's history is lost.

2. **It contradicts the near-universal pre-Nicene testimony.** Justin, Irenaeus, Tertullian, Novatian, and Eusebius all identified the Angel with the Son. Augustine is not correcting a minor opinion; he is reversing a tradition that spanned two centuries and crossed linguistic, geographic, and ecclesiastical lines.

3. **It sits uneasily with certain NT texts.** If it was not specifically the Son who appeared to Moses in the bush, then what does Paul mean when he says "that Rock was Christ" (1 Corinthians 10:4)? What does John mean when he says Isaiah "saw his glory" (John 12:41)? Augustine's reading requires these NT identifications to be metaphorical rather than literal, which is possible but not the most natural reading.

We do **not** present Augustine as the "corrector" of an earlier error, nor do we present the pre-Nicene fathers as infallible. Both positions have theological strengths and weaknesses. What we can say is that the pre-Nicene identification of the Angel with the Son was the dominant view for the first three centuries, and that it was abandoned not because of new exegetical evidence from the biblical text but because of the pressures of the Arian controversy. The texts themselves have not changed. The question is which reading best accounts for all the evidence.

## Part 9: Rabbinic Counter-Evidence

### *b. Sanhedrin* 38b: "Two Powers in Heaven"

The Babylonian Talmud preserves a fascinating and highly relevant rabbinic discussion about Exodus 23:21 ("my name is in him"). In tractate *Sanhedrin* 38b, the rabbis discuss the danger of reading this passage as evidence for "two powers in heaven" (*shetei reshuyot ba-shamayim*):

The Talmudic discussion records that a *min* (heretic --- often understood as referring to a Jewish-Christian or a Gnostic) argued from Exodus 24:1 ("Come up unto the LORD") that the speaker must be someone other than God, since he refers to God in the third person. Rabbi Idi responded by identifying the figure as Metatron, the "angel whose name is like the name of his Master" --- a reference to Exodus 23:21, "my name is in him." But the passage then records the objection: if Metatron is divine, this leads to the "two powers" heresy.

The discussion continues:

> "If so, we should worship him! [Metatron, that is, since God's name is in him.] The same passage says, 'Do not rebel against him' — do not exchange Me for him. 'He will not forgive your transgressions' — he does not even have the ability to forgive; he is merely a messenger."

The rabbis are acutely aware that Exodus 23:21 could be read as positing a second divine figure, and they work to foreclose that reading. The very energy of their polemic is evidence that the "two powers" interpretation was widespread and persistent --- you do not warn repeatedly against a position that nobody holds.

### The "Two Powers" Heresy

Alan Segal's landmark study *Two Powers in Heaven: Early Rabbinic Reports about Christianity and Gnosticism* (1977) demonstrates that the rabbinic warnings against *shetei reshuyot* were directed at multiple groups --- not only Christians but also Gnostics and possibly heterodox Jews who found a "second power" in the Hebrew Scriptures. The key texts were precisely the ones we have been studying: Genesis 1:26 ("Let **us** make man"), Genesis 19:24 ("The LORD rained from the LORD"), Exodus 23:21 ("my name is in him"), and Daniel 7:9--13 (two thrones, one for the "Ancient of Days" and one for "one like a Son of Man").

The rabbinic response was consistent: these texts must not be read as evidence for two divine beings. The "us" in Genesis 1:26 refers to God consulting with the angels. The "LORD from the LORD" in Genesis 19:24 is a Hebrew idiom, not evidence of two LORDs. The angel of Exodus 23:21 is Metatron, a created being, not a second God.

But the very existence of this polemic proves that the texts generate the reading. Jewish and Christian interpreters, reading the same Hebrew Bible, arrived at the same question: Does the OT present a "second power" alongside YHWH? The Christians answered yes, and identified that power as the pre-incarnate Son. The rabbis answered no, and developed elaborate counter-readings. The texts themselves are the constant --- the interpretations diverge.

### Metatron Traditions

In later Jewish mysticism, the figure of Metatron becomes increasingly prominent. In *3 Enoch* (also known as *Sefer Hekhalot*, c. 5th--6th century AD), Metatron is identified as the translated patriarch Enoch, elevated to the status of "the lesser YHWH" (*YHWH ha-qatan*). He sits on a throne, bears the divine name, and acts as God's vice-regent.

The parallels with the Angel of the LORD tradition are striking:

| Attribute | Angel of the LORD (OT) | Metatron (Jewish Mysticism) | Christ (Christianity) |
|-----------|----------------------|---------------------------|---------------------|
| Bears God's name | Exodus 23:21 | 3 Enoch 12:5 | Philippians 2:9 |
| Called "Lord" / divine titles | Judges 6:14, Genesis 16:13 | "Lesser YHWH" | John 20:28 |
| Mediates God's presence | Exodus 33:14--15 | Throne-attendant | Hebrews 1:3 |
| Judges / rules | Judges 2:1--5 | Prince of the World | Matthew 25:31--32 |
| Sent by God | Exodus 23:20 | Sent on missions | John 17:3 |

The Metatron tradition represents a **Jewish parallel** to the Christian Angel-Christology --- a way of accounting for the "second divine figure" in the OT without identifying that figure with a divine Son. For the Metatron mystics, the figure is an exalted angel (originally human, in the Enoch traditions); for the Christians, the figure is the eternal Son of God. Both traditions are reading the same OT data and reaching for the same category --- a divine mediator who bears God's name and exercises God's authority.

## Patterns and Observations

### The Consistency of the Pre-Nicene Witness

The evidence surveyed in this chapter reveals a remarkable consistency across the first three centuries of Christianity. The following table summarizes the pre-Nicene witnesses and their positions:

| Author | Date | Location | Angel = Son? | Key Text |
|--------|------|----------|-------------|----------|
| Justin Martyr | c. 155--160 | Rome | **Yes** | *Dialogue with Trypho* 56--62, 126--128 |
| Theophilus of Antioch | c. 180 | Antioch | **Yes** | *To Autolycus* II.22 |
| Irenaeus of Lyon | c. 180 | Lyon (Gaul) | **Yes** | *Against Heresies* III.6, IV.7, IV.20 |
| Clement of Alexandria | c. 200 | Alexandria | **Yes** | *Stromata* VII.2 |
| Tertullian | c. 207--213 | Carthage | **Yes** | *Against Praxeas* 14--16; *Against Marcion* III.9 |
| Origen | c. 230 | Alexandria/Caesarea | **Yes** (cautiously) | *De Principiis* I.3; *Against Celsus* V.53 |
| Novatian | c. 250 | Rome | **Yes** | *On the Trinity* 18--26 |
| Eusebius | c. 315 | Caesarea | **Yes** | *Ecclesiastical History* I.2 |

The geographic spread is as notable as the temporal span. These authors wrote from Rome, Antioch, Lyon, Alexandria, Carthage, and Caesarea. They wrote in Greek and Latin. They addressed Jewish, pagan, Gnostic, Sabellian, and Marcionite opponents. They had different theological emphases and different philosophical frameworks. Yet on the specific question of whether the Angel of the LORD was the pre-incarnate Son, they are **unanimous**.

This unanimity is not the product of a single school or a single theological tradition. It represents a broad, cross-traditional consensus that the OT texts themselves --- not any particular interpretive framework --- generated the identification.

### The Jewish Evidence Confirms the Reading's Antiquity

The Jewish sources demonstrate that the "second divine power" reading of the Angel of the LORD passages is **older than Christianity**:

- **Philo** (pre-Christian) identified the Angel as the Logos, the "firstborn" image of God, a "second God."
- **The Targumim** substituted *Memra* ("Word") in passages where God appeared or acted directly, showing that Jewish interpreters felt the need for a divine intermediary.
- **The Talmud** warned against the "two powers in heaven" heresy, proving that the "second power" reading was widespread enough to require formal rabbinic refutation.
- **The Metatron traditions** developed a Jewish alternative to the Christian identification --- an exalted angel rather than a divine Son, but filling the same structural role.

The Christians did not invent the problem. They inherited it from the OT text and from Jewish exegetical tradition. Their answer (the pre-incarnate Son) was distinctively Christian; the question itself (Who is this second figure?) was Jewish before it was Christian.

### The Post-Nicene Shift Was Theological, Not Exegetical

The abandonment of the Angel-Christology was driven by the Arian controversy, not by any new evidence from the biblical text. Augustine's alternative reading rests on the philosophical principle of inseparable operations, not on a fresh exegesis of Genesis 16, Exodus 3, or Judges 13. The texts have not changed. The pre-Nicene reading of these texts was not shown to be exegetically wrong; it was abandoned because the "angel" language could be exploited by those who denied the Son's full deity.

This observation does not settle the question. Augustine may have been right that the incarnation is so unique that pre-incarnation appearances in bodily form are problematic. The inseparable-operations principle may genuinely require us to resist assigning the OT theophanies to one person of the Trinity. These are real theological arguments, not mere political calculations. But the student of this tradition must be honest: the pre-Nicene reading was the older reading, the more widespread reading, and the reading that grew directly out of the OT text.

## Summary

The evidence of Antiquity yields several clear findings:

**Where the evidence is strong:**
- The pre-Nicene church, from Justin Martyr through Eusebius, identified the Angel of the LORD with the pre-incarnate Son of God. This identification was unanimous among all surviving pre-Nicene authors who addressed the question.
- Jewish sources (Philo, the Targumim, rabbinic polemics) confirm that the "second divine power" reading of the Angel passages predates Christianity and was deeply rooted in Jewish exegesis.
- The identification was used against multiple opponents: Jews (Justin vs. Trypho), Gnostics (Irenaeus), modalists (Tertullian vs. Praxeas), Marcionites (Tertullian vs. Marcion), and pagans (Origen vs. Celsus). It was not a niche argument but the standard Christian reading of the OT theophanies.

**Where the evidence requires caution:**
- The pre-Nicene fathers used subordinationist language ("second God," "another God and Lord") that, while not intended to deny the Son's deity, could be --- and was --- misread as doing so. The Nicene definition of *homoousios* was a necessary correction, even if it required abandoning some pre-Nicene vocabulary.
- Augustine's alternative reading has genuine theological merit and should not be dismissed as mere capitulation to Arian pressure. The uniqueness of the incarnation and the unity of trinitarian action are real concerns.
- The Apostolic Fathers do not address the Angel-Christology question, and we cannot assume from their silence either agreement or disagreement.

**What this means for the study:**
The SCAR Quadrilateral asks what the ancient church believed. The answer, for the first three centuries, is clear: the Angel of the LORD was the pre-incarnate Son of God. This was not a peripheral opinion but the central argument for the Son's pre-existence and distinction from the Father. The post-Nicene shift complicates the picture but does not erase it. The reader must weigh the pre-Nicene consensus against the post-Nicene correction and decide whether the earliest Christians were reading the text rightly --- whether the Angel who said "I am the God of thy father" was indeed the one who would later say, "Before Abraham was, I am."
