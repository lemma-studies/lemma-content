# Chapter 4: Reality --- The Major Views Today

## Introduction

The first three chapters of this study laid a foundation of evidence. Chapter 1 (Scripture) surveyed every major Angel of the LORD passage in the Hebrew Bible, documenting a figure who speaks as YHWH, receives worship as YHWH, is identified as YHWH --- and yet is sent by YHWH, distinguished from YHWH, and addressed as a messenger. Chapter 2 (Christocentrism) examined the New Testament's claim that Christ was active in the Old Testament, while also presenting the Hebrews 1--2 argument that Christ is superior to all angels. Chapter 3 (Antiquity) traced the near-unanimous pre-Nicene identification of the Angel with the pre-incarnate Son, along with the Augustinian reversal that reshaped Western theology after Nicaea, and the Jewish parallels in Philo's Logos and the Targum Memra.

Now we must ask: **What do believers actually believe?**

This is the question of *Reality* --- the fourth pillar of the SCAR Quadrilateral. Reality does not mean "the real answer" in the sense of a verdict. It means: *What are the living theological positions held by real communities of faith, and what does each position produce in the lives of those who hold it?* The SCAR framework insists that theological positions be evaluated not only by their exegetical arguments but by their pastoral fruit --- what they yield in worship, in discipleship, in the believer's experience of God.

Four major views compete for the identity of the Angel of the LORD. Each has serious advocates. Each can mount a substantial biblical and theological case. Each produces recognizable pastoral fruit. And each faces honest challenges that its own adherents must reckon with.

This chapter presents all four views at their absolute strongest. The test is simple: a thoughtful adherent of each view should be able to read the section devoted to their position and say, *"Yes --- that is what I believe, and that is why I believe it."* We are not looking for a winner. We are looking for clarity.

The four views are:

1. **Christophany** --- the Angel is the pre-incarnate Christ, the second person of the Trinity appearing before his incarnation.
2. **Created Angel** --- the Angel is a created angelic being, perhaps the highest of all angels, who represents God so completely that he speaks in God's name.
3. **Theophany of the Father** --- the Angel is God himself (or the undifferentiated divine essence) manifesting in a form humans can encounter, with no "second person" involved.
4. **Literary/Revelatory Device** --- "the Angel of the LORD" is a narrative convention for describing divine encounters, not an ontological claim about a specific being.

Each view has its own inner logic, its own reading of the key texts, its own community of practice. Let each speak for itself.

## View 1: Christophany --- The Pre-Incarnate Christ

### Who Holds This View

The Christophany view is probably the most widely held position among conservative Protestants today. Its advocates include most evangelicals, Reformed theologians, many Baptists, many Pentecostals, and a significant portion of the broader Protestant world. Key modern advocates include Charles Ryrie, Wayne Grudem, John MacArthur, James Borland (whose monograph *Christ in the Old Testament* is the most sustained modern defense), and Michael Heiser (with important nuances about the divine council framework). Historically, this was the unanimous position of the pre-Nicene church fathers --- Justin Martyr, Irenaeus, Tertullian, Novatian, Eusebius of Caesarea --- who identified the Angel of the LORD with the pre-incarnate Logos without hesitation or qualification.

The view's pedigree is both ancient and broad. It was the consensus reading of Christianity for its first three centuries, and it remains the default position in evangelical seminaries, systematic theology textbooks, and popular Bible teaching today.

### The Core Claim

The Angel of the LORD is the second person of the Trinity --- God the Son --- appearing in visible, pre-incarnate form before his birth in Bethlehem. He is not a created being. He is not the Father manifesting himself. He is the eternal Son, exercising divine prerogatives because he IS God, yet distinct from the Father because the Trinity is a mystery of personal distinction within the one divine essence.

The Son did not begin to exist at the incarnation. He has always been. And before he took on permanent human nature in the womb of Mary, he appeared to his people in temporary, visible theophanies --- to Hagar in the wilderness, to Abraham at Mamre, to Jacob at Peniel, to Moses at the bush, to Joshua outside Jericho, to Gideon at the winepress, to Manoah and his wife, to Isaiah in the temple.

### The Argument

**1. The Identification Pattern**

The most powerful evidence for the Christophany view is the persistent pattern documented in Chapter 1: the Angel of the LORD speaks as God, is identified as God, exercises divine authority, receives worship --- yet is also sent, distinguished from YHWH, and described as a messenger. This pattern of *identification with simultaneous distinction* is precisely what trinitarian theology predicts. The Son is fully God (identification) and yet personally distinct from the Father (distinction).

Consider the burning bush. Exodus 3:2 introduces the scene:

> "And the angel of the LORD appeared unto him in a flame of fire out of the midst of a bush: and he looked, and, behold, the bush burned with fire, and the bush was not consumed."
> --- Exodus 3:2 (KJV)

But when the figure speaks, he speaks as God himself:

> "Moreover he said, I am the God of thy father, the God of Abraham, the God of Isaac, and the God of Jacob. And Moses hid his face; for he was afraid to look upon God."
> --- Exodus 3:6 (KJV)

The Angel IS God --- not God's representative delivering a message in the third person, but God himself speaking in the first person: "I am the God of thy father." And Moses responds not with the posture of one hearing a messenger but with the fear of one standing before God himself.

**2. Jacob's Parallel: The Angel as Redeemer**

Genesis 48:15--16 is one of the most theologically dense passages in the Pentateuch. As the aged Jacob blesses Joseph's sons, he invokes three descriptions of the divine reality that has governed his life:

> "And he blessed Joseph, and said, God, before whom my fathers Abraham and Isaac did walk, the God which fed me all my life long unto this day, The Angel which redeemed me from all evil, bless the lads; and let my name be named on them, and the name of my fathers Abraham and Isaac; and let them grow into a multitude in the midst of the earth."
> --- Genesis 48:15--16 (KJV)

The grammar is crucial. Jacob uses a singular verb --- "bless" (*yevarekh*) --- governing all three descriptions: "God before whom my fathers walked," "the God who fed me," and "the Angel who redeemed me." The three are grammatically one. Jacob is not asking God to bless the boys and also asking an angel to bless them. He is asking one being --- described in three ways --- to bless them.

The word "redeemed" (*go'el*) carries immense theological weight. The *go'el* is the kinsman-redeemer, the nearest relative who has the right and the obligation to rescue, ransom, and restore. In Isaiah, this title belongs exclusively to YHWH:

> "Thus saith the LORD the King of Israel, and his redeemer the LORD of hosts; I am the first, and I am the last; and beside me there is no God."
> --- Isaiah 44:6 (KJV)

> "As for our redeemer, the LORD of hosts is his name, the Holy One of Israel."
> --- Isaiah 47:4 (KJV)

Jacob calls the Angel his *go'el* --- his redeemer. In the Old Testament, only God redeems. The Christophany advocate sees here a clear witness: the Angel is not merely an agent who delivers God's message but the divine person who IS the redeemer of Israel.

**3. "My Name Is in Him"**

In Exodus 23:20--21, YHWH makes an extraordinary statement about the Angel he is sending before Israel:

> "Behold, I send an Angel before thee, to keep thee in the way, and to bring thee into the place which I have prepared. Beware of him, and obey his voice, provoke him not; for he will not pardon your transgressions: for my name is in him."
> --- Exodus 23:20--21 (KJV)

As Chapter 1 documented, the Hebrew word *shem* ("name") in the ancient Near East carried far more than a label. The *shem* of God is his character, his authority, his active presence. When YHWH says "my name is in him," he is saying that the Angel carries the divine presence itself. This explains why the Angel can exercise divine prerogatives --- forgiving or refusing to forgive transgressions, demanding absolute obedience --- because the divine *shem* is not merely *upon* him (as it is upon the temple) but *in* him.

The Christophany advocate connects this to John 17:11, where Jesus prays:

> "And now I am no more in the world, but these are in the world, and I come to thee. Holy Father, keep through thine own name those whom thou hast given me, that they may be one, as we are."
> --- John 17:11 (KJV)

And to Philippians 2:9--10:

> "Wherefore God also hath highly exalted him, and given him a name which is above every name: That at the name of Jesus every knee should bow, of things in heaven, and things in earth, and things under the earth;"
> --- Philippians 2:9--10 (KJV)

The Son carries the Father's name --- not as a title bestowed on a creature, but because the divine name is intrinsic to his being. The Angel of Exodus 23 carries the divine name because he IS the divine Son.

**4. Hosea's Interpretation**

Hosea 12:3--5 is especially significant because it represents one Old Testament author interpreting an earlier Angel of the LORD passage:

> "He took his brother by the heel in the womb, and by his strength he had power with God: Yea, he had power over the angel, and prevailed: he wept, and made supplication unto him: he found him in Bethel, and there he spake with us; Even the LORD God of hosts; the LORD is his memorial."
> --- Hosea 12:3--5 (KJV)

The sequence is unmistakable. Jacob wrestled with "God" (v.3). This is identified as wrestling with "the angel" (v.4). And this angel is then identified as "the LORD God of hosts" (v.5). Hosea is not writing in the second century AD with trinitarian categories at hand. He is an eighth-century BC prophet interpreting the Genesis narrative, and his interpretation is that the Angel IS the LORD God of hosts.

The Christophany advocate sees this as decisive intra-biblical evidence: the Old Testament itself identifies the Angel as YHWH, long before any Christian theologian made the claim.

**5. The New Testament Connections**

Chapter 2 documented the New Testament passages that identify Christ with Old Testament theophanic appearances. The key texts:

Paul, writing about the wilderness generation:

> "And did all drink the same spiritual drink: for they drank of that spiritual Rock that followed them: and that Rock was Christ."
> --- 1 Corinthians 10:4 (KJV)

The verb is emphatic. Paul does not say the rock *represented* Christ or *pointed to* Christ. He says the Rock WAS Christ. The one who accompanied Israel through the wilderness --- the same one called "the Angel" in Exodus 23 and "the Angel of his presence" in Isaiah 63:9 --- was the pre-incarnate Son.

Stephen, in his speech before the Sanhedrin, directly connects Christ to the Angel of the burning bush:

> "And when forty years were expired, there appeared to him in the wilderness of mount Sina an angel of the Lord in a flame of fire in a bush. When Moses saw it, he wondered at the sight: and as he drew near to behold it, the voice of the Lord came unto him, Saying, I am the God of thy fathers, the God of Abraham, and the God of Isaac, and the God of Jacob."
> --- Acts 7:30--32 (KJV)

Stephen identifies the speaker at the bush as "the Angel of the Lord" who is simultaneously "the God of Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob." His entire speech builds toward the claim that the Jewish authorities have always resisted the one God sent --- culminating in the "Just One" (Acts 7:52), Jesus Christ. The Angel at the bush and the Christ on the cross are, for Stephen, the same person.

John, reflecting on Isaiah's temple vision:

> "These things said Esaias, when he saw his glory, and spake of him."
> --- John 12:41 (KJV)

The "him" is Christ (see John 12:37--40). John's claim is extraordinary: when Isaiah saw the LORD sitting on his throne, high and lifted up, with the seraphim crying "Holy, holy, holy" (Isaiah 6:1--3), Isaiah saw the glory of Christ. The pre-incarnate Son was the one enthroned in the temple.

**6. The Pre-Nicene Consensus**

As Chapter 3 documented, the earliest church fathers --- the generation closest to the apostles and their immediate successors --- unanimously identified the Angel of the LORD with the pre-incarnate Logos. Justin Martyr built his entire dialogue with Trypho around this identification. Irenaeus argued that the Son was the one who appeared to Abraham, wrestled with Jacob, and spoke from the bush. Tertullian used the Angel appearances as evidence for the real distinction between Father and Son against the modalists. Novatian devoted extended chapters to proving that the one who appeared in the Old Testament was the Son, not the Father.

This was not a minority opinion or a speculative theological experiment. It was the universal reading of the pre-Nicene church. For the Christophany advocate, this near-unanimous patristic consensus carries significant weight: the Christians closest in time to the apostolic generation, working with the Greek text and the Jewish interpretive traditions, saw the Angel as Christ.

**7. The Invisibility Argument**

The New Testament insists repeatedly that no one has ever seen God the Father:

> "No man hath seen God at any time; the only begotten Son, which is in the bosom of the Father, he hath declared him."
> --- John 1:18 (KJV)

> "Not that any man hath seen the Father, save he which is of God, he hath seen the Father."
> --- John 6:46 (KJV)

> "Who only hath immortality, dwelling in the light which no man can approach unto; whom no man hath seen, nor can see."
> --- 1 Timothy 6:16 (KJV)

Yet the Old Testament records multiple instances where people SAW God --- Jacob says "I have seen God face to face" (Genesis 32:30); Moses, Aaron, Nadab, Abihu, and the seventy elders "saw the God of Israel" (Exodus 24:10); Isaiah cries "mine eyes have seen the King, the LORD of hosts" (Isaiah 6:5). If the Father has never been seen, then who was seen? The Christophany view answers: the Son. He is "the image of the invisible God" (Colossians 1:15), the one who has always made the unseen Father visible, knowable, and accessible.

**8. The Unity of Redemptive History**

For the Christophany advocate, the deepest argument is not any single proof-text but the coherence of the whole biblical narrative. The one who drew near to Hagar in her despair is the same one who said, "Come unto me, all ye that labour and are heavy laden, and I will give you rest" (Matthew 11:28). The one who wrestled with Jacob until daybreak is the same one who wrestled with sin and death at Calvary. The one who stood in the fire of the bush without being consumed is the same one who descended into the fire of divine judgment and emerged victorious.

If Christ is absent from the Old Testament --- if he only enters the story at Bethlehem --- then the Old Testament is a book about a God who is not yet fully revealed, and the incarnation is an interruption rather than a culmination. But if Christ was the one who walked with Israel from the beginning, then the incarnation is the natural climax of a story that was always about him.

### Pastoral Fruit

The Christophany view produces distinctive pastoral fruit that its adherents experience as transformative:

**It deepens worship.** When believers see Christ in the burning bush, at the threshing floor of Araunah, in the furnace with the three Hebrew youths, their sense of his personal involvement in human history expands immeasurably. He is not a distant deity who finally decided to get involved at Bethlehem. He has always been the one who comes.

**It reveals the unity of Scripture.** The same Savior who walked with Israel walked to Calvary. The Old Testament is not merely "background" for the real story that begins in Matthew. It IS the story --- the same story, with the same central character, unfolding across millennia. The Bible becomes one book with one hero.

**It strengthens trinitarian theology.** The distinction between Father and Son is not a New Testament invention imposed on earlier texts. It is woven into the fabric of the Old Testament itself --- in the Angel who IS YHWH yet speaks of YHWH in the third person, who carries the divine name yet is sent by the one whose name he carries. The doctrine of the Trinity does not have to be read into the Old Testament; it can be read out of it.

**It grounds the incarnation.** If the Son had never appeared before Bethlehem, then the incarnation is his first act of condescension. But if he had always been the one who drew near to his people --- in the garden, at the bush, at Peniel, in the furnace --- then the incarnation is not an interruption but a deepening of what he has always done. He who appeared temporarily in the Old Testament took on permanent human nature in the New. The incarnation fulfills what the theophanies anticipated.

**It makes the Old Testament personally Christ-centered.** Not merely typologically so --- not merely "this event reminds us of Christ" --- but actually, really, historically Christ-centered. He was there. He spoke. He acted. He redeemed.

### Honest Challenges

No responsible advocate of the Christophany view should ignore the following difficulties:

**The Hebrews 1--2 problem.** The entire argument of Hebrews 1 is that Christ is *superior* to the angels. "For unto which of the angels said he at any time, Thou art my Son, this day have I begotten thee?" (Hebrews 1:5). "Being made so much better than the angels, as he hath by inheritance obtained a more excellent name than they" (Hebrews 1:4). If Christ IS "the Angel of the LORD," does this not place him in the very category Hebrews is at pains to distinguish him from? The Christophany advocate must explain how Christ can be called *mal'akh* (messenger) in the Old Testament without being reduced to the angelic category that Hebrews rejects.

The standard response is that *mal'akh* is a *functional* term --- it describes what someone does (delivers a message), not what someone is (a created spirit being). Christ functioned as God's messenger without being an angel in the ontological sense, just as he functioned as a servant without being a slave in the ontological sense. Hebrews rejects the idea that Christ belongs to the created angelic order; it does not reject the idea that the Son delivered messages from the Father.

This response has real force, but it must be admitted that it requires careful theological distinction-making that the ordinary reader of Hebrews would not naturally arrive at. The tension is real.

**The post-Nicene rejection.** Augustine, the most influential theologian in Western church history, explicitly rejected the Christophany reading in *De Trinitate* II--III. His concern was not exegetical but theological: if the Son is "the Angel," Arians can argue that the Son is a subordinate being, a mere messenger. After Nicaea, the church was rightly vigilant against any formulation that could be used to deny the Son's full co-equality with the Father. John of Damascus, the great systematizer of Eastern Orthodox theology, followed a similar trajectory. The Christophany advocate must reckon with the fact that the post-Nicene church moved away from this reading, and it moved away for serious theological reasons.

**The anachronism question.** Did Abraham, Moses, or Hosea understand themselves to be encountering a "second person of the Trinity"? Almost certainly not. The doctrine of the Trinity was not articulated in its mature form until the fourth century AD. The Christophany advocate is not claiming that the Old Testament writers had a fully developed trinitarian theology. The claim is that the reality they encountered --- a divine figure who is both identified with and distinguished from YHWH --- is best explained by trinitarian theology, even though the writers themselves could not have articulated it in those terms. This is a legitimate hermeneutical move (we regularly understand earlier events more fully in light of later revelation), but it must be made with honesty about what the Old Testament writers did and did not understand.

**The category confusion risk.** *Mal'akh* means "messenger." Using it as a christological title risks conflating the Son with the created angelic order in popular understanding, even if the theological distinction is carefully maintained. The Arian crisis demonstrated exactly this danger: Arius could point to the Angel of the LORD passages and say, "See --- the Son is a creature, a messenger, subordinate to the Father." The Christophany view must be stated with precision to avoid inadvertently supporting the very heresy it opposes.

**The diversity of Angel passages.** Not every "angel of the LORD" passage demands a Christophany reading. Numbers 22:22--35 (Balaam's donkey) and 2 Samuel 24:16 (the destroying angel) involve angelic figures who act with divine authority but may well be created beings. The Christophany advocate must either argue that EVERY "angel of the LORD" is Christ (which strains some passages) or acknowledge that the phrase sometimes refers to created angels and sometimes to the pre-incarnate Son (which raises the question of how to tell the difference).

## View 2: Created Angel --- A Special Angelic Being

### Who Holds This View

The created angel view is held by many modern critical scholars who approach the text from a historical-critical perspective, by Jehovah's Witnesses (who identify the Angel as Michael the archangel), by some Jewish interpreters both ancient and modern, by Socinians and Unitarians who deny the Trinity, and by some scholars in the Churches of Christ and other restoration traditions. The Jewish *shaliah* (agency) principle is the key hermeneutical tool, though the view does not require any particular commitment to rabbinic theology.

It is worth noting that this view is also implicit in much popular-level Bible reading. Many Christians who have not thought carefully about the question simply assume that "the angel of the LORD" is an angel --- a powerful created being, perhaps Gabriel or Michael --- who delivers God's messages. This unreflective popular assumption shares the same basic logic as the scholarly version of the view.

### The Core Claim

The Angel of the LORD is a created angelic being --- perhaps the highest and most exalted of all angels --- who represents God so completely that he speaks in God's name and with God's full authority. He is not God. He is not a "second person" of a triune Godhead. He is the supreme example of the ancient principle that a king's authorized agent speaks as the king himself.

The reason the Angel can say "I am the God of Abraham" is not that he IS God but that he is God's fully authorized representative, empowered to speak in the first person on God's behalf --- just as a king's ambassador might say "I declare war" when it is the king, not the ambassador, who holds the sovereign authority.

### The Argument

**1. The *Shaliah* (Agency) Principle**

In Jewish law, a fundamental maxim governs the relationship between a sender and his representative: *sh'luho shel adam k'moto* --- "the agent of a man is as the man himself" (Mishnah Berakhot 5:5; Kiddushin 41b). This principle means that an authorized agent (*shaliah*) acts with the full legal authority of the one who sent him. What the agent does, the sender has done. What the agent says, the sender has said.

This principle was not invented by the rabbis. It reflects ancient Near Eastern diplomatic convention. When a king's envoy arrived at a foreign court, he spoke in the first person: "I, the Great King, say unto you..." The envoy was not claiming to BE the king. Everyone understood the convention. The envoy's words carried royal authority because the king had authorized them.

The created angel advocate argues that this convention explains everything puzzling about the Angel of the LORD. When the Angel says "I am the God of thy father" (Exodus 3:6), he is not claiming to be God. He is speaking as God's authorized *shaliah*, delivering God's words in the first person. When the Angel accepts worship, it is because worship offered to the authorized agent is received by the sender. When the text identifies the Angel as YHWH, it is because the agent IS "as the man himself" --- functionally, not ontologically.

**2. The Meaning of *Mal'akh***

The Hebrew word *mal'akh* means "messenger" --- one who is sent. This is a functional term describing a created being who serves the purposes of the one who sent him. The Christophany view asks this word to carry a weight it was never designed to bear. Nowhere in the Hebrew Bible does *mal'akh* describe a divine being. It describes prophets (Haggai 1:13), priests (Malachi 2:7), human messengers (Genesis 32:3), and heavenly servants (Psalm 103:20). In every case, the *mal'akh* is subordinate to the one who sends him.

If God intended to communicate that the one appearing in these theophanies was himself --- a divine person, co-equal with the Father --- why would he use a word that, in every other context, describes a creature? The word itself resists the Christophany reading.

**3. Hebrews 1--2: Christ Is NOT an Angel**

The epistle to the Hebrews makes the most sustained argument in the New Testament that Christ is categorically superior to the angels:

> "Being made so much better than the angels, as he hath by inheritance obtained a more excellent name than they. For unto which of the angels said he at any time, Thou art my Son, this day have I begotten thee? And again, I will be to him a Father, and he shall be to me a Son?"
> --- Hebrews 1:4--5 (KJV)

> "And again, when he bringeth in the firstbegotten into the world, he saith, And let all the angels of God worship him. And of the angels he saith, Who maketh his angels spirits, and his ministers a flame of fire. But unto the Son he saith, Thy throne, O God, is for ever and ever: a sceptre of righteousness is the sceptre of thy kingdom."
> --- Hebrews 1:6--8 (KJV)

> "But to which of the angels said he at any time, Sit on my right hand, until I make thine enemies thy footstool? Are they not all ministering spirits, sent forth to minister for them who shall be heirs of salvation?"
> --- Hebrews 1:13--14 (KJV)

The argument of Hebrews 1 is clear, forceful, and sustained: Christ is NOT an angel. Angels are created servants. Christ is the Son. Angels worship Christ. Christ sits at God's right hand; no angel ever has. The created angel advocate argues that this is the New Testament's own answer to the question: whatever the Angel of the LORD was, Christ is something categorically different and higher. To call Christ "the Angel of the LORD" is to undo what Hebrews labors to establish.

**4. The Old Testament Did Not Have Trinitarian Theology**

The doctrine of the Trinity was not articulated until the fourth century AD. The Old Testament writers --- Moses, the Judges narrator, Hosea --- were strict monotheists operating within the framework of the Shema:

> "Hear, O Israel: The LORD our God is one LORD:"
> --- Deuteronomy 6:4 (KJV)

Reading a "second person of the Trinity" into Genesis or Exodus imposes a later theological framework on texts that were written centuries before those categories existed. The Old Testament writers would have understood the Angel as God's chief messenger --- his supreme representative --- without ever conceiving of a "second divine person." Responsible exegesis requires reading texts in their original context, not through the lens of later doctrinal development.

**5. Angel Passages That Do Not Demand Christophany**

Not all Angel of the LORD passages fit the Christophany pattern. In Numbers 22:22--35, the angel of the LORD stands in the road to oppose Balaam. He acts with divine authority, but nothing in the passage identifies him as YHWH. He is a heavenly being executing a divine commission:

> "And God's anger was kindled because he went: and the angel of the LORD stood in the way for an adversary against him."
> --- Numbers 22:22 (KJV)

In 2 Samuel 24:16, the destroying angel is clearly distinct from YHWH who commands him:

> "And when the angel stretched out his hand upon Jerusalem to destroy it, the LORD repented him of the evil, and said to the angel that destroyed the people, It is enough: stay now thine hand."
> --- 2 Samuel 24:16 (KJV)

Here YHWH and the angel are in direct conversation, with YHWH issuing commands and the angel obeying. This is a straightforward created-being-serving-God scenario. If SOME Angel of the LORD passages clearly involve created beings, the burden of proof falls on the Christophany advocate to explain why OTHER passages must be read differently.

**6. The Metatron Tradition**

Later Jewish mysticism developed the figure of Metatron --- a supreme angelic being called the "lesser YHWH" (*YHWH ha-katan*) whose "name is like his Master's." In 3 Enoch 12:5, the divine name is placed upon Metatron, and he is given authority to speak on God's behalf, rule over the heavenly hosts, and represent God to humanity. This is precisely the description given to the Angel of the LORD in Exodus 23:20--21 --- a being who carries God's name and exercises God's authority while remaining a created servant.

The Metatron tradition shows that Judaism found a way to account for the Angel of the LORD phenomena without resort to trinitarian theology. A supremely exalted angel who carries the divine name is not God but is treated as God for all practical purposes. Whether one accepts the Metatron tradition as historically reliable or not, it demonstrates that the data in the text can be coherently read without positing a "second divine person."

**7. Daniel 10 and 12: The "Great Prince"**

The created angel view finds significant support in Daniel, where a heavenly being of extraordinary power and authority appears in terms reminiscent of the Angel of the LORD:

> "And at that time shall Michael stand up, the great prince which standeth for the children of thy people."
> --- Daniel 12:1 (KJV)

Michael is called "the great prince" --- an exalted heavenly figure with a special commission to protect Israel. Some advocates of the created angel view (especially Jehovah's Witnesses) identify Michael with the Angel of the LORD: the same supreme angelic being, Israel's guardian, who appears throughout the Old Testament as God's chief representative.

### Pastoral Fruit

The created angel view produces its own recognizable pastoral fruit:

**It preserves God's transcendence.** God works through agents and intermediaries without compromising his otherness, his holiness, his sovereign distance from the created order. He does not need to "come down" personally; he has servants of extraordinary power and faithfulness who carry his authority and accomplish his will.

**It respects the Old Testament on its own terms.** Rather than reading New Testament categories backward into texts that were written centuries earlier, the created angel view lets the Old Testament speak in its own voice. The Hebrew Bible presents a world populated by heavenly beings who serve God --- the *bene elohim* (sons of God), the heavenly council, the *mal'akhim* (messengers). The Angel of the LORD fits naturally into this world as its highest and most authoritative member.

**It avoids the complexity of the "angel = God" equation.** If the Angel IS God, then we must explain why God would call himself a "messenger," why the messenger language doesn't compromise divine dignity, and how a divine person can be "sent" without implying subordination. The created angel view has none of these problems. A created being who is sent by God and speaks on God's behalf is exactly what the word *mal'akh* describes.

**It aligns with Hebrews 1--2.** The New Testament's own argument about Christ and the angels is clear: Christ is categorically superior to all angels. The created angel view takes this argument at face value rather than requiring theological qualifications to reconcile it with a Christophany reading.

### Honest Challenges

**The redeemer problem.** Genesis 48:15--16 places "God" and "the Angel" in grammatical parallel, with a singular verb, and calls the Angel the *go'el* --- the redeemer. In the Old Testament, only God redeems. The agency principle can explain why an agent speaks God's words, but can it explain why an agent is called God's exclusive title? A king's ambassador may speak in the king's name, but the ambassador is never called "the king." The *go'el* language seems to exceed what the agency model can bear.

**The Hosea identification.** Hosea 12:3--5 identifies the Angel with "the LORD God of hosts." This is not a narrator's fluid language. It is a prophetic interpretation that explicitly equates the Angel with YHWH. The agency principle would have to argue that Hosea means "the agent who represented the LORD God of hosts" --- but that is not what the text says. The text says the Angel IS "the LORD God of hosts; the LORD is his memorial."

**The worship problem.** In Judges 13:19--20, Manoah offers a sacrifice and the Angel of the LORD ascends in the flame of the altar:

> "So Manoah took a kid with a meat offering, and offered it upon a rock unto the LORD: and the angel did wondrously; and Manoah and his wife looked on it. For it came to pass, when the flame went up toward heaven from off the altar, that the angel of the LORD ascended in the flame of the altar. And Manoah and his wife looked on it, and fell on their faces to the ground."
> --- Judges 13:19--20 (KJV)

The Angel ascends in the sacrificial flame --- accepting, or at least associated with, the offering that is made "unto the LORD." When Manoah realizes what has happened, his terror is absolute: "We shall surely die, because we have seen God" (Judges 13:22). This is not the response of a man who met a messenger. It is the response of a man who believes he met God. Similarly, Hagar calls the Angel *El Ro'i* --- "the God who sees me" (Genesis 16:13). The agency principle explains why a messenger speaks God's words, but it does not easily explain why human beings consistently react to this messenger as if they have encountered God himself.

**Paul's blunt identification.** Paul does not say the Rock *represented* Christ or *prefigured* Christ. He says "that Rock WAS Christ" (1 Corinthians 10:4). The agency principle requires that Paul be speaking loosely, using "was" when he means "represented." But Paul is making a theological argument, not speaking casually. The directness of his identification is a problem for the created angel view.

**The pre-Nicene consensus.** The earliest church fathers --- the generation closest to the apostles --- unanimously rejected the created angel reading. Justin, Irenaeus, Tertullian, Novatian, and Eusebius all argued that the Angel was NOT a created being but the divine Logos. The created angel advocate must explain why every major theologian in the first three centuries of Christianity got this wrong.

**No explicit Michael identification.** While some advocates identify the Angel of the LORD as Michael the archangel, no Old Testament or New Testament passage explicitly makes this connection. Daniel's Michael and the Angel of the LORD appear to be distinct figures. The identification is an inference, not a textual claim.

## View 3: Theophany of the Father --- Direct Divine Manifestation

### Who Holds This View

This view was championed by Augustine of Hippo in *De Trinitate* (books II--III), and it became the dominant Western reading after the fourth century. It is held in its mature form by much of the Eastern Orthodox tradition, by Oneness Pentecostals (who reject the Trinity in favor of modalism), and by some modern systematic theologians who follow Augustine's caution about assigning specific OT appearances to specific persons of the Trinity.

Augustine's influence on this question is difficult to overstate. His rejection of the pre-Nicene Christophany reading reshaped Western theology for over a thousand years. When the medieval church read the Angel of the LORD passages, they read them through Augustinian lenses --- and those lenses did not see the pre-incarnate Son.

### The Core Claim

The Angel of the LORD is God himself --- the one God, undifferentiated by person --- manifesting in a form that humans can perceive. The "angel" language describes the *mode* of God's appearing, not a *separate being* who appears. God is both the sender and the sent, both the one on the throne and the one in the bush, because God in his sovereign freedom can manifest himself without requiring a "second person" to serve as intermediary.

In the Oneness Pentecostal formulation, God is one person who manifests in different modes --- as Father in creation and sovereignty, as Son in redemption, as Spirit in indwelling. The Angel of the LORD is God in his "going forth" mode, his self-manifestation to his people. There is no need for a distinct second person because the one God can do all things, including appearing visibly to his creatures.

In the Augustinian formulation, the question is more carefully nuanced: we cannot determine from the Old Testament text alone *which* person of the Trinity appeared. It may have been the Son, the Father, the Spirit, or the Trinity acting as a whole. The pre-Nicene assumption that it was always the Son is unwarranted.

### The Argument

**1. The Primacy of Divine Unity**

The Old Testament's foundational theological claim is the oneness of God:

> "Hear, O Israel: The LORD our God is one LORD: And thou shalt love the LORD thy God with all thine heart, and with all thy soul, and with all thy might."
> --- Deuteronomy 6:4--5 (KJV)

> "I am the LORD, and there is none else, there is no God beside me."
> --- Isaiah 45:5 (KJV)

> "See now that I, even I, am he, and there is no god with me: I kill, and I make alive; I wound, and I heal: neither is there any that can deliver out of my hand."
> --- Deuteronomy 32:39 (KJV)

The theophany advocate argues that reading a "second God" or a "second person" into these narratives undermines the force of Old Testament monotheism. When the bush burns and God speaks, the simplest and most faithful reading is that GOD appeared --- not a second divine person who is somehow God and yet not the God who speaks in Deuteronomy 6:4.

The early Christians who encountered the Angel texts were already committed to a binitarian or trinitarian framework. They saw what they expected to see. But the Hebrew text itself --- read in its own context, by its own community --- speaks of one God who acts, one God who appears, one God who redeems.

**2. The Simplest Reading of the Text**

Consider Exodus 3 without trinitarian presuppositions. The narrative flow is straightforward:

> "And the angel of the LORD appeared unto him in a flame of fire out of the midst of a bush."
> --- Exodus 3:2 (KJV)

> "And when the LORD saw that he turned aside to see, God called unto him out of the midst of the bush, and said, Moses, Moses. And he said, Here am I."
> --- Exodus 3:4 (KJV)

> "Moreover he said, I am the God of thy father, the God of Abraham, the God of Isaac, and the God of Jacob."
> --- Exodus 3:6 (KJV)

Verse 2 introduces the scene: the "angel of the LORD" appears. But from verse 4 onward, the speaker is identified simply as "the LORD" and "God." The theophany advocate reads this as a single event: God appeared. The "angel" in verse 2 describes the mode of appearance (a visible, flame-like manifestation), not a separate being who then steps aside while God speaks. God himself came in angelic guise --- and then the narrative drops the angelic language and simply calls him what he is: the LORD.

This reading has the virtue of simplicity. It does not require distinguishing between two divine persons, explaining how one person can be sent by the other, or reconciling the "messenger" terminology with divine dignity. God appeared. That is the story.

**3. Augustine's Argument from *De Trinitate***

Augustine's treatment in *De Trinitate* II--III is the most sophisticated version of this view. His argument runs as follows:

*First:* The pre-Nicene fathers assumed that the Son was the one who appeared in every Old Testament theophany. But this assumption rests on a chain of reasoning that is not itself found in Scripture: (a) the Father has never been seen, (b) someone was seen in the OT, (c) therefore the Son was seen. Augustine challenges step (c): why must it be the Son? Could it not be the Trinity acting as a whole? Could it not be the Father appearing through a created medium (a flame, a voice, a bodily form)?

*Second:* The visible forms in the Old Testament --- the flame of the bush, the cloud and fire of Sinai, the "man" who wrestled Jacob --- are not the divine nature itself but *created signs* through which God makes himself known. Just as God spoke through the mouth of Balaam's donkey without *being* the donkey, God appeared through angels, flames, and visions without any particular person of the Trinity being exclusively identified with those appearances.

*Third:* If we say the Son appeared in every Old Testament theophany, we risk implying that the Son is *more visible* than the Father, which implies that the Son is *more material* or *more creaturely* than the Father --- exactly the Arian conclusion. The safest course is to say that the one God appeared through created media, without specifying which person was involved.

Augustine's concern is not primarily exegetical but pastoral and theological: in the post-Arian context, any formulation that could distinguish the Son from the Father in terms of visibility, materiality, or accessibility to creatures is dangerous. Better to attribute the theophanies to God without specification than to hand ammunition to heretics.

**4. The Old Testament Never Identifies the Angel as "the Son" or "the Word"**

This is a simple but powerful observation. No Old Testament passage says, "the Angel of the LORD, who is the second person of the Godhead" or "the Angel, who is the Word." The identification of the Angel with the Son is a Christian theological inference drawn by reading the OT through the NT. The theophany advocate argues that this inference, while understandable, is not required by the text and may actually distort the text's own claims.

When the Angel says "I am the God of thy father" (Exodus 3:6), the simplest reading is that God is identifying himself as God --- not that a second divine person is identifying himself as God while the first divine person remains hidden. The trinitarian reading adds a layer of complexity that the text itself does not demand.

**5. God's Transcendence Does Not Prevent Appearance**

The Christophany view often argues that the Father CANNOT be seen (John 1:18) and therefore MUST NOT have been the one who appeared. The theophany advocate challenges this reasoning. "No man hath seen God at any time" may refer to seeing God's full, unveiled essence --- his infinite glory --- not to any mediated appearance whatsoever.

Moses saw God's "back parts" but not his face:

> "And he said, Thou canst not see my face: for there shall no man see me, and live. And the LORD said, Behold, there is a place by me, and thou shalt stand upon a rock: And it shall come to pass, while my glory passeth by, that I will put thee in a clift of the rock, and will cover thee with my hand while I pass by: And I will take away mine hand, and thou shalt see my back parts: but my face shall not be seen."
> --- Exodus 33:20--23 (KJV)

God can appear partially, mediately, in diminished glory, without his full essence being visible. A partial theophany does not require a second divine person. It requires only that the infinite God accommodates himself to the limitations of his finite creatures --- which is, after all, what all divine revelation involves.

**6. The Oneness Perspective**

For Oneness Pentecostals, the Angel of the LORD is simply God manifesting himself --- the same one God who later manifested as Jesus of Nazareth and who now manifests as the Holy Spirit. There is one divine person, not three, and the various "persons" of traditional trinitarianism are modes of the one God's activity.

In this framework, the question "Was it the Father or the Son who appeared?" is malformed. There is only one God. He appeared. The "angel" language is anthropomorphic accommodation --- a way of describing God's visible presence without implying that someone SAW the full divine essence.

The strength of the Oneness reading is its radical simplicity. It has no "two persons" to reconcile, no "sending" relationship to explain, no Hebrews 1 problem. God appeared in the Old Testament. God appeared as Jesus in the New Testament. God appears as the Spirit now. One God, one story, no complexity.

### Pastoral Fruit

**It preserves divine unity with maximum simplicity.** The Shema is the heartbeat of biblical faith: "The LORD our God is one LORD." The theophany view affirms this oneness without the complexity of trinitarian distinctions within the Godhead. For believers who struggle with how "three persons" can be "one God," this view offers a clear, simple affirmation: the one God who spoke to Moses is the one God who died on Calvary. Full stop.

**It avoids the "two Gods" problem.** Throughout history, both Jews and thoughtful Christians have worried that trinitarianism implies two Gods (or three). The theophany view eliminates this concern. There is one God. He appeared in the Old Testament. He became incarnate in the New Testament. There is no "second God" standing beside the first.

**It emphasizes God's direct, personal care.** In the theophany view, when God appeared to Hagar, it was not a delegate or an intermediary. It was GOD --- the same God who created the heavens and the earth, the same God who parted the Red Sea, the same God who will judge the nations. He came personally. He heard her weeping. He saw her need. The comfort of this view is the comfort of directness: God himself draws near.

**It respects the Shema.** Whatever else can be said about the Angel of the LORD, the theophany view keeps the center of gravity where the Old Testament puts it: on the absolute oneness of God.

### Honest Challenges

**The third-person problem.** If the Angel IS the Father (or the undifferentiated God), why does the Angel sometimes speak of YHWH in the third person? In Zechariah 3:2:

> "And the LORD said unto Satan, The LORD rebuke thee, O Satan; even the LORD that hath chosen Jerusalem rebuke thee: is not this a brand plucked out of the fire?"
> --- Zechariah 3:2 (KJV)

The one identified as "the LORD" says "the LORD rebuke thee" --- referring to YHWH in the third person. This pattern --- YHWH speaking of YHWH as another --- is exactly what trinitarian theology predicts (the Son speaking of the Father, or vice versa) but is difficult to explain if only one divine person is present. Why would God refer to himself in the third person?

**The New Testament identifies Christ, not the Father.** When the New Testament connects Old Testament theophanies to a specific divine person, it consistently identifies Christ:

> "And did all drink the same spiritual drink: for they drank of that spiritual Rock that followed them: and that Rock was Christ."
> --- 1 Corinthians 10:4 (KJV)

> "These things said Esaias, when he saw his glory, and spake of him."
> --- John 12:41 (KJV)

If any person of the Trinity could have appeared in the Old Testament, why does the New Testament always and only identify the Son? Paul does not say "that Rock was God" or "that Rock was the Father." He says it was *Christ*. John does not say Isaiah saw "God's glory" generically. He says Isaiah saw *his* --- Christ's --- glory. The theophany view must explain why the New Testament is so specific if the Old Testament was so generic.

**The pre-Nicene consensus rejected this reading.** The first three centuries of Christian theology unanimously identified the Angel as the Son, not the Father. Tertullian specifically argued against the theophany-of-the-Father reading in *Against Praxeas*, calling it Sabellian (modalist). The theophany advocate must reckon with the fact that this view was the *heretical* position in the second and third centuries --- the very position the church rejected before Nicaea made it safe for Augustine to rehabilitate it.

**The modalist problem.** In its Oneness Pentecostal form, this view IS modalism --- the doctrine that Father, Son, and Spirit are not distinct persons but modes of the one God's activity. The church rejected modalism in the third century (Sabellius was condemned, Praxeas was refuted by Tertullian), and the ecumenical creeds explicitly affirm three distinct persons. The Oneness version of the theophany view requires rejecting the creedal consensus of Christianity.

In its Augustinian form, the view is not modalist --- Augustine affirmed three persons. But Augustine's argument that "any person could have appeared" effectively prevents the identification that the New Testament seems to make. If the NT says it was Christ, and Augustine says we cannot know which person it was, then Augustine is contradicting the NT's own identification.

**The "simplicity" argument cuts both ways.** The claim that the theophany reading is "simpler" assumes that simpler is better. But the text itself is not simple. The Angel IS YHWH and yet speaks of YHWH in the third person. He carries the divine name and yet is "sent." He accepts worship and yet is called a messenger. The data is complex, and a complex explanation (the distinction between two divine persons) may be more faithful to the complexity of the data than a simple explanation that smooths over the tensions.

## View 4: Literary/Revelatory Device --- No Ontological Claim

### Who Holds This View

This view is held by some critical scholars, some mainline Protestant theologians, and some academic Old Testament scholars who resist importing systematic theological categories into ancient narrative texts. Its advocates are often found in university departments of religious studies or biblical studies programs that prioritize historical-critical method. It is less common in confessional settings and more common in academic ones.

It is worth distinguishing this view from pure skepticism. Many scholars who hold this view are sincere believers who affirm the reality of divine revelation. Their claim is not that God did not encounter people in the Old Testament but that the *literary form* in which those encounters are narrated should not be pressed into service as an ontological claim about a specific being --- whether divine or angelic.

### The Core Claim

"The Angel of the LORD" is a literary convention that Israelite narrators used to describe divine encounters. The fluidity between "Angel" and "God" in the narratives is not an ontological statement about a second divine person or a specific angelic being. It is a *narrative technique* --- a way of preserving both God's transcendence (he is too holy to walk about casually among mortals) and his immanence (he really does encounter people, really does intervene, really does speak).

When the narrator writes "the angel of the LORD appeared" and then shifts to "the LORD said," this is not a claim about two beings present in the scene. It is the storyteller moving between registers --- introducing the encounter with "angel" language (which signals "this is a mediated divine encounter") and then dropping the mediating language to let God speak directly. The technique serves the theological purpose of the narrative, not an ontological claim about the Angel's nature.

### The Argument

**1. Different Authors, Different Centuries, No Unified "Angel Theology"**

The Angel of the LORD appears in texts spanning centuries of Israelite history --- from the patriarchal narratives (Genesis) to the monarchy (Judges, Samuel) to the postexilic period (Zechariah). These texts were composed by different authors in different historical contexts with different theological concerns.

Reading them as a single coherent narrative about one figure --- whether the pre-incarnate Christ, a specific archangel, or the Father --- may impose a systematic theological unity that the texts themselves do not claim. Genesis does not say "the Angel who appeared to Hagar is the same one who will appear to Moses." Judges does not say "the Angel who appeared to Gideon is the one Hosea will later identify as YHWH." The connecting thread that links all these appearances into a single figure is a reader's construction, not a textual claim.

**2. The Fluidity Is Literary, Not Ontological**

The alternation between "Angel" and "YHWH" in the narratives follows a recognizable literary pattern. The "angel" language typically appears at the beginning of the encounter --- in the narrator's introduction --- while "YHWH" language appears when direct speech begins. This is a narrative frame, not an ontological claim.

Consider Judges 6:11--14:

> "And there came an angel of the LORD, and sat under an oak which was in Ophrah, that pertained unto Joash the Abiezrite: and his son Gideon threshed wheat by the winepress, to hide it from the Midianites. And the angel of the LORD appeared unto him, and said unto him, The LORD is with thee, thou mighty man of valour."
> --- Judges 6:11--12 (KJV)

> "And the LORD looked upon him, and said, Go in this thy might, and thou shalt save Israel from the hand of the Midianites: have not I sent thee?"
> --- Judges 6:14 (KJV)

The narrator introduces the figure as "an angel of the LORD" (v.11--12), but by verse 14, the narrator calls the same figure "the LORD." The literary-device advocate reads this not as a claim that the angel IS ontologically YHWH but as the narrator's shift from mediated language ("an angel came") to direct language ("the LORD said") --- a standard feature of theophanic narrative in the ancient Near East.

**3. Ancient Near Eastern Parallels**

Israel's literary conventions did not develop in isolation. The cultures surrounding Israel also narrated divine encounters using "messenger of the god" language. In Mesopotamian and Egyptian texts, a god's messenger may speak in the god's name, and the narrative may shift between referring to the messenger and referring to the god. This does not imply a "second divine person" or a specific angelic being. It is a storytelling convention.

The literary-device advocate argues that Israel's Angel of the LORD narratives participate in this broader ANE tradition of narrating divine encounters through messenger language. The Israelite narratives are theologically distinctive (because YHWH is not like the pagan gods), but their literary technique is culturally situated.

**4. The Old Testament Does Not Systematize**

No Old Testament writer ever says, "The Angel of the LORD who appeared to Abraham is the same figure who appeared to Moses." No passage offers a systematic theology of the Angel. The individual narratives present individual encounters. The connection between them is a theological construction built by later readers --- by the pre-Nicene fathers, by medieval theologians, by modern systematic theologians.

This does not mean the connection is wrong. It means it is an *interpretation*, not a datum. The literary-device view asks us to sit with the individual narratives before rushing to systematize them, to hear what each story says on its own terms before asking what all the stories say together.

**5. The New Testament Reinterprets**

When Paul says "that Rock was Christ" (1 Corinthians 10:4) or John says Isaiah saw Christ's glory (John 12:41), they are engaging in *christological rereading* of Old Testament texts. This is a legitimate and important hermeneutical practice. But it is *reinterpretation* --- reading earlier texts through later theological lenses. It tells us what the New Testament authors believed about Christ's relationship to the Old Testament. It does not necessarily tell us what the Old Testament authors themselves intended.

The distinction matters. The literary-device view affirms that the New Testament authors saw Christ in the Old Testament. It simply notes that this is a *theological reading* imposed on texts that, in their original context, may not have been making the same claim. The question "What did Moses mean?" and the question "What does Paul see in Moses?" may have different answers.

### Pastoral Fruit

**It liberates readers from over-systematizing.** Ancient texts are not systematic theology textbooks. They are narratives, poems, oracles, and laws, written over centuries by different authors in different contexts. The literary-device view gives readers permission to appreciate the diversity and richness of the biblical text without forcing every passage into a single theological framework.

**It respects the Old Testament on its own terms.** The Hebrew Bible has its own voice, its own concerns, its own theological emphases. Before asking "What does this passage tell us about the Trinity?" we should ask "What was this passage saying to its original audience?" The literary-device view insists on the priority of the first question.

**It avoids premature closure.** In academic and pastoral settings, premature theological conclusions can shut down genuine inquiry. The literary-device view keeps the question open: "What is the Angel of the LORD?" may not have a single, definitive answer --- and that is acceptable. Mystery is not a failure of theology but a feature of it.

**It allows the Old Testament to speak before being harmonized with the New.** When we rush to read every Old Testament passage through New Testament lenses, we risk losing what the Old Testament says on its own. The literary-device view asks us to listen to Genesis as Genesis, to Judges as Judges, to Zechariah as Zechariah --- and only then to ask how the New Testament reads them.

### Honest Challenges

**The consistency problem.** If the "angel of the LORD" were merely a literary convention with no ontological referent, we might expect significant variation in how different authors use it. But the pattern documented in Chapter 1 is remarkably consistent across centuries and genres: the Angel appears, speaks as God, is identified as God, exercises divine prerogatives, yet is sent by God and described as a messenger. This consistency across different authors, different centuries, and different literary contexts suggests something more than a narrative technique. Literary conventions do not typically produce this level of theological coherence across such a wide body of literature.

**Jacob speaks in-world.** Genesis 48:15--16 is not a narrator's frame. It is a character --- Jacob --- speaking within the narrative, blessing his grandsons, and naming "the Angel which redeemed me from all evil" as the one he wants to bless them. This is not a storytelling technique. It is a theological claim made by a character in the story about the identity of the being who has protected and redeemed him. If the Angel is only a literary device, Jacob does not know it.

**Hosea systematizes.** The literary-device view argues that no Old Testament writer systematizes the Angel traditions. But Hosea 12:3--5 does exactly that. Hosea takes the Genesis narrative of Jacob wrestling, identifies the opponent as "the angel," and then identifies the angel as "the LORD God of hosts." This is one Old Testament author interpreting another, drawing a direct line between the Angel and YHWH. The systematizing impulse is not a later Christian invention; it is present in the Hebrew Bible itself.

**The New Testament treats the figure as real.** Whatever we say about literary conventions, the New Testament authors clearly treat the Angel of the LORD as a real being, not a narrative technique. Stephen, in Acts 7:30--38, narrates the burning bush encounter as a historical event involving a real angelic figure who is also God. Paul, in 1 Corinthians 10:4, identifies a real person --- Christ --- as the one who accompanied Israel. The literary-device view must either argue that the New Testament authors misunderstood the Old Testament literary convention or that the New Testament's christological reading is a legitimate theological development that goes beyond what the OT authors intended. The first option is problematic for anyone who takes the New Testament's authority seriously. The second is defensible but must be stated openly.

**It risks flattening the text.** By reducing the Angel to a narrative convention, this view may dismiss the theological claims the authors appear to be making. When Manoah says "We shall surely die, because we have seen God" (Judges 13:22), he is not commenting on a literary technique. He is expressing genuine terror at a genuine encounter. When Hagar names the Angel *El Ro'i* (Genesis 16:13), she is not labeling a narrative frame. She is naming the God who saw her. The literary-device view must reckon with the in-world reactions of the characters, which consistently treat the Angel as a real, terrifying, divine presence.

**Historical consensus runs against it.** Across the vast majority of Christian and Jewish history, readers have understood the Angel of the LORD as a real being --- not a literary convention. The pre-Nicene fathers, the medieval church, the Reformers, the rabbis, and the vast majority of modern readers all treat the Angel as ontologically real. The literary-device view is a distinctly modern academic reading. This does not make it wrong, but it does mean it stands against the overwhelming weight of interpretive tradition.

## Pastoral Considerations

Regardless of which view a believer holds, several cross-cutting pastoral truths deserve attention. These are not arguments for or against any position. They are wisdom for how the church handles a question that touches on deep mysteries and stirs deep convictions.

### 1. This Is an Intra-Family Debate

All four views --- except possibly the literary-device view in its most skeptical form --- affirm the reality of God's encounters with his people in the Old Testament. Views 1 (Christophany), 3 (Theophany of the Father), and most advocates of View 2 (Created Angel) affirm the full deity of Christ. The debate is about the *identity* of the Angel of the LORD, not about the *nature* of God or the *divinity* of Christ.

A believer who sees the Angel as the pre-incarnate Christ and a believer who follows Augustine's theophany reading worship the same triune God and confess the same risen Lord. They disagree about how Christ was present in the Old Testament, not about whether Christ is God. The Christophany debate should never become a test of orthodoxy or a litmus test for fellowship.

Even the created angel view, in most of its forms, is held by believers who affirm Christ's deity on other grounds. A Christian who believes the Angel of the LORD was a created being but who confesses that "Jesus Christ is Lord, to the glory of God the Father" (Philippians 2:11) stands within the household of faith. The Jehovah's Witness version of this view, which denies Christ's deity entirely, is a different matter --- but the view itself does not require that denial.

### 2. The Identity of the Angel Does Not Determine Salvation

No creed in the history of Christianity has included a statement about the identity of the Angel of the LORD. The Apostles' Creed, the Nicene Creed, the Chalcedonian Definition, and the Athanasian Creed are all silent on this question. It is not a matter of first-order doctrine. A believer can be fully orthodox, fully saved, fully faithful, and fully uncertain about who the Angel of the LORD is.

This does not mean the question is unimportant. It means the question belongs to the category of theological inquiry --- the church thinking carefully about what Scripture says and means --- rather than to the category of confessional requirements. Christians should study it with rigor and discuss it with charity.

### 3. The Question Is HOW, Not WHETHER

For the majority of Christians, the real question is not whether Christ is God but *how Christ was present in the Old Testament*. The Christophany view says he was present as the Angel, visibly and personally. The theophany-of-the-Father view says God (including the Son) was present but we cannot isolate the Son as the specific one who appeared. The literary-device view says Christ was present in the sense that all of Scripture ultimately points to him, even if he was not personally, visibly present in every theophanic encounter.

These are different answers to the same question, and the question itself affirms what matters most: Christ is Lord of all Scripture, Old Testament and New.

### 4. Where Scripture Is Genuinely Ambiguous, Charity Must Govern

The Angel of the LORD question IS genuinely ambiguous in places. The data from Chapter 1 resists easy categorization. The New Testament connections from Chapter 2 are powerful but not exhaustive. The patristic evidence from Chapter 3 is near-unanimous but reversed after Nicaea. Honest scholars and faithful believers land in different places.

Where honest ambiguity exists, the love and unity commands apply with full force:

> "Beloved, let us love one another: for love is of God; and every one that loveth is born of God, and knoweth God."
> --- 1 John 4:7 (KJV)

> "A new commandment I give unto you, That ye love one another; as I have loved you, that ye also love one another. By this shall all men know that ye are my disciples, if ye have love one to another."
> --- John 13:34--35 (KJV)

> "Him that is weak in the faith receive ye, but not to doubtful disputations."
> --- Romans 14:1 (KJV)

> "Let us therefore follow after the things which make for peace, and things wherewith one may edify another."
> --- Romans 14:19 (KJV)

The test of our theology is not only whether we get the answer right but whether we treat one another rightly in the process of seeking the answer. A theologian who holds the correct view of the Angel of the LORD but holds it with arrogance and contempt for those who disagree has failed a more important test than the exegetical one.

### 5. The Richest Reading May Be the One That Generates the Most Worship

This is not a hermeneutical principle in the formal sense. It is a pastoral observation. When the question is genuinely ambiguous --- when the text can be responsibly read in more than one way --- there is wisdom in asking: *Which reading draws me closer to God? Which reading deepens my worship? Which reading makes me love Christ more?*

If seeing Christ in the burning bush deepens your awe of his eternal, active love for his people, pursue that reading. Let it feed your devotion. If seeing the Father's direct, personal care for Hagar in the wilderness strengthens your trust in his nearness, receive that gift. Let it deepen your prayer.

The Angel of the LORD passages are, at their irreducible core, stories about God drawing near. Before they are arguments to be won, they are encounters to be wondered at. Hagar, alone in the desert, weeping --- and God finds her. Jacob, terrified of his brother, fleeing in the night --- and God wrestles him into blessing. Moses, a fugitive tending sheep in the wilderness --- and God speaks from a bush that burns but is not consumed. Gideon, the weakest man in the weakest clan, hiding his harvest from the enemy --- and God sits down under his oak tree and calls him a mighty man of valor.

Whatever we conclude about the *identity* of the figure in these stories, we must not lose the *wonder* of the stories themselves. The God of Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob is a God who comes. He is not distant. He is not detached. He finds people in their lowest moments and speaks their names. That is the testimony of every Angel of the LORD passage, and that testimony stands regardless of which view one holds.

> --- Isaiah 57:15 (KJV)

## Summary

This chapter has presented four major theological views of the Angel of the LORD, each at its strongest, with its best arguments, its pastoral fruit, and its honest challenges.

**The Christophany view** sees the Angel as the pre-incarnate Son of God --- fully divine, personally present, active in Israel's history from the beginning. Its strength is the weight of the identification pattern, the New Testament connections, and the pre-Nicene consensus. Its challenge is the Hebrews 1--2 argument and the post-Nicene rejection.

**The created angel view** sees the Angel as God's supreme representative --- a created being who speaks with divine authority under the *shaliah* principle. Its strength is the plain meaning of *mal'akh*, the Hebrews 1 argument, and respect for the Old Testament's own categories. Its challenge is the *go'el* language, the Hosea identification, and the worship the Angel receives.

**The theophany-of-the-Father view** sees the Angel as God himself appearing without a "second person" involved. Its strength is the simplicity of divine unity and the Augustinian caution against specifying which person appeared. Its challenge is the third-person pattern, the New Testament's consistent identification of Christ, and the modalist pedigree of its strongest form.

**The literary-device view** sees the Angel as a narrative convention for describing divine encounters. Its strength is its respect for the literary character of the text and its resistance to premature systematization. Its challenge is the consistency of the pattern across centuries, the in-world character reactions, and the New Testament's treatment of the figure as ontologically real.

What emerges from this survey is not a winner but a tension --- a productive, reverent tension between four readings that each illuminate something the others risk obscuring. The Christophany view illuminates Christ's eternal presence. The created angel view illuminates the majesty of God's transcendent otherness. The theophany view illuminates the simplicity of divine unity. The literary-device view illuminates the richness of the text as text.

No single view captures everything the biblical data demands. Each view has its own irreducible strength --- a piece of the evidence it handles better than any other view --- and each has its own irreducible weakness --- a piece of the evidence it must strain to accommodate.

Chapter 5 (Synthesis) will ask whether these tensions can be held together, whether the SCAR Quadrilateral as a whole points toward any resolution, and what the implications are for how the church reads the Old Testament, understands Christ, and worships the God who has always --- always --- drawn near to his people.
