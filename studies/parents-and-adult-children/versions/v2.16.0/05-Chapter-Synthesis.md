# Chapter 5 — Synthesis

## Overview (Synthesis)

This chapter brings the four SCAR components together. Where do they
agree? Where do they leave tension? What remains for the reader — the
parent, the adult child, the pastor — to weigh?

Before the four-component summary and the presentation of views, one
biblical pattern deserves to be surfaced on its own. It cuts across
every view the chapter will subsequently present, and it emerges from
Scripture more consistently than any single view. It is the pattern
of *what shape the parent's love takes* when the adult child chooses.

## 5.0 The Biblical Pattern of the Parent's Posture

Scripture is remarkably consistent about one thing that the
governance-focused debates often miss. Where Scripture describes God's
own parenthood, or narrates human parents relating to adult offspring,
three distinct postures recur. They are not competitors; each fits
different circumstances, and mature parenthood in Scripture practices
all three at different times toward the same child.

The three postures are:

1. **Release-and-await** — the parent honors the adult child's
   autonomous choice, even a ruinous one, without withdrawing love.
2. **Intercession-without-restraint** — the parent brings the adult
   child to Christ when the parent cannot restrain, heal, or reach.
3. **Dedication** — the parent releases the child *to God* rather than
   to the world, in vocation, calling, or self-offering.

Each is grounded in a distinct scriptural pattern. None of the four
views presented in §5.3 (nor any answer to the orthogonal
Arrangement Question) makes any of these postures impossible, but
some views tend to emphasize one over the others. Naming the three
directly gives the reader a scriptural vocabulary for what mature
Christian parenthood actually looks like across the arc of an adult
child's life.

All three postures below are exercises of *sense (4) authority* in
the vocabulary of §1.1.1 — the priestly office of the parent,
which every faithful view (1a, 1b, 2a, 2b) affirms as lifelong.
Release-and-await, intercession-without-restraint, and dedication
are three modes in which the priestly office is discharged, none of
them requiring sense (1) directive authority to be exercised at
the moment, and none of them foreclosed by the disagreements over
sense (1) that occupy Chapter 4. This is why the chapter is
placed at §5.0 as common ground rather than at §5.3 as a view
among views: it describes what all faithful parenthood of adult
children shares, not what any particular view uniquely commends.

### 5.0.1 Release-and-Await

The first posture is the most difficult and the most extensively
witnessed. The parent — divine or human — releases the adult child to
choices the parent would not have made, sometimes to certain ruin, and
maintains love and hope without exercising restraint. This is not
abandonment. It is release-in-continuing-love, with grief and watchful
awaiting for return.

**Samson and his parents (Judges 14; the passage is block-quoted at
§1.4.5).** Samson, an adult under the
Nazirite vow, demands a Philistine wife: "I have seen a woman in
Timnath of the daughters of the Philistines: now therefore get her
for me to wife" (Judg. 14:2). His parents object on covenantal
grounds: "Is there never a woman among the daughters of thy brethren,
or among all my people, that thou goest to take a wife of the
uncircumcised Philistines?" (v. 3). Samson insists: "Get her for me;
for she pleaseth me well." And his parents *comply* against their own
judgment. The narrator immediately supplies the theological key:

> But his father and his mother knew not that it was of the LORD,
> that he sought an occasion against the Philistines (Judg. 14:4).

The parents' compliance is not portrayed as their failure. They spoke
the right word. They were overruled by their adult son. God worked
through the son's ruinous autonomous choice to accomplish His own
purpose. Judges 14:4 is one of the Old Testament's clearest
providence-and-freedom hinges, and it sits directly on the
parent-and-adult-child question.

**The Prodigal Son (Luke 15:11-32).** Jesus's own parable is the
paradigmatic statement of this posture. The younger son demands his
inheritance — culturally tantamount to wishing his father dead. The
father grants it and *funds the departure* ("he divided unto them his
living," v. 12). He does not restrain the son's journey "into a far
country." He does not intervene during the ruin. He watches for return
without fetching. When the son "came to himself" (v. 17) and returned,
the father ran, embraced, and restored.

The parable is Jesus's revelation of God the Father's own posture
toward the autonomous adult child who chooses ruinous freedom. It is
told to the Pharisees — elder-brother analogs — as a rebuke of their
resentment that Jesus receives sinners. Two distinct claims about
the parable are commonly conflated and are worth separating:
(a) *descriptively,* the parable reveals the Father's posture toward
the ruinous adult child, and (b) *prescriptively,* human parents may
imitate that posture. Claim (a) is direct — the father in the
parable is a Christological/theological figure for God the Father.
Claim (b) is an inference by analogy — from what God does to what
human parents may do — and while the analogy is warranted (Christians
are elsewhere told to imitate the Father, Eph. 5:1), the parable
does not itself issue the imperative "parents, do likewise." Faithful
readers who take the parable prescriptively for human parenting are
making a defensible move; they should recognize they are making one.

*An objection worth naming, and the reading that answers it.* A
specific objection to any 2b-shaped use of Luke 15 as a paradigm
deserves an answer. The parable's rhetorical shape assumes that the
son's leaving is not honorable — the inheritance demand was
culturally tantamount to wishing the father dead, the departure is
into a "far country" and dissolute waste, and the return is only
what a starving man does. If the Prodigal is the paradigm, does the
paradigm quietly assume that adult-child departures generally *are*
or *will be* dishonorable? And how does that square with the many
scriptural cases (Elisha, Timothy, Rebekah, the Zebedee sons at
Christ's call) in which the departure is called-out, obedient, and
honorable in every register? The reading 2b's advocates press is that Jesus
chose *this* parable — a maximally dishonorable leaving — precisely
because its argumentative shape is *a fortiori:* if the Father's
love holds where the son has effectively wished him dead, it holds a
fortiori for every ordinary and honorable departure that a faithful
family will actually experience. Luke 15 is not the ordinary case
2b describes; it is the test case that certifies the posture at its
hardest, so that the honorable departures cited above can be held
under the same fundamental stance without residual anxiety. Two
observations reinforce this reading. The parable is told to
Pharisees, not to prodigals — its rhetorical target is elder-brother
resentment against grace given to sinners, which requires the son's
dishonor as the setup that makes the Father's love stunning enough
to expose it. And the parable comes third in a series of three (lost
sheep, lost coin, lost son) in which the shepherd and the woman
*seek* what is lost; the human father alone waits, precisely because
the human son alone has agency to leave and to return. The three
parables together depict a range of loss-and-recovery relations;
only the third requires the released party's own volition. The
parable is therefore doing exactly what 2b needs: showing that even
in the case where the parental posture is most tested — release into
what looks like sheer waste — the Father's love does not fail. The
ordinary case is easier, and the parable secures it.

**Absalom (2 Samuel 13-18).** The tragic version. David does not
effectively restrain his adult son's rebellion. Absalom mounts a coup,
sleeps publicly with David's concubines to seal the break, and dies
in battle. David's cry — "O my son Absalom, my son, my son Absalom!
would God I had died for thee, O Absalom, my son, my son!"
(2 Sam. 18:33) — is the Old Testament's most devastating
parent-loses-child lament. Same posture as the Prodigal Father, but
without the return. Release-in-love does not guarantee outcome.

**Esau (Genesis 26:34-35).** "And Esau was forty years old when he
took to wife Judith the daughter of Beeri the Hittite, and Bashemath
the daughter of Elon the Hittite: which were a grief of mind unto
Isaac and to Rebekah." Isaac and Rebekah did not restrain the
covenantally-improper marriages of their adult son, though they
grieved them. The blessing passes to Jacob; Esau is not restored to
covenantal centrality, though he does later reconcile with Jacob
personally.

**Rehoboam (1 Kings 12).** Solomon's careful preparation of the
kingdom cannot restrain his adult son's rash choice. Ten tribes are
lost. Even the wisest father in Israel's history cannot prevent an
adult son from a ruinous decision.

**Hosea 11 — the pattern stated as divine self-revelation.** "When
Israel was a child, then I loved him, and called my son out of Egypt.
As they called them, so they went from them: they sacrificed unto
Baalim, and burned incense to graven images.... How shall I give thee
up, Ephraim? how shall I deliver thee, Israel?... mine heart is
turned within me, my repentings are kindled together." (Hos. 11:1-2,
8). The prophetic corpus's most explicit statement of God-as-Father
releasing an adult-son people to Baal-worship and aching to restore.
This is the Old Testament theological ground on which Jesus builds
Luke 15.

**Peter's denial and restoration (Luke 22:31-32; John 21:15-19).**
The pattern is practiced by the Son toward His disciple. Jesus
foretells the denial — "Satan hath desired to have you, that he may
sift you as wheat" — and does not restrain it. He prays for the
restoration on the far side: "but I have prayed for thee, that thy
faith fail not: and when thou art converted, strengthen thy brethren"
(Luke 22:31-32). On the beach after the resurrection, the threefold
restoration mirrors the threefold denial (John 21:15-17). The Son
practices Prodigal-Father posture toward His disciple: release-in-
prayer, receive-in-love, restore-in-commission.

#### The Featured Contrast — Samson's Parents and Eli

The Release posture is often mistaken for its failure mode:
*permissive parenting through weakness.* The clearest way to see the
difference is to place Samson's parents alongside Eli.

**Samson's parents (Judg. 14, as told above).** They *speak the
right word.* They
raise their covenantal objection clearly and to their son's face:
"Is there never a woman among the daughters of thy brethren, or among
all my people, that thou goest to take a wife of the uncircumcised
Philistines?" They are then overruled by their adult son and comply
with his choice. The narrator immediately identifies the providential
frame — "it was of the LORD" (Judg. 14:4). Their compliance is
release, not abdication.

**Eli (1 Sam. 2-4).** Eli *knows* his adult sons Hophni and Phinehas
are wicked. He hears the reports: "Why do ye such things? for I hear
of your evil dealings by all this people. Nay, my sons; for it is no
good report that I hear" (1 Sam. 2:23-24). But his rebuke is weak,
and, critically, Eli fails to exercise the priestly-household
jurisdiction that was still his when the sons served under him at
Shiloh. Samuel receives the word of judgment: "For I have told him
that I will judge his house for ever for the iniquity which he
knoweth; because his sons made themselves vile, and he restrained
them not" (1 Sam. 3:13). Hophni and Phinehas die; the ark is
captured; Eli falls backward and dies; his daughter-in-law names her
newborn Ichabod — "the glory is departed from Israel" (1 Sam. 4:21).

The two cases look superficially similar (parents whose sons make
ruinous choices) but are theologically opposite. The difference is
not compliance-vs-restraint; it is *timing, jurisdiction, and the
presence of grieving parental voice.*

- **Timing** — Samson's parents speak *before* the marriage. Eli
  tolerates ongoing wickedness his sons had been committing for
  years.
- **Jurisdiction** — Samson is an adult and his marriage decision is
  his own to make. Hophni and Phinehas are under Eli's priestly
  authority in the ongoing conduct of the tabernacle service.
- **Grieving parental voice** — Samson's parents raise a clear
  covenantal objection and lament the choice. Eli's rebuke is weak
  ("Why do ye such things?") and issued too late to be pastoral
  correction.

**Canonical echo — Adonijah (1 Kings 1:6).** The narrator's
editorial verdict on David's parenting of Adonijah is compact and
severe: "And his father had not displeased him at any time in
saying, Why hast thou done so?" The construction is not a report
of Adonijah's psychological experience but Scripture's own
diagnosis of paternal failure toward an *adult* son. David never
called Adonijah to account, and the omission stands as the
narrative background to Adonijah's fatal attempt on the throne.
The parallel to Eli is close — failure to confront wickedness in
an adult son — and it sharpens the middle-mode point: Scripture
commends the *confrontation of an adult child* by the parent
without collapsing that confrontation into directive authority.
What David owed Adonijah, and Eli owed Hophni and Phinehas, was
not a command he could enforce but a covenantal word he could
name; the failure was not that they could not enforce, but that
they never named.

The Samson-Eli contrast should discipline the Release-and-await
reading. It is not a warrant for silence, weakness, or permissiveness.
It is a warrant for *speaking the covenantal word and then honoring
the adult child's freedom* — and for exercising active jurisdiction
where jurisdiction is still yours.

#### Summary of Release-and-Await

Scripture's testimony is that mature parenthood toward an autonomous
adult child looks like this: speak the covenantal word plainly, then
release; grieve when the child chooses ruin; do not withdraw love;
watch for return; run when return comes. This is God's own posture
toward His people, and it is the posture Jesus taught in the parable
and practiced with Peter.

### 5.0.2 Intercession-Without-Restraint

The second posture appears throughout the Gospels. The parent — or
parental figure — cannot restrain, cannot heal, cannot reach the
adult (or adult-ish) child. What the parent *can* do is bring the
child to Christ.

**Jairus and his daughter (Mark 5:21-43; Luke 8:41-56).** Jairus's
twelve-year-old daughter is dying. He cannot heal her. He falls at
Jesus's feet: "My little daughter lieth at the point of death: I pray
thee, come and lay thy hands on her, that she may be healed; and she
shall live" (Mark 5:23). Word comes that she has died. Jesus says:
"Be not afraid, only believe." He goes to the house, takes her by the
hand, says "Talitha cumi," and she rises.

**The father of the demon-possessed boy (Mark 9:14-29).** The
disciples have failed to cast out the spirit. The father brings the
boy to Jesus. Jesus asks how long. "Of a child.... but if thou canst
do any thing, have compassion on us, and help us." Jesus replies: "If
thou canst believe, all things are possible to him that believeth."
The father's cry — "Lord, I believe; help thou mine unbelief"
(Mark 9:24) — is the paradigmatic prayer of the intercessory parent
who cannot restrain and cannot heal.

**The widow of Nain (Luke 7:11-17).** The son is already dead in the
funeral procession. The widow has not brought him; she has *carried
him toward the grave.* Jesus intervenes uninvited. "And he came and
touched the bier: and they that bare him stood still. And he said,
Young man, I say unto thee, Arise." Christ acts where the parent no
longer can. This is intercession-in-extremis where the intercession
is Christ's own.

**The Syrophoenician woman (Mark 7:24-30; Matt. 15:21-28).** A
Gentile mother whose daughter is possessed. She cannot reach the
daughter's affliction. She reaches Christ instead, and persists
through apparent refusal: "Yes, Lord: yet the dogs under the table
eat of the children's crumbs" (Mark 7:28). Jesus commends her faith
and heals the daughter at a distance.

**The parent whose adult child has walked away.** The Gospel cases
above are illness and demonization; the church's classic case is
the child's own departure from the faith. Its exemplar is Monica
of Thagaste, whose years of prayer for Augustine the paper treats
at §5.4 and in the Conclusion — intercession-without-restraint
sustained across decades, joined to the release of §5.0.1: no
chasing, no relenting in prayer, and the door kept open.

#### Summary of Intercession-Without-Restraint

The pattern is consistent. The parent's competence has run out. The
child is beyond the parent's reach — through illness, demonization,
distance, death, or the child's own hardened choice. The Christian
parental response Scripture models is *not* the resumption of
restraint; it is intercession. The parent brings the unreachable
child to Christ. Faith persists through delay and apparent refusal.

Every view presented in §5.3 must answer: when your view of parental
authority runs out — because the child is adult, or beyond reach, or
in ruin — what do you do? Scripture's answer is not another mode of
control. It is intercession.

### 5.0.3 Dedication

The third posture is the reverse of release-to-the-world. Instead of
the adult child leaving the parent's covering for autonomous choice
(as in the Prodigal), the parent voluntarily releases the child *to
God* for vocation, calling, or self-offering. In its purest form, the
parent's release is offered before the child ever independently
chooses.

**Hannah dedicating Samuel (1 Sam. 1-2).** Hannah vows before Samuel
is born: "then I will give him unto the LORD all the days of his
life, and there shall no razor come upon his head" (1 Sam. 1:11).
When Samuel is weaned, she takes him to Shiloh and gives him to Eli:
"For this child I prayed; and the LORD hath given me my petition
which I asked of him: therefore also I have lent him to the LORD; as
long as he liveth he shall be lent to the LORD" (1 Sam. 1:27-28).
Her song of dedication (1 Sam. 2:1-10) becomes a template for
Christian dedication and is directly echoed in the Magnificat.

*Hannah as initiator of vocational household transfer.* Hannah's
case is not only the paradigm of parental dedication in the abstract;
it is the paradigm of the parent as *initiator* of the vocational
household transfer treated in §1.4.6. Samuel does not choose Shiloh;
Hannah chooses it for him, before he can. She brings him to the
house of the LORD at Shiloh — where Eli's household, the very
household whose sons are corrupt (1 Sam. 2:12-17), keeps the
sanctuary; Hannah gives Samuel into the priestly office at the very
time its corruption stands on the narrative record. She transfers him
formally with sacrifice ("with three bullocks, and
one ephah of flour, and a bottle of wine," 1:24). Elisha's case
(§1.4.6) is child-initiated: Elijah calls, Elisha follows, the
parents are informed post-decision. Hannah's case is parent-
initiated: the parent identifies the call before the child can, and
enacts the transfer while the child is still young enough that
dedication precedes independent consent. Both are honored in
Scripture; the Hannah shape is the one that reaches most directly
into Christian traditions of infant baptism, child dedication, and
early formal consecration. It also names, for the present question,
that a parent's Dedication posture is not passive — Hannah does not
merely *release* Samuel from the household; she *transfers* him
into the house of the LORD, enacting the vow with travel,
sacrifice, and formal presentation. But the text records no
assessment of the receiving authority: the vow is made to the LORD
(1:11), and her own words name the recipient — "I have lent him to
the LORD" (1:28). Eli receives the child only as the keeper of the
LORD's house, and his household's corruption is already on the
narrative record (2:12-17), with its judgment to follow (2:27-36;
3:11-14). In Hannah's case the dedicating act is addressed to God;
the human hands that receive the child — even unworthy ones — are,
in the narrative's frame, entrusted to Him.

**Mary at the foot of the cross (John 19:26-27).** Mary has released
her adult Son to His mission — a mission that ends in the crucifixion
she is now witnessing. Jesus from the cross provides for her care
through John: "Woman, behold thy son!... Behold thy mother!" Mary's
release is not release-to-the-world (as in the Prodigal). It is
release-to-the-Father's-will. The Son's mission included His mother's
lifelong grief. She does not resist it; she stands at the foot of
the cross.

**Gethsemane — the Son's own submission (Matt. 26:39; Mark 14:36;
Luke 22:42).** The perfect parallel case, inverted. The Son requests
release from the cup: "Father, if thou be willing, remove this cup
from me: nevertheless not my will, but thine, be done." The Father
does not grant the release. The Son submits. This is the perfect
Son-toward-Father analog of the Dedication posture: the beloved child
does not go into a far country; the beloved child accepts the cup the
Father does not remove. It is the vocation of Christ Himself.

**Second Samuel 7:14 — the covenantal formula.** Speaking to David
about Solomon: "I will be his father, and he shall be my son. If he
commit iniquity, I will chasten him with the rod of men, and with the
stripes of the children of men. But my mercy shall not depart away
from him." This is chastisement without withdrawal of mercy — the
covenantal principle underneath Dedication. The dedicated child is
disciplined but not disowned.

#### The Wider Christian Tradition of Dedication

Christian tradition has recognized Dedication as a distinct parental
and personal vocation from the earliest centuries. The desert
tradition (Anthony and after), the Third Orders of the mendicant
movements (Franciscan, Dominican, Carmelite tertiaries), the Beguines
of the medieval Low Countries, Ignatian dedication, the Orthodox
practice of monastic tonsure, Anglican religious communities, and the
modern Catholic Order of Consecrated Virgins all give institutional
form to Dedication.

For present purposes, three things about the tradition matter:

- Dedication has always been recognized as *distinct from marriage
  and distinct from ordinary single life,* not a lesser or higher
  version of either.
- Dedication may be initiated by the child (most commonly) or offered
  by the parents (as with Hannah), and the church has practiced both.
- Dedication in every tradition preserves *continuing kinship* — the
  dedicated child does not cease to be son, daughter, brother,
  sister. The Order-of-Virgins liturgy, monastic clothing, and Third
  Order formation all continue to name and honor the natural family.

The relevance for this paper: whatever view a reader adopts about
household authority in the ordinary case, Dedication remains a live
Christian option that has been practiced continuously since the early
church, and it changes the parent-and-adult-child question when the
adult child is being called to it.

### 5.0.4 How the Three Postures Relate

The three biblical postures are not competing views. They are
different modes of the same underlying reality: *the parent's love
outlasts the parent's control.* Mature Christian parenthood practices
all three at different times toward the same child.

- **Release-and-await** is the posture when the adult child chooses.
  The parent has spoken the covenantal word; the child has decided;
  the parent releases without withdrawing love.
- **Intercession-without-restraint** is the posture when the adult
  child is beyond the parent's reach. The parent's competence is
  exhausted; the parent brings the child to Christ.
- **Dedication** is the posture when the adult child is called to
  a vocation the parent must not obstruct. The parent releases the
  child *to God's use,* not to the world's choices.

The views presented in §5.3 (Views 1a, 1b, 2a, 2b, and the orthogonal
Arrangement Question) differ over the *structure* of household authority and,
in the Arrangement Question's case, over the mediation of the
marriage covenant itself. They do not, if faithful, differ over
these three postures. Any view that cannot practice Release will
collapse into control that Scripture forbids. Any view that cannot
practice Intercession will run out of faith at the point where the
parent's competence runs out. Any view that cannot practice
Dedication will foreclose vocations that Scripture and Christian
tradition uniformly honor.

The reader is invited to hold the three postures in view as the four
SCAR components are summarized and the views presented.

### 5.0.5 A Practitioner's Operational Frame — Cop → Coach → Consultant

One of the useful practitioner-level frames the paper's readers may
already know, and which maps closely onto the four-senses vocabulary
of §1.1.1, is Dwight Cover's Cop → Coach → Consultant developmental
chart (Christian Heritage Conference 2025 talk, "Dads, Prepare and
Launch Sons as Men of God"; chart PDF in the author's possession,
shared by Cover; Cover credits Gregg Harris as an influence and
extends the material). Cover diagrams the parent's
role across the child's development on two crossing axes:
*parental discipline* (100% at infancy, declining to 0% at adult),
and *the child's self-discipline* (0% at infancy, rising to 100%
at adult). The young-adult zone in which the two curves cross is
labeled "Battle of the Wills" — the turbulence Cover treats as a
predictable feature of the release, not a failure of formation.
The parent's role across the developmental arc moves through three
named stages:

- **COP** — the control mode of the earliest years. Parental
  discipline is at maximum; the child's self-discipline is under
  formation. Directive authority carries the whole weight.
- **COACH** — the influence mode of the transition years. Parental
  discipline is declining, self-discipline is rising, and the
  parent's contribution has shifted from *directing what happens*
  to *shaping how the young adult decides.*
- **CONSULTANT** — the counsel mode of the adult years. Parental
  discipline is at or near zero as the parent-facing intervention.
  The relationship is characterized as "shoulder-to-shoulder,
  man-to-man respect" — the adult child as, in Cover's phrase,
  "fellow-heir of the grace of life."

This developmental picture maps onto the paper's four-senses
vocabulary as follows:

| Cover's stage | Paper's senses in operation (§1.1.1) |
|---|---|
| **COP** (control) | Sense (1) directive authority in the formation years, with senses (2) formative office and (3) counsel operating implicitly through it. |
| **COACH** (influence) | Sense (2) formative office and sense (3) counsel come to the front; sense (1) directive authority is winding down through the release seam; sense (4) intercession is intensifying. |
| **CONSULTANT** (counsel) | Sense (3) counsel and sense (4) intercession as the modes in which the parent-child relation continues; sense (2) formative office continues through modeling; sense (1) directive authority is released. |

Two things are worth noting. First, this is a
*practitioner-articulated* picture — a father of eight (six sons,
two daughters), articulating what he tried to do across the actual
developmental arc — that arrives at the same distinction the paper
draws lexically at §1.1.1. That such a picture is available to
practitioners in the field who take household piety seriously is
evidence that the four-senses distinction is not an academic
imposition on the material; it is the shape the material actually
has when a thoughtful practitioner tries to describe how the
parent's role changes. Second, the picture explicitly *targets*
release. Cover's stated goal at adulthood is "shoulder-to-
shoulder, man-to-man respect" and the son as "fellow-heir of the
grace of life" — a launched-well outcome, not a
continuously-covered outcome. The chart is a working practitioner's
statement of the status-bounded reading toward which the paper's
own lexical lean runs (§1.1.1, on the balance of §1.2.1's
considerations): sense (1) directive authority bounded to the
formation years, with what continues after that carried by senses
(2), (3), and (4).

The paper offers Cop → Coach → Consultant to readers as one
practitioner-tested picture of how the four-senses transition
looks in a household that is trying to run it well; it is not the
only such picture, and other faithful households will hold it
differently. It is not offered as a doctrine but as a working
frame — the sort of thing a parent might helpfully post above a
desk in the season when the "Battle of the Wills" is live. A
fairness note: Cover articulated his chart as practical fatherly
counsel, not as a systematic argument in the doctrinal contest
this chapter conducts. The paper's use of the chart as
corroborating evidence for the §1.1.1 four-senses reading is the
paper's use, not Cover's stated aim, and the sorting-note at §4.5
records his own daily-life register (1a-with-launched-well) so
that his voice is not enlisted for the doctrinal contest without
that sorting being visible.

## 5.1 Where All Four Components Agree

Five convergences are clear:

1. **Honor is lifelong.** Scripture (Exod. 20:12; Eph. 6:2-3),
   Christocentrism (John 19:26-27; Mark 7:9-13), Antiquity (universal
   patristic teaching), and Reality (every faithful arrangement)
   agree without exception.

2. **Marriage is the paradigmatic leaving.** Scripture (Gen. 2:24),
   Christocentrism (Matt. 19:5), Antiquity (uncontested), and Reality
   (every arrangement) agree.

3. **Parents provide for children; adult children provide for aging
   parents.** Scripture (2 Cor. 12:14; 1 Tim. 5:4, 8), Christocentrism
   (John 19:26-27), Antiquity (repeated exhortations), and Reality
   (every arrangement) agree.

4. **Christ is the first allegiance.** Scripture (Matt. 10:37;
   Luke 14:26), Christocentrism (the entire pattern), Antiquity
   (consecrated singleness for the kingdom), Reality (every
   arrangement disclaims itself if it displaces Christ).

5. **Unmarried adulthood is an honorable state.** Scripture
   (1 Cor. 7:7-8, 32-34), Christocentrism (Jesus's own life),
   Antiquity (Justin, Athenagoras, Cyprian, Methodius), Reality (every
   arrangement acknowledges it, though they order it differently).

## 5.2 Where the Components Create Tension

Four tensions remain:

**Tension A — Obey or Honor?** Scripture uses ὑπακούω (obey) for
children and τίμα (honor) for the lifelong duty; but Scripture does
not fix the age of transition. Christocentrism suggests the transition
is real (Jesus at twelve is subject; Jesus at thirty is itinerant).
Antiquity does not codify a transition. Reality varies widely — from
age 18 launch to lifelong household residence. The tension is real
and Scripture leaves it open.

**Tension B — Sons and Daughters, Same or Different?** Scripture treats
them the same in general commands (Eph. 6:1-4; Col. 3:20-21) and
different in some particulars (Num. 30; 1 Cor. 7:36-38). Christocentrism
is largely silent on the distinction. Antiquity honors both consecrated
sons and consecrated daughters, with some asymmetry in ecclesial roles.
Reality shows a full spectrum: strict asymmetry in some faithful
households, full symmetry in others. Scripture does not compel a single
answer.

**Tension C — Household Authority After Adulthood?** Scripture's direct
texts on continuing paternal authority over unmarried adults are few
and interpretively contested (Num. 30, 1 Cor. 7:36-38). Christocentrism
sets a boundary (no tradition may nullify honor; no earthly bond may
rival Christ) but does not dictate a mechanism. Antiquity practices
variability. Reality shows four viable arrangements in the daily-life
dimension. This is the tension most productive of the modern debates
about *daily household life* and least resolvable from Scripture
alone.

**Tension D — Where Does Parental Authority Most Naturally Sit?**
Scripture places parental authority heavily at *the marriage moment*
— Genesis 24, 28, Exodus 22:16-17, Deuteronomy 22:16, Judges 14,
Ruth 3-4, 1 Corinthians 7:36-38. Scripture also assumes household
piety across the young-adult years (Prov. 1-7; Titus 2). Christocentrism
is comparatively silent on the marriage-arrangement question because
Jesus was unmarried. Antiquity assumes parental and pastoral
involvement in marriages (Ignatius, *To Polycarp* 5:2) while showing
variable household arrangements. Reality shows some cultures
concentrating parental office at the marriage moment (Orthodox
Jewish, Indian Christian, historic Christian courtship) and others
diffusing it (mainstream evangelical). Scripture does not compel a
single answer, but the *marriage-arrangement dimension* is
textually witnessed more extensively than the daily-life-authority
dimension.

## 5.3 The Views, Presented Neutrally

The tensions do not dissolve; they yield distinct, defensible views.
Each view is held by believers who love Scripture, honor Christ, and
seek the good of parents and children.

This section presents the arrangements at their steel-manned best,
together with the orthogonal *Arrangement Question* that any of
them may answer in more than one way.
All four views are presented here in short form; **View 1b's
full presentation lives at §4.1b** — its exegetical case (Haustafel
as ordering continuing parent-child relation), its historical
positioning (as its advocates present it, continuous with Puritan
household religion, Reformed household covenant, Catholic paternal
role, and Anabaptist family-integrated practice — a continuity
Appendix B.1 examines), its representative works, and its
contemporary carriers are all concentrated there so the exegetical,
historical, and Catholic/Reformed threads can be traced together in
one place. §5.3 does not repeat that full presentation here and
instead directs readers to §4.1b for it. §5.5 names two failure modes — the Clutch
and the Abandonment — that any of these views can drift into when
its central good is treated as the god rather than as pointing to
God; naming a failure mode is not the same as naming the view it
can drift from.

### View 1a — Household Continuity (Prudential Covering)

**The view.** Remaining in the parental household until marriage is
the ideal biblical pattern. Departure before marriage is a principled
exception, undertaken with parental blessing rather than as a
cultural rite of passage. For daughters especially, the household
provides an unbroken protective covering that passes to the husband
at marriage.

**The argument from Genesis 2:24.** The paradigmatic leaving is
leaving *into* cleaving — leaving one covenant household for the
formation of a new one. Scripture nowhere prescribes a distinct
leaving apart from marriage.

**The argument from the honor commandment and Ephesians 6:1-4.**
The household piety these texts envision does not evaporate at
eighteen: honor, teachability, and the ordered home continue as
pattern for the resident adult — though 1a reads the
obedience-imperative itself as tracking the household-dependent
stage (§1.2.1); the no-upper-age-limit reading of the imperative
is §4.1b's claim.

**The argument from Titus 2 and Proverbs 31.** The formation of
women as "keepers at home" and as substantive contributors to the
household economy assumes a settled household in which unmarried
daughters have meaningful and formative work.

**The argument from Numbers 30.** The father's veto of an unmarried
daughter's vow witnesses the protective household pattern; the
abiding-category reading of "in her father's house in her youth"
is 1b's stronger claim (§1.9, §4.1b), on which 1a's counsel does
not depend.

**The argument from 1 Corinthians 7:36-38.** On the father-of-the-
virgin reading, Paul assumes and endorses paternal decision-making
about a daughter's marriage.

**The argument from John 19:26-27.** Christ arranges Mary's ongoing
care through the beloved disciple — protective continuity as the
Lord's own practice at the cross. (The systematic
always-under-some-head paradigm built on this text is §4.1b's
argument, not 1a's.)

**The argument from the biblical narrative.** The multi-generational
household — Abraham and Isaac, Jesse and David, Boaz and Ruth in
Naomi's line — is the presumed setting of biblical ethics.

**The view, at its best.** Preserves purity, cultivates family
ministry, resists the atomization of modern life, honors Genesis 2:24
as the paradigmatic transition, and — as articulated by more moderate
proponents such as Scott Brown, Jason Dohm, and Steve Walker — treats
adult children as adults, counseling rather than commanding, and
refusing ultimatums from either side.

**Representative published works.** The Puritan household-worship
warrant (Matthew Henry, Cotton Mather, William Gouge, Richard
Baxter) is examined against its primary sources at §B.1.6-B.1.7.
The contemporary family-integrated case is developed in Voddie
Baucham, *Family Driven Faith* (2007), *What He Must Be If He
Wants to Marry My Daughter* (2009), and *Family Shepherds:
Calling and Equipping Men to Lead Their Homes* (Crossway, 2011); Doug Wilson, *Reforming
Marriage* (1995) and *Standing on the Promises* (1997); and the
teaching of the National Center for Family-Integrated Churches
(NCFIC) under Scott Brown and Jason Dohm. For the harder
daughters-at-home case, sorted to View 1b, see §4.1b and §B.1.4.

### View 1b — Continuous Doctrinal Authority (Systematic Covering)

**The view.** The outward practice of View 1a — remaining in the
parental household until marriage, under a father's protective
covering — is here held not as prudential pattern but as a
*systematic requirement* of Scripture: every believer stands under
a defined continuing authority, and for the unmarried adult child
that authority is the parent. The daughter's covering passes to
the husband at marriage; the son's covering ordinarily transitions
into the eldership of a local church.

**The argument from the fifth commandment as permanent office.**
Honor of parents is nowhere age-bounded in Scripture. The
commandment does not become "honor your parents until you are
eighteen." Honor is read as including, for the unmarried, a
continuing submission to parental direction — not merely counsel.

**The argument from Numbers 30.** The father's veto of an
unmarried daughter's vow is read not merely as an ancient legal
provision but as a standing pattern: the unmarried daughter's
spiritual and moral commitments remain under her father's covering
until they pass to a husband. The vow-veto category continues.

**The argument from the Haustafel without age terminus.**
Ephesians 6:1-3 and Colossians 3:20 address "children" without
imposing an age limit at which the address expires. The New
Testament nowhere marks the transition-point that a launching
paradigm would require.

**The view, at its best.** Preserves an unbroken structure of
authority through the vulnerable pre-marriage years; refuses the
modern atomization of the adult child into a self-directing
individual before he or she is embedded in the next covenantal
household; takes the Haustafel and the honor commandment at their
uninterrupted word.

**Representative published works.** Watchman Nee, *Spiritual
Authority* (1972; continued in Witness Lee's Living Stream
Ministry as *Authority and Submission* — see §B.1.1); Bill
Gothard, *Institute in Basic Life Principles* seminar teaching
(1961–), including the *Life Notebook* "Friends" section on
*Chain of Counsel* and *Eight Qualities Essential for Marriage*
(§B.1.3); Doug Phillips (Vision Forum), *The Tenets of Biblical
Patriarchy* (2007). Scholarly-register exegetical work
supporting components of the position — including William
Einwechter on the daughters-component from Numbers 30 and 1 Cor.
7:36-38, and Douglas Wilson on courtship (*Her Hand in Marriage,*
Canon Press, 1997) — is treated at §B.1.8, which also sorts these
as partial-component carriers rather than as systematic advocates
of the whole doctrine and notes attendant biographical or public-
record caveats a reader is entitled to know about (see §B.1.4).
The systematic form of the position is treated in full at §4.1b;
on the contested question of continuity with older streams —
Puritan household religion, Reformed covenant, Catholic paternal
role, Anabaptist family-integrated practice — see Appendix §B.1.

### View 2a — Prudential Launching (Continuing Honor Without Continuing Directive Authority)

**The view.** Obedience is proper to minor children under the parental
roof; honor is lifelong and inviolable. The unmarried adult exists as
an adult under Christ, owing honor and consultation to parents but
not obedience as if still a child. Directive authority ends; relational
obligation and moral honor continue.

**The argument from Ephesians 6.** Paul deliberately distinguishes
ὑπακούω (v. 1) from τίμα (v. 2). Honor is quoted from the Decalogue
because it is universal; obedience is not because it is age-bounded.

**The argument from Christocentric silence.** Jesus does not
inaugurate a continuing legal category of paternal authority over
adult unmarried offspring. His own leaving for the Father's business,
without ceremony or explicit release, becomes a pattern.

**The argument from 1 Corinthians 7.** Paul addresses "the unmarried"
directly as agents — commending, exhorting, and instructing them
without routing his exhortations through their fathers.

**The view, at its best.** Preserves adult agency under Christ,
matches the New Testament pattern of direct address to adult
believers, and prevents authority-arrangements that outlive their
purpose — without releasing the adult child from the honor
commandment or from ongoing counsel-seeking.

**Representative published works.** The mainstream evangelical case
is developed across John MacArthur's expository commentaries on
Ephesians (*The MacArthur New Testament Commentary: Ephesians,*
1986) and 1 Corinthians (1984), and in his pastoral counseling
materials; James Dobson, *Bringing Up Boys* (2001), *Bringing Up
Girls* (2010), and *Parenting Isn't for Cowards* (1987); Wayne
Grudem, *Systematic Theology* (1994) on the household commandments;
and the general teaching of Focus on the Family through
denominational family-life curricula. J. Vernon McGee's *Thru the
Bible* commentary on Ephesians 6 takes the honor-continues,
obedience-ends reading as the plain-sense biblical position. The
adult-child-specific pastoral treatment is Ross Campbell with Gary
Chapman, *Parenting Your Adult Child: How You Can Help Them
Achieve Their Full Potential* (Northfield, 1999), whose statement
of the transition is direct: "We can no longer operate with the
attitude that 'Father — or mother — knows best.' Young adults are
not convinced. We must join them as fellow pilgrims rather than
speak down to them from a position of superiority" (p. 32) — and
whose account of the parent's remaining office is relational: "Your
most important job just now is to maintain and attempt to improve
your relationship with your child" (p. 28). The "fellow pilgrims"
framing converges, from the mainstream-evangelical side, with the
"shoulder-to-shoulder, man-to-man respect" language of the
household-continuity boundary-case practitioners at §B.1.8 — the
two views describing the same adult-to-adult terminus from
opposite starting points.

### View 2b — Release with Prayer

**The view.** At the point where directive authority has ended (as
in View 2a), the parent honors the adult child as one who now stands
directly before Christ. When the adult child chooses in ways the
parent would not have chosen — including ruinous ways — the parent
speaks the covenantal word (as Samson's parents did), releases the
adult child to their decision, grieves what deserves grief,
intercedes without ceasing (Mark 9:24), and stands watchful for
return. Love and prayer do not end because control has. This is a
distinct shape from 2a's launched-into-adulthood pattern: 2b is
release-with-continuing-priestly-office, articulated most
explicitly through the parable of the prodigal father.

**The arguments, in brief** (full presentation at §4.2b): Christ's
own parable (Luke 15:11-32 — the Father's release-wait-restore
posture, with the prescriptive-vs-descriptive question recorded at
§5.0.1); Samson's parents (Judg. 14 — "it was of the LORD," as
quoted at §4.2b); Hosea 11 (God's own Father-posture toward His
adult-son people, the ground on which Luke 15 builds); the priority
of Christ (Matt. 10:37; Luke 14:26 — the parent's continuing office
priestly, not directive); and Peter's restoration (Luke 22:31-32;
John 21). At its best the view preserves adult agency under Christ
and the parent's continuing intercessory office, and refuses the
drift into release-in-indifference that §5.5 names as the
*Abandonment* failure mode — faithful 2b is release *with* prayer,
not release *from* prayer.

**Representative published works.** See §4.2b — Ruth Bell Graham's
*Prodigals and Those Who Love Them* (1991) at their head, with
Keller, Piper, and Elliot; the candid limitation note (no
book-length systematic advocacy of 2b exists in the current
evangelical catalogue) is recorded there.

### Note on the "Household-Formed but Kingdom-Directed" Pattern

Earlier drafts of this study distinguished a View 3 to name
households in which the adult unmarried child remains materially
and relationally embedded in a household of formation — the
parental household or another's — while directly
answerable to Christ for vocational and spiritual choices — Elisha
under Elijah, Samuel under Eli, the Zebedee sons under Christ. On
further reflection the arrangement is not a separate view but a
theological accent within View 1a (household continuity described
in kingdom-directed rather than covering-forward terms), together
with the biblical pattern of **vocational household transfer**
treated in full at §1.4.6.

The Reality-leg witness of household-continuity communities —
Amish, conservative Mennonite, Hutterite, Beachy Amish, Orthodox
Jewish, East Asian Christian, and much of the Global South — is
folded into View 1a's Reality-leg documentation below, §5.5's
Reality-Leg Notes, and §B.3.5. The gospel-shaped household literature of Timothy Keller
(*Center Church,* 2012; *The Prodigal God,* 2008; *Counterfeit
Gods,* 2009) and the Anabaptist household literature of Dean Taylor
(*A Change of Allegiance,* 2009; *Marriage, Divorce & Remarriage*),
John Howard Yoder (*The Politics of Jesus,* 1972), and Menno Simons
are cited in Appendix A alongside the other View 1a and Vocational-
Transfer sources.

### The Arrangement Question — Parental Mediation of the Marriage Covenant

**The question, not a fifth arrangement** (as §4.5 opens): who
initiates and mediates the marriage covenant itself is a distinct,
orthogonal question that a household holding any of 1a, 1b, 2a, or
2b may answer by parental mediation, by pastoral / ecclesial
mediation, or by leaving the initiation to the couple with parental
blessing. The full steel-manned presentation — Dwight Cover's
threefold biblical pattern, the supra-cultural argument, the
bride-price grammar, the redemptive-history typology, the
ecclesial-mediation third mode, and the representative works —
lives at §4.5; what follows is the short form.

**The parental-mediation view, in brief.** Parental authority is
concentrated at the marriage-covenant seam — initiating, mediating,
or blessing — while the daily-life question (residence, work,
travel) is left comparatively open (§4.5, Description).

**The argument from the biblical narrative.** Scripture's most
extensive treatment of parents and adult unmarried children is not
about daily household management but about marriage arrangement:
Genesis 24 (Abraham for adult Isaac at forty), Genesis 28:1-2 (Isaac
for Jacob), Exodus 22:16-17 (father's veto), Deuteronomy 22:16
(father's legal voice), Judges 14 (Samson's parents), Ruth 3-4
(Naomi guiding and Boaz negotiating through the elders), and
1 Corinthians 7:36-38 (Paul's virgin passage on the father-of-the-
virgin reading).

**The argument from covenant.** Marriage is a covenant, and every
biblical covenant is publicly witnessed and mediated. Parental
participation is one of the primary mediations Scripture assumes.

**The argument from Antiquity.** Ignatius (*To Polycarp* 5:2)
requires marriages formed "with the approval of the bishop ... not
after their own lust" (as quoted at §3.4). Tertullian critiques secret marriages. The
pre-Nicene church treats marriage as ecclesial and familial, not
private.

**The argument from realism.** Young adults are often ill-positioned
to evaluate a mate on their own limited experience. Marriage is a
lifelong covenant transaction, not a romantic self-discovery.

**The view, at its best.** Recovers the covenant character of
marriage. Reduces the burden on young people to serve as their own
matchmakers. Draws families and the church into the formation of new
covenants. Allows the daily household to breathe — the adult child
may live at home, in vocational service, or launched — while
concentrating parental office at the seam where the biblical text
most consistently places it.

## 5.4 Practical Guidelines Applicable to All Faithful Views

Regardless of which view a family holds, these apply — each grounded
in Scripture, in the pre-Nicene witness, or in the pattern common
across the faithful Realities of Chapter 4:

**For parents:**

- Provide for the child through minority and into adulthood as
  required; teach the fear of the LORD; pray (Eph. 6:4; Prov. 22:6;
  2 Cor. 12:14).
- Do not provoke to wrath (Eph. 6:4; Col. 3:21).
- Command is proper to the minor, counsel to the adult; the shift is
  presupposed by Genesis 2:24 (§1.3) and by the pre-Nicene expectation
  of adult household formation (§3.1.2, §3.10). View 1b reads
  the shift as delayed until marriage; Views 2a and 2b as accompanying
  the transition from minority; View 1a holds the command-to-counsel
  shift as proper to adulthood while its commended pattern keeps the
  household's covering and consultation in place until marriage.
- Do not confuse the child's honor with the child's agreement.
  Honor is the child's duty (Exod. 20:12; Eph. 6:2), and the
  parent's task is to receive honor as the child renders it, not
  to withhold relationship until the child agrees. Where parent
  and adult child disagree, honor persists through the disagreement
  and is not thereby forfeit; a parent who treats continued
  relationship as revocable on the child's agreement has confused
  honor with compliance.
- Prepare the child for the paradigmatic leaving of Genesis 2:24,
  whether into marriage or into a consecrated calling honored across
  the pre-Nicene church (§3.4-3.9; 1 Cor. 7:32-35).
- Lay up for the child, not the child for the parent, in the years of
  provision (2 Cor. 12:14); the direction reverses when the years turn
  (1 Tim. 5:4, 8, 16; Matt. 15:4-6).

**For unmarried adult children:**

- Honor is not optional; it is not qualified by the parent's
  honorableness, nor retired at adulthood (Exod. 20:12; Mark 7:9-13).
  Honor does not require agreement (§1.1).
- Give priority to Christ, and use that priority as fuel for love of
  parents, not as a pretext to neglect them (Matt. 10:37;
  Mark 7:9-13).
- If a parent's counsel is unwise, dissent within honor; if a parent
  commands sin, obey God rather than men (Acts 5:29).
- Seek counsel (Prov. 11:14, 15:22), including from parents where
  they are living and willing (Gen. 24; Judg. 14:3).
- Prepare for the paradigmatic leaving of Genesis 2:24, in either
  marriage or a settled consecrated calling (§3.4-3.9).
- When the years turn, provide for parents as Scripture requires
  (1 Tim. 5:4, 8, 16; Matt. 15:4-6; John 19:26-27).

**For churches:**

- Care pastorally for unmarried adults, married adults, and elderly
  parents as members of the household of faith (1 Tim. 5:1-16; §1.6).
- Bring the marriages of your young under pastoral counsel and
  blessing — the practice Ignatius describes (*To Polycarp* 5:2;
  §3.4); how far counsel extends toward mediation is part of the
  Arrangement Question (§4.5), which faithful churches answer
  differently.
- Do not require of families a household template that Scripture does
  not require, and do not permit a template that Scripture forbids
  (§1.8; §3.11).

**For parents whose adult child has walked away.** This is its own
category and needs its own counsel. The son or daughter has left the
faith, left the church, taken a partner outside covenant, or set
their face against the household's confession. What does Scripture
give you?

- The parable of Luke 15:11-32 is Christ's own answer, and it is
  precisely calibrated to this situation. The father *lets the son
  go* with his portion; he does not follow, does not badger, does
  not compel. He also does not stop watching the horizon. When the
  son is "yet a great way off" the father runs. The father's task
  is not to *fetch* the son. It is to *release, watch, pray, and be
  ready.* This is §5.0.1's release joined to §5.0.2's
  "intercession-without-restraint,"
  applied to the exact case: unrelenting prayer, no relenting on
  the household's confession, no cutting off, and no chasing.
- The Old Testament gives you a companion figure: Samuel, who mourned
  for Saul until the LORD said, "How long wilt thou mourn for Saul,
  seeing I have rejected him …?" (1 Sam. 16:1). Grief is right; but
  grief can become its own idol. Samuel is sent to Bethlehem to
  anoint the next king while Saul is still on the throne. The
  parent whose son has walked away is not thereby retired from
  every other calling. There is still a Bethlehem to go to.
- The specifically maternal witness is Monica of Thagaste, mother of Augustine, who prayed
  for Augustine's conversion through his Manichean years and beyond
  — seventeen years by the traditional reckoning — following him
  from Thagaste to Carthage to Rome to Milan, until Ambrose received
  him.
  Augustine credits her tears explicitly (*Confessions* 3.11-12,
  9.10-11). This is not a promise that every walked-away child will
  return; it is a witness that the calling to pray is real, is
  long, and is honored in ways not always seen in this life.
- The modern evangelical companion to Monica is Ruth Bell Graham,
  who waited through the prodigal years of two of her sons and
  wrote the waiting down. Her division of labor is exact: "We
  mothers must take care of the possible and trust God for the
  impossible... We are to love, affirm, encourage, teach, listen
  and care for. We cannot convict of sin, create hunger and
  thirst after God, or convert" — and her list under "My Part
  (the possible)" runs: "love — love expressed; to pray
  intelligently, logically, urgently, without ceasing, in faith;
  enjoy being a mother; provide a warm, happy home; minister to
  their physical and emotional needs as I am able" (*Prodigals
  and Those Who Love Them*, Focus on the Family, 1991, p. 101).
  The same book closes an anthologized prodigal story with a
  mother's letter that is the whole posture in one sentence: "if
  ever your heart should turn homeward, remember the door stands
  widely open, and there is nothing but joy that you will bring
  to us all" (p. 154).
- Practically: do not make honor conditional on the child's return
  (Exod. 20:12 is not qualified by the child's obedience either;
  the same principle runs both directions); do not let the walked-
  away child define the household's worship or witness (the
  household continues its confession); do not sever contact unless
  Scripture requires it (which it rarely does at the seam of
  parental relation, cf. Matt. 15:4-6); *do* pray, *do* keep the
  door open, *do* watch the horizon, *do* the next thing the LORD
  has given you today. Elisabeth Elliot's phrase — "do the next
  thing" — was originally counsel to those grieving. It applies
  here.
- A note on hope: the parable ends in restoration; the parent has
  no guarantee that this particular story will. The parent's
  faithfulness is not conditioned on the outcome. Hannah's
  Magnificat, Mary's Magnificat, and the widow of Nain's silence
  (Luke 7:11-15 — she did not even ask; Christ raised her son
  unbidden) together stake out the range: from confident
  intercession to silent grief that Christ meets. All three are
  honored postures.

### The Marriage-Arrangement Dimension

The marriage moment is treated directly by all four SCAR components;
guidelines here rest on their combined witness.

**For parents at the marriage moment:**

- Ensure the child's genuine consent, freely given (Gen. 24:57-58;
  Ketubot 39a-b, §3.1.2). Consent is presupposed in the Genesis
  pattern, required in rabbinic practice, and from the fifth century
  entrenched in canon law (§4.3.1).
- Do not let alliance, provision, or your own vision of the child's
  future *override* the child's consent or the child's calling before
  Christ (Matt. 10:37). Alliance and provision are proper concerns of
  the parent's office — Genesis 24 involves both — but coercion and
  the bypassing of consent are not.
- When the marriage is entered, withdraw; the child now leaves and
  cleaves (Gen. 2:24; Mark 10:7-8).

**For adult children at the marriage moment:**

- Welcome parental counsel and mediation even where the decision is
  yours (Prov. 11:14, 15:22; Judg. 14:1-3).
- Discern the covenant partner by the biblical marks: shared faith
  (2 Cor. 6:14; 1 Cor. 7:39), godly character (Prov. 31; Ruth), and
  calling (1 Cor. 7:17, 20).

**For churches at the marriage moment:**

- Bring the marriage under pastoral counsel and blessing, as
  Ignatius describes (*To Polycarp* 5:2; §3.4), in whatever form
  the household's answer to the Arrangement Question takes.
- Support families where parents are absent, unfit, or unbelieving,
  as the church did for widows and orphans (1 Tim. 5; Acts 6:1-6).

### Commonly Offered Counsel Not Directly Derived from SCAR

The following counsel is widely offered in modern pastoral writing on
these questions. Each is compatible with the SCAR-derived guidelines
above and often prudent, but each rests on cultural judgment or
contemporary prudential inference rather than direct scriptural,
patristic, or empirical warrant. Readers weighing these should weigh
them accordingly, and should not attribute them to the SCAR analysis
itself.

**On the parent–adult child relationship:**

- *"Transfer responsibility deliberately; do not create perpetual
  minors."* Restates the command-to-counsel shift in the modern
  rhetorical register. The underlying shift is SCAR-grounded (§1.2,
  §3.1.2, §3.10); "perpetual minors" is a 20th-century diagnostic
  framing.
- *"Never withdraw love on account of disagreement."* Restates "do
  not provoke to wrath" (Eph. 6:4) in the modern therapeutic register
  of "withdrawal of love." The underlying prohibition is SCAR-grounded;
  "withdrawal of love" as a diagnostic category is not itself biblical
  language.
- *"Make provision now for aging; do not require adult children to
  invent the provision under duress later."* Prudential extension of
  2 Cor. 12:14 and 1 Tim. 5:8. Compatible with those texts but not
  itself commanded there.
- *"If you receive material provision, receive it with gratitude and
  reasonable deference; if you provide your own, do so without
  arrogance."* General Christian ethic on receiving and giving. Not
  distinctively derived from SCAR on this particular topic.
- *"Do not turn a household template into a doctrinal shibboleth."*
  The paper's own synthesis judgment. Compatible with §1.8 and §3.11,
  but a modern rhetorical caution rather than a scriptural or
  patristic command.
- *"Restore the church's role in courtship and marriage counsel where
  it has been lost."* Prudential diagnosis paired with the Ignatius
  model. The Ignatius role itself is SCAR (§3.4); the "where it has
  been lost" diagnosis is modern.

**On the marriage moment:**

- *"Pray and examine before initiating any conversation about a
  match."* General Christian counsel; not specifically SCAR-derived
  on this topic.
- *"Do not use the marriage arrangement as an instrument of family
  alliance or financial arrangement."* A modern Western-egalitarian
  prohibition. Scripture and Antiquity accept alliance and financial
  dimensions of marriage as normal (Genesis 24 involves both; ketubot
  and dowry practice presuppose both). What SCAR actually forbids is
  the *overriding* of consent, as the SCAR-only guideline above
  states. This blanket prohibition should be held loosely and applied
  to abuses, not to the presence of alliance or provision as such.
- *"Discern what parents alone cannot: whether you can love this
  person for a lifetime."* Modern romantic-love framing. Biblical
  criteria for the covenant partner are shared faith (2 Cor. 6:14;
  1 Cor. 7:39), godly character (Prov. 31; Ruth), and calling
  (1 Cor. 7:17, 20), as the SCAR-only bullet above states. "Love this
  person for a lifetime" as a decision-criterion is a 20th-century
  framing.
- *"Provide teaching, counsel, and structured accountability during
  courtship and betrothal."* Prudential elaboration of the Ignatius
  model (*To Polycarp* 5:2); the specific mechanisms are modern.

## 5.5 Failure Modes Common to All Views

Each view has a central good — family, launched adulthood,
priestly-releasing love, parental mediation — and each view can
drift the same way: when that central good ceases to point to
Christ and becomes a god in its own right. The failure is not
merely *forgetting* Christ; it is *replacing* Him with the good
the view was built around. A failure mode is not the view. The
Prodigal parable diagnoses the failure modes without collapsing the
doctrines that can drift into them. One naming convention governs
throughout: the **Clutch** and the **Abandonment** are the
*characteristic* drifts of 1b and 2b respectively — the named modes
their own strongest advocates must guard against — but they are not
exclusive to those views; a 1a household can drift into the Clutch
and a 2a household into the Abandonment, which is why the paper
elsewhere speaks of households of any view "drifting toward" either
mode.

- **View 1a (Household Continuity, Prudential Covering) fails when
  the household becomes the treasure.** Covering hardens into
  control. A father's will substitutes for God's. Marriage is
  delayed indefinitely. Adult children are treated as perpetual
  minors. Household continuity becomes a mark of piety rather than
  a means to it.
- **View 1b (Continuous Doctrinal Authority) is prone to the
  Clutch.** In its failure mode the covering itself becomes the
  idol — safety-under-authority hardens into a pledge of *spiritual
  safety* (remain under the covering, therefore no harm) and the
  direct answerability to Christ, the searching of Scripture, and
  the Spirit's own leading that the New Testament *did not
  subordinate* to any human office are quietly folded into the
  covering along with the directive authority. Ordered-means-of-
  grace becomes an alibi for evasion on both sides: the covering is
  invoked to guarantee what only Christ can guarantee; the parent's
  ministering-under-God is imitated in the ministering only, not in
  the under-God. Advocates of 1b who name the Clutch honestly, and
  who insist that the covering is one *ordered means* rather than
  the source of safety, describe the discipline in a form that
  avoids this drift. Naming the failure mode is not naming the
  doctrine — the ordered household in Ephesians 5-6 *ministers
  under Christ*; it does not stand between the believer and Him.
- **View 2a (Prudential Launching) fails when independence becomes
  the treasure.** The "advisory" mode is asserted so strongly that
  honor thins into optional courtesy, counsel is treated as
  elective, household piety disintegrates without a positive
  replacement, and the launched adult drifts into whatever the
  ambient culture already wanted.
- **View 2b (Release with Prayer) is prone to the Abandonment.** In
  its failure mode the release itself becomes the idol — autonomy
  hardens into a pledge of *spiritual safety* (no coercion,
  therefore no harm) and the continuing love, prayer, honor, and
  door-open watchfulness that Luke 15's father *did not release*
  are quietly released along with the directive authority.
  Direct-answerability-to-Christ becomes an alibi for withdrawal
  on both sides: Christ's priority is invoked to justify what
  Christ has not authorized; the Father's own release-in-
  continuing-love is imitated in the release only, not in the
  continuing love. Advocates of 2b who name the Abandonment
  honestly, and who insist that release without continuing prayer,
  honor, and counsel is not the Prodigal father's shape, describe
  the discipline in a form that avoids this drift. Naming the
  failure mode is not naming the doctrine — the Prodigal father
  *released and kept praying*; he did not abandon.
- **The Twin Idolatries.** The **Clutch** (a drift-mode of 1b) and
  the **Abandonment** (a drift-mode of 2b) are inverted forms of
  the same refusal. In the vocabulary of §1.1.1, the Clutch is the
  *idolatry of sense (1)* — directive authority absolutized into a
  guarantor of spiritual safety, so that the covering itself
  becomes what saves. The Abandonment is the *amputation of senses
  (3) and (4)* — counsel and priestly intercession dropped along
  with the released directive authority, so that release means
  release from the whole parental office rather than only from its
  directive mode. The Clutch promises *"I am safe because I remain
  under my covering"*; the Abandonment promises *"I am safe because
  I have thrown off every covering."* Both refuse the Father — one
  by clutching, one by fleeing. Both terminate honor at the point
  control ends. The pastoral temptations of each look opposite; the
  underlying refusal is the same. Neither drift can produce the
  lifelong honor Scripture requires (Exod. 20:12; Matt. 15:4-6),
  and neither can imitate the Father's own posture in Luke 15. The
  Prodigal parable diagnoses both — and shows that faithful
  releasing and faithful covering share a common center in the
  Father's undying love.
- **The Arrangement Question fails when the arrangement itself
  becomes the treasure.** Parental mediation hardens into coercion.
  Family alliance or financial interest overrides genuine consent.
  The couple's own discernment is bypassed. Parents extend the
  arrangement office into ongoing management of the marriage after
  the wedding. The parental office, given by God to serve the
  covenant, becomes a claim of continuing ownership.

**Canonical illustration: Joash and Jehoiada (2 Chron. 24).** The
scriptural case that names the deepest form of the Clutch failure
mode *from the formed child's side* is Joash. Rescued as an infant
from Athaliah's murder of the royal seed by his aunt Jehosheba and
hidden in the temple by Jehoiada the priest, Joash is crowned at
seven and "did that which was right in the sight of the LORD all
the days of Jehoiada the priest" (2 Chron. 24:2). When Jehoiada
dies at 130, the princes of Judah approach; Joash listens to them,
forsakes the LORD, and has Jehoiada's own son Zechariah stoned to
death in the court of the LORD's house (2 Chron. 24:15-22). The
pattern is the exact inverse of the Rechabites of Jer. 35 (§4.1b):
where the Rechabites kept the ancestral command faithfully across
the generations, Joash's faith held only for the lifespan of the
priestly-household scaffold that supported it, and collapsed the
moment that scaffold was removed. The formation was strong; it was
never *interiorized*. The text stands as Scripture's own diagnosis
that formation-under-priestly-authority is not itself sufficient to
produce a Christ-following adult — the *goal* of formation is a
heart in which the covenant is inscribed (Deut. 6:6-9; Jer. 31:33;
2 Cor. 3:3), not compliance under a scaffold. Every view treated in
this paper has to reckon with the Joash pattern on its own terms:
1a and 1b as the failure mode their central good is most vulnerable
to, 2a and 2b as the reason mere release cannot itself secure the
interiorization the release presupposes.

**A second canonical illustration: Samuel's sons (1 Sam. 8:1-5).** A
companion case rounds out the Joash pattern from the parental side
rather than the formed-child's side. Samuel — the paradigmatic
priestly-prophetic figure of the transitional era, himself formed
under Eli (whose parental failure toward his own sons is treated at
§5.0.1) — appoints his own sons Joel and Abijah to be judges over
Israel. *"And his sons walked not in his ways, but turned aside after
lucre, and took bribes, and perverted judgment"* (1 Sam. 8:3). The
elders of Israel bring this failure to Samuel as the immediate
occasion for their request for a king (1 Sam. 8:4-5). The narrative
places the deepest priestly-prophetic formation of the era — Samuel's
own — into the same pattern Joash instances: a form of household
piety that does not, by the mere fact of its own faithfulness,
guarantee the interiorization of that piety in the next generation.
Scripture presents this without simple moral reduction of Samuel, and
without excusing Joel and Abijah — precisely as it presents Eli
without excusing Hophni and Phinehas. The datum every view has to
carry: even the most faithful household formation is neither the
cause nor the guarantee of the son's own heart before God. The
Rechabites are the counter-witness that shows what faithful
multigenerational formation *can* produce; Joash, Joel and Abijah,
Hophni and Phinehas, and Adonijah (§5.0.1) are the canonical witnesses
that show that it does not always.

The love-and-unity test — sharpened in §5.7 to include the questions
of discipleship, witness, church participation, and cost — catches
each failure mode early, if it is applied honestly.

### Reality-Leg Notes on Household-Continuity Communities

The Reality-leg data on household-continuity practice is
disproportionately shaped, in the North American conversation, by
the twentieth-century movements treated at §4.1b and by their
documented failures. That data is real and stays; it is not the
whole picture, and it does not describe the largest and longest-
running Christian communities that hold something like View 1a in
practice.

**Amish and conservative Mennonite (Old Order, Beachy Amish,
Hutterite).** Community documentation from Donald Kraybill,
*The Riddle of Amish Culture* (Johns Hopkins, 2001) and
*The Amish* (with Karen Johnson-Weiner and Steven Nolt, Johns
Hopkins, 2013); John Hostetler, *Amish Society* (Johns Hopkins,
1993); and the Young Center for Anabaptist and Pietist Studies
at Elizabethtown College tracks a household-continuity pattern
with unusually high retention (Kraybill et al. 2013 report rates
broadly in the 80-95% band, varying by affiliation; northern
Indiana at ~95%; see §B.3.5 for detail), stable multi-
generational households, apprenticeship-into-vocation, and
locally-mediated marriage formation. The pattern is neither
Gothard-style umbrella nor Nee-style delegated authority; it is
straightforwardly the older Anabaptist reading of Haustafel and
household-covenant piety worked into a community rule of life.
Failure modes exist (shunning practices — *Meidung* — bear the
main critical weight in the outside literature; the Amish
themselves defend the practice as an ecclesial discipline distinct
from parent-child cutoff), but the pattern's endurance and its
generational retention constitute Reality-leg data that any
serious treatment of View 1a has to reckon with.

**Global South Christianity.** The demographic center of world
Christianity has shifted decisively to Africa, Asia, and Latin
America. Philip Jenkins, *The Next Christendom: The Coming of
Global Christianity* (Oxford UP, 2002/2011), documents the size
and shape of the shift. Household patterns in most of these
contexts — West African Christian households, East Asian
Christian households, Latin American Catholic households — sit
closer to View 1a's multi-generational household continuity than
to the North American launched-at-18 pattern, without ever having
passed through the twentieth-century umbrella-doctrine controversies.
Anthropological and pastoral literature (Andrew Walls, *The
Missionary Movement in Christian History,* Orbis 1996; Lamin
Sanneh, *Whose Religion Is Christianity?,* Eerdmans 2003) treats
the shift and its household implications. The Reality-leg
observation is straightforward: the majority-world Christian
household today looks more like §4.1a than like §4.2a; comparable
outcome studies on Christian Smith's measures do not yet exist for
these households (§B.3.4).

**Bearing on the SCAR analysis.** The Reality leg on View 1a is
not exhausted by the twentieth-century North American
umbrella-doctrine testimony (Recovering Grace and West 2024,
treated at §4.1b Challenges); those describe
a specific North American subculture running the harder 1b form
of the teaching. The wider Reality-leg witness — Amish, Anabaptist,
Hutterite, Global South, Orthodox Jewish continuities — attests,
where measured, durable generational transmission (retention:
§B.3.5); the launched American samples show lower retention on
the instruments used there (§B.3.1-B.3.2), though populations,
instruments, and denominators differ (§B.3.4). That does not decide the view
question; other faithful views can point to their own Reality-leg
witnesses — for 2a, the resilient-disciple cohort of Kinnaman and
Matlock's study (§B.3.2), young adults who retained Christian
practice *through the launch*, and the vocational-transfer and
missionary record of §1.4.6 and §4.3.5, which is a launched
record; for 2b, the pastoral literature and Ruth Bell Graham's
first-person testimony gathered in §5.3's
Release-with-Prayer block and §5.4. It does mean that the case
against View 1a cannot rest on selective sampling of one troubled
subculture.

## 5.6 Pastoral Edge Cases and Exceptions

The four views and the Arrangement Question are formulated with
an implied ordinary case: two believing biological parents, a
household intact through the child's minority, an adult child
whose vocational, spiritual, and marital paths lie ahead. Real
households present many cases that do not fit that frame. These
are not failure modes — they are pastoral realities the ordinary-case
frame does not describe. The paper cannot address each in detail;
five are named here because readers regularly ask.

**Blended families.** Where an adult unmarried child now lives
under the household headship of a stepparent — or where the
biological parents live in separate households, either or both
remarried — the Haustafel's addressee-set and the fifth
commandment's honor-object multiply. Scripture is not silent on
step-relations (Ruth to Naomi is the paradigmatic case of loyalty
across a non-biological seam) but does not systematize them. The
practical shape: honor is owed to each biological parent
regardless of remarriage; the stepparent's household authority
during the child's minority tracks residence and household
membership rather than biology; adult-child obligations to
stepparents are pastoral and prudential rather than commanded in
the categorical mode of Exod. 20:12. Where a stepparent has
raised the child as one of the household's own, the honor
commandment applies in substance, though the biblical vocabulary
does not name that case directly.

**Widowed and single parents.** The Haustafel envisions two
parents; the biblical world often did not have them. Widows are a
recurrent category in Scripture (1 Tim. 5; Acts 6:1-6; Ruth) and
receive their own specific pastoral attention. The adult unmarried
child of a widowed parent bears an intensified form of the ordinary
provision-and-care obligation (1 Tim. 5:4, 8), and the church's
role thickens (1 Tim. 5:3-16 assumes the church acts where the
biological family cannot). Where a parent has raised the child
alone through no fault of their own, honor is undiminished; where
the single-parent situation reflects the parent's own covenantal
failure (a father who abandoned the household, a mother who left
the covenant), honor is still owed, on the fifth commandment's
own logic that it does not qualify honor by the parent's
honorableness, though the shape of expressed honor may be more
constrained than the ordinary case.

**The parent's continuing need.** The four views describe the seam
between minority and adulthood as if the household's ordinary
gravity ran one way — the child's dependency lessens, the parent's
oversight releases. In many households this ordinary gravity is
reversed. A widowed or single parent may need the unmarried adult
child's presence, provision, or care before the child's own marriage
has occurred. First Timothy 5:4 addresses precisely this case: "if
any widow have children or nephews, let them learn first to shew
piety at home, and to requite their parents: for that is good and
acceptable before God." The apostle sets this obligation *before*
the church's own duty of relief. Deuteronomy 27:16 and Ruth's care
for Naomi (Ruth 2:2, 18) supply the Old Testament pattern; Jesus's
own commission of John from the cross (John 19:26-27) elevates it
into a christological act. The bearing on the four views is this:
where a parent's genuine need runs before marriage, the adult
child's ordinary trajectory (whichever view describes it) is
modified by the honor commandment's provision-clause — not
suspended, but reshaped. Views 2a and 2b, in particular, must
resist the drift by which "launched" becomes "unavailable"; Views
1a and 1b must resist the parallel drift by which the parent's
need becomes a covering-justifying claim that would not otherwise
be made. In all four views, the parent's need reorders the
household's ordinary shape without changing the doctrine.

**Persistent disability or dependency in the adult child.** The
Haustafel and the fifth commandment assume an adult child who at
some point becomes practically capable of independent household
formation. Not every adult child does. A child with profound
intellectual or developmental disability, a child with chronic
mental illness that prevents independent living, a child whose
physical condition requires continuous care — these cases are what
the four views most conspicuously do not address. Scripture
supplies partial patterns rather than a doctrine: David's care for
Mephibosheth (2 Sam. 9), the presence in the covenant community of
those who "cannot recompense thee" (Luke 14:14), the church's
inclusion of "the feeble" (1 Cor. 12:22, ἀσθενέστερα,
*asthenestera*) as
indispensable. The practical implication is that the leaving-seam
that the ordinary case treats as biographical becomes, in these
cases, eschatological: the seam that never arrives in this life is
carried in the household of faith by the parents until they can no
longer carry it, at which point either siblings, the extended
covenant family, the local church, or specialized institutions of
Christian mercy must receive what the biological parents can no
longer bear. All four views recognize the obligation, but none of
them shapes its practical form; the church's diaconal office and
the covenant community's willingness to bear one another's burdens
(Gal. 6:2) supply what the views alone cannot. The parent whose
adult child is permanently dependent occupies a household shape the
Haustafel does not describe and to which the ordinary seam-language
does not apply; honor, provision, priestly intercession (sense (4)
of §1.1.1), and the church's own care converge on this case
without any of the four views being the frame in which the
household lives.

**Immigrant and culturally-displaced households.** Where an
immigrant Christian household holds an inherited household pattern
(often closer to §4.1a) inside a host culture whose default is
§4.2a, the adult child stands at a cross-pressure the ordinary
case does not describe. Some accommodations are prudential and
proper (learning the language of the host culture's pastoral
counsel, negotiating with a spouse from a different background);
some are not (adopting the host culture's default view because it
is the default). The SCAR framework itself is culturally located
— written in English, from North American church traditions,
mostly for households navigating English-speaking Christianity —
and immigrant readers may find that their inherited streams
(East Asian Christian household culture, African diaspora
household culture, Latin American Catholic household culture)
already carry answers that the American conversation is only
beginning to recover. The framework's non-negotiables (honor,
Christ-priority, no forbidding or compelling of marriage, adult
consent, singleness not less than marriage) hold across cultures;
the arrangement space in which the four views operate is where
faithful cultural inheritance can supply what the American
default cannot.

## 5.7 The Ultimate Test, Restated

Every arrangement — Views 1a, 1b, 2a, or 2b; whichever answer to
the Arrangement Question; sons or daughters; symmetric or
asymmetric — must pass two groups of questions.

*The family test* — the questions any warmly-affectionate household,
Christian or not, might set for itself:

1. Does it **promote love** in the household? (John 13:34-35)
2. Does it **preserve unity** between parent and adult child? (John
   17:20-23)
3. Does it **enable lifelong honor** of father and mother, with
   content? (Exod. 20:12; Mark 7:9-13; 1 Tim. 5:4)
4. Does it **keep the peace** of the household? (Rom. 12:18;
   1 Pet. 3:8-9)

*The discipleship test* — the questions only a Christian household
can honestly ask itself and answer:

5. Does it **form a disciple**, or merely a well-adjusted family
   member? (Matt. 28:19-20; Luke 14:26-33)
6. Does it produce **visible witness** — enough that outsiders ask
   what makes this household different? (Matt. 5:14-16; 1 Pet.
   3:15)
7. Is the **church of Jesus Christ genuinely part of the
   discernment**, not merely officiating at the wedding? (Acts 15;
   1 Cor. 5:1-13, 12:12-27; Ignatius, *To Polycarp* 5:2; §3.4)
8. Does it **leave Christ above every human bond, including this
   household's own — and is the household willing to bear the cost
   of doing so?** (Matt. 10:37; Luke 14:27-33; John 15:18-20;
   2 Tim. 3:12)

The Matt. 10:37 question sits *here* rather than in the family
test: "he that loveth father or mother more than me is not worthy
of me" is a discipleship claim, not a family-relations metric, and
its placement in the discipleship set is the point at which the two
tests diverge. A warmly-affectionate secular family can pass all
four family questions — that is the diagnostic thought-experiment
this section runs. Only a Christian household can pass all eight.
Where an arrangement satisfies both groups, it is faithful — even
where it differs sharply from another faithful family's arrangement.
Where it satisfies the first four and cannot answer the second four,
the household has become a good thing that has quietly displaced
the Best Thing.

### Worksheet

For household use in prayerful discernment. Take the specific
arrangement in view — an adult child's continuing residence, a
proposed launch, a suitor received, a call declined, a marriage
arranged or a marriage entered without arrangement — and answer
each question in a sentence or two, in writing, before the Lord.
Do not answer in the abstract; answer for *this case*.

*The family test.*

1. **Love.** In what specific ways does this arrangement promote
   love between parent and adult child? Where is love strained?
2. **Unity.** Does this arrangement preserve unity, or does it
   proceed over an unresolved tension? Name the tension.
3. **Honor with content.** Does this arrangement enable honor that
   includes provision, consultation, reverence, and remembrance —
   or does it enable a thinner honor?
4. **Peace of the household.** Is the household at peace over this
   arrangement, or is the peace pretended? Who in the household
   still carries the disquiet?

*The discipleship test.*

5. **Formation of disciples.** Does this arrangement form disciples
   of Jesus Christ, or does it form well-adjusted family members
   who happen to attend church?
6. **Visible witness.** Would an outsider observing this household
   ask what accounts for what they see? Or would the household look
   like the ambient culture with slightly better manners?
7. **Church discernment.** Is the local church actually a
   participant in this discernment — with real weight, not merely
   ceremonial presence — or is the church being asked to bless a
   decision already made?
8. **Christ above every bond.** If this arrangement had to be
   surrendered tomorrow for the sake of Christ, would the household
   be willing? Is there any human bond in this arrangement that
   would resist being subordinated to Christ?

*Reading the result.* If the household can answer all eight
honestly and finds no fault, proceed with confidence. If one or two
answers press, name the pressure; do not paper it over. If the
first four are fine and the second four cannot be answered, ask
whether the household is discipling itself out of Christian
distinctness. The worksheet is not a scoring device; it is a way
of making prayerful conversation concrete.

*One qualifier — the mixed-household case.* Where the household is
mixed in the sense of §1.4.10 (unbelieving parents, or a parent
whose command runs against Christ's), the family test may fail on
Questions 1-4 through no fault of the arrangement itself. Christ
Himself says households will divide over Him — "I came not to send
peace, but a sword ... a man's foes shall be they of his own
household" (Matt. 10:34-36, quoting Micah 7:6). Where the division
is caused by fidelity to Christ rather than by the arrangement, the
family-test failure indicts the *circumstance*, not the household;
and Paul's own qualifier "if it be possible, as much as lieth in
you" (Rom. 12:18, the very text of Q4) already names the limit.
The worksheet is not a device for extorting peace from the
disciple whom Christ has called to carry a cross the household
refuses.
