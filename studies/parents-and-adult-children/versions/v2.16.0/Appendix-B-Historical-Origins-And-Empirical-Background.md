# Appendix B — Historical Origins and Empirical Background

This appendix consolidates material that is *contextual* to the
positions catalogued in Chapter 4 but not directly load-bearing on the
SCAR analysis. §B.1 traces the modern origins of View 1b (the
Continuous Authority / "Umbrella" Doctrine). §B.2 traces the
modern origins of View 2 (Launched at Adulthood, in both its
prudential (2a) and release-with-prayer (2b) forms). §B.3 surveys the empirical / sociological
literature relevant to the "Reality" leg of the quadrilateral,
particularly with respect to Views 2a and 2b, with methodological
caveats.

## B.1 Modern Origins of View 1b (Continuous Authority / "Umbrella" Doctrine)

**A note before the history begins.** This section traces the
twentieth-century origins of *View 1b specifically* — the doctrine
that adult unmarried believers require continuous covering by a
defined human authority as a condition of spiritual protection or
God's blessing. It does *not* trace the origins of View 1a
(Household Continuity / Prudential Covering), which has a genuine
Reformed and Puritan household-worship warrant treated in §B.1.7
below. Views 1a and 1b are frequently conflated, both by their
critics and by some of their proponents. They should be
distinguished. View 1a's classical warrant is the Puritan doctrine
of family religion — the household as a "little church" — grounded
in Deuteronomy 6:6-7, Psalm 78:5-8, Ephesians 6:4, and the pastoral
literature of the Reformed household tradition. View 1b's warrants
are the four modern strands surveyed below and are, whatever their
intended continuity with older piety, distinct twentieth-century
developments.

A further methodological word is owed. This section's history is
*scandal-shaped* — Nee, the Shepherding Movement, Gothard, Vision
Forum, Sproul Jr. — while §B.2 tells View 2's social history in a
neutral register and treats outcomes only in §B.3. That asymmetry is
not a rhetorical thumb on the scale; it is a fact about the material.
The twentieth-century movements that formalized the umbrella /
covering claim into a systematic requirement are the doctrine's
carriers, and their record is largely what a reader will encounter
if she searches. §B.1.6 explicitly disclaims the genetic fallacy —
scandal in a movement does not by itself falsify a doctrine held by
persons outside that movement. §B.1.7 then reads the older Puritan,
Reformed, and Catholic primary sources on their own terms and finds
that they in fact bound directive obedience at adulthood, which
places 1b's claim of continuity with those older streams in
contested territory. Readers who hold 1b on independent exegetical
grounds should read the history as history and the exegesis on its
merits.

The doctrine as such — with the umbrella / covering imagery, and the
systematic claim that being outside a defined human authority exposes
the believer to spiritual attack — is a twentieth-century development.
It arises in four related but distinct strands, developed largely
independently at first and later cross-pollinating.

### B.1.1 Watchman Nee — *Spiritual Authority* (1948 lectures / 1972 English)

Watchman Nee (*Ní Tuòshēng*, 1903-1972) delivered the lectures
collected as *Spiritual Authority* (originally *Quánbǐng yǔ Shùnfú*,
"Authority and Submission") to Local Church workers on Kuling Mountain
in 1948; the English translation was published in 1972 by Christian
Fellowship Publishers (New York), which renders Nee's *dàibiǎo
quánbǐng* as "delegated authority" and "representative authority"
(the rendering "deputy authority" belongs to the Living Stream
Ministry literature). The book articulates three related
claims that would become foundational for later covering theology,
each quoted here from the 1972 translation. (a)
Authority is God's fundamental way of ordering the universe:
"All things are created through God's authority and all physical
laws of the universe are maintained by His authority ...
Authority is a tremendous thing in the universe — nothing
overshadows it" (p. 10). (b) God
delegates authority through representative authorities — parents,
husbands, elders, governments — through whom obedience to God is
mediated: "To rebel against God's representative authority is the
same as to rebel against God" (p. 20; the domains are worked out
at pp. 61-66 for governments and the home, p. 190 for elders).
(c) Submission to the delegated authority *as such* is
required, even where the delegated authority is unjust, on the pattern
of David refusing to lift his hand against Saul (1 Sam. 24; 26;
expounded at pp. 41-44): "Whether the one in authority is right
or wrong does not concern us ... The obedient needs only to obey;
the Lord will not hold us responsible for any mistaken obedience,
rather will He hold the delegated authority responsible for his
erroneous act" (p. 71). Nee's own ecclesiology (the Little Flock /
Local
Church) makes the elder the ordinary representative authority for
believers not otherwise under a father or husband.

Nee's book has been the direct or indirect source for much of the
subsequent covering literature; the Shepherding Movement leaders read
and cited him, and Bill Gothard's teaching shows strong structural
parallels. Later critics (both Chinese-language and Western) have
argued that Nee's application of David-and-Saul as a general
authority-submission principle overreads a narrative text; defenders
reply that Nee's exposition carries its own qualification
apparatus, which fairness requires stating. Nee treats Acts 5:29
("We must obey God rather than men") as a genuine exception
where "the delegated authority here had distinctly violated
God's command" (p. 74), and draws a formal distinction between
*submission* and *obedience*: "Should the delegated authority
issue an order clearly contradicting God's command, he will be
given submission but not obedience" (pp. 108-109). The book's
second half, moreover, is aimed at the authority-holder rather
than the subordinate: "Those who seek to exercise authority
should not be given authority" (p. 175); "The Lord does not come
to be authority; He comes to serve" (p. 176). Critics reply in
turn that the exception is confined to direct contradiction of
God's command — leaving the unjust-but-not-impious order, which
is the practically common case, under the p. 71 rule — and that
the twentieth-century applications (§§B.1.2-B.1.4) conserved
Nee's authority-structure while shedding his restraints on the
authority-holder.

**A living continuation of the Nee stream.** The Living Stream
Ministry (Anaheim, California; founded by Witness Lee, Nee's
principal co-worker, in 1974) keeps *Spiritual Authority* in print
under the title *Authority and Submission* and continues to teach
the deputy-authority doctrine through the network of "local
churches" associated with the Lee stream. As a systematic carrier
of the 1b covering doctrine as such, this stream is (a) currently
active in print and in teaching, (b) not implicated in the specific
misconduct findings that attach to the American IBLP-Vision-Forum
stream, (c) theologically contested on other grounds — the ground
of contention is chiefly the local-churches ecclesiology and its
associated christological formulations, not sexual or financial
misconduct — and (d) importantly outside the American homeschool-
movement genealogy that dominates §B.1.3-§B.1.4. Its inclusion in
this appendix does two things: it is honest about where the
deputy-authority doctrine's most sustained living continuation
now lives, and it complicates any framing that assumes the doctrine
is coextensive with the American movements. Readers who wish to
evaluate 1b's most current systematic articulation in print, and
who have found the American vehicles disqualified by the Reality-
leg data catalogued in §§B.1.3-B.1.4, will find *Authority and
Submission* to be its most direct current expression. The paper
does not endorse the local-churches ecclesiology; it simply names
where the doctrine's living systematic voice presently sits.

### B.1.2 The Shepherding Movement (Fort Lauderdale, 1966-1989)

The Shepherding (or Discipleship) Movement began with the Holy Spirit
Teaching Mission (HSTM, 1966), reorganized as Christian Growth
Ministries (CGM) in 1972 (CGM had begun publishing *New Wine*
magazine in 1969), and crystallized in 1974 when five
charismatic teachers — Bob Mumford, Derek Prince, Charles Simpson,
Don Basham, and Ern Baxter, known as the "Fort Lauderdale Five" —
formally submitted to one
another in a chain of "personal shepherds." Their teaching held that
every believer must be personally shepherded by another believer to
whom he owed obedience; the shepherd in turn was shepherded by another;
the chain of covering ran up through the Five themselves. Marriage,
vocational, geographic, and financial decisions were routinely made
under shepherd direction.

The movement was widely criticized in the mid-1970s by Pat Robertson,
Jimmy Swaggart, and Kathryn Kuhlman, and was the subject of a formal
"shepherding controversy" convened by the Assemblies of God and others
in 1975. Derek Prince publicly withdrew and repented in 1983; Bob
Mumford issued a formal apology in 1989-1990, "A Formal Repentance
Statement to the Body of Christ" (published widely in charismatic
periodicals; see *Ministries Today*, Jan/Feb 1990). The movement
formally dissolved in the late 1980s. The specific practices repented
of included the routine subordination of family and vocational
decisions to the shepherd, and the pastoral use of the covering
language to prevent members from leaving. (Sources: S. David Moore,
*The Shepherding Movement: Controversy and Charismatic Ecclesiology*,
T&T Clark / Continuum, 2003; Jamie Buckingham, "The End of the
Discipleship Era," *Ministries Today*, Jan/Feb 1990; Robert
Digitale, "Shepherding Movement: An Idea Whose Time Has Gone?"
*Christianity Today*, 19 March
1990. On the Nee → Shepherding link: Allan Clare's circulated
review *A Little History: The Truth about the Shepherding
Movement* (archived at archive.org); Christian Research Institute
material on the Local Church controversy.)

### B.1.3 Bill Gothard — the "Umbrella of Protection" (1961-2014)

Bill Gothard's ministry began in 1961 as Campus Teams, was renamed the
Institute in Basic Youth Conflicts (IBYC) in 1974, and again renamed
the Institute in Basic Life Principles (IBLP) in 1989. The Basic
Seminar reached an estimated 2.5 million attendees between the 1960s
and the 2010s. Gothard's "Umbrella of Protection" diagram — God over
father, father over mother, parents over children — was the visual
centerpiece of the teaching: living under one's proper umbrella
protects from spiritual attack; stepping out from under the umbrella
(for example, an adult unmarried daughter moving into her own
apartment) exposes the person to demonic influence.

Gothard's Advanced Training Institute (ATI, homeschool arm, launched
1984) and the Character First! program extended the teaching into
family and civic life. The Duggar family (of *19 Kids and Counting*)
was the most publicly visible ATI household. In February-March 2014,
Recovering Grace (recoveringgrace.org), an ex-IBLP watchdog site,
compiled testimonies from more than thirty women alleging inappropriate
conduct by Gothard over decades; the number reached at least 34 by
2015. Gothard resigned from IBLP in March 2014; the civil suit
*Wilkinson et al. v. IBLP* filed October 20, 2015 in DuPage County,
Illinois alleging sexual abuse, harassment, and cover-up was
voluntarily dismissed on statute-of-limitations grounds in February 2018. The 2023 Amazon Prime
documentary *Shiny Happy People: Duggar Family Secrets* brought
renewed public attention. IBLP continues to operate. (Sources: Kathryn
Joyce, *Quiverfull: Inside the Christian Patriarchy Movement*, Beacon
Press, 2009; the *Recovering Grace* archive; "The Cult Next Door,"
*Chicago Magazine*, July 2016; *Shiny Happy People: Duggar Family
Secrets*, Amazon Prime, 2023.)

**The "Chain of Counsel" doctrine — Gothard's operational vocabulary
for the marriage-formation window.** Gothard's *Basic Life
Principles* seminar and the accompanying *Life Notebook* material
name a specific operational form the covering claim takes at the
seam between formation and marriage: the *chain of counsel*. In
the Life Notebook's "Friends" section, under the heading "Eight
Qualities Essential for Marriage," the third essential quality is
listed as **"Harmony with Authority,"** with the practical
instruction to "establish chains of counsel" — a defined list
of persons (family members, friends, associates, spiritually
mature adults, teachers and spiritual leaders) to whom the
believer is accountable for major decisions, with special weight
given to parents. The specific catechetical script for a daughter
handling an approach from a suitor — "Before I can say yes, we'll
need to check with my father. Can you stop over and meet him?" —
and for the marriage question itself — "Before I could give you an
answer on marriage, you would have to discuss it with my father" —
appears in the same document. The paternal-mediation-of-courtship
practice thus becomes not merely a household preference but a
*marriage-formation precondition*: **"Harmony with Authority"** is
listed as one of eight qualities essential for a happy marriage,
and the recommended wording for a young adult asking forgiveness
of the parent — *"I have come to realize that I was wrong in
rejecting your authority and being ungrateful for all you have
done; I am calling to ask, will you forgive me?"* — categorizes
resistance to the parent's authority as by-definition sin
requiring restitution. This is the specific *operational* form the
Nee deputy-authority doctrine takes in the American
homeschool-movement setting: chain of counsel as social structure,
harmony with authority as marriage precondition, paternal
mediation of courtship as the operational script.

**The Life Notebook material continues to circulate.** A first-
person report from within IBLP-attender family networks (2026,
in the author's possession) — the "Eight Qualities Essential for
Marriage" material forwarded to the author by a church elder as
current pre-marital counsel — indicates that the doctrine
continues to circulate in materially unchanged form through the
same seminar-attender-to-household channel Gothard's ministry has
always used. That the doctrine's transmission has continued
through informal-network sharing rather than through fresh
systematic book publication is itself a data point about how
covering theology has *always* moved (Gothard's own model was
seminar attendance → notebook → household application), and the
mode persists.

### B.1.4 Doug Phillips, Vision Forum, and Biblical Patriarchy (1998-2014)

Doug Phillips founded Vision Forum in 1998 as an outgrowth of Vision
Forum Ministries (formally established 1998). Its published *Tenets
of Biblical Patriarchy* (2007) systematized the covering doctrine
into an explicit patriarchal ecclesiology: the father is priest and
authority of his household; unmarried adult daughters remain under
paternal authority "until given in marriage" to a husband who becomes
their new head; the notion of an independent unmarried adult daughter
as a normative pattern is rejected. Vision Forum's *Return of the
Daughters* documentary (2007) and the Botkin sisters' *So Much More*
(2005) popularized the daughter-at-home ideal.

Phillips resigned from Vision Forum Ministries on October 30, 2013,
following the disclosure of what he termed an "inappropriate
relationship" with Lourdes Torres-Manteufel, a young woman his family
had taken into their household. Vision Forum Ministries dissolved in
November 2013. Torres-Manteufel filed a civil lawsuit against Phillips
in Bexar County, Texas (April 15, 2014), alleging predatory grooming
from age 15 and a sexually abusive relationship spanning years; the
suit was settled in 2018. The National Center for Family-Integrated
Churches (NCFIC), which had been closely associated with Phillips,
continued under other leadership and articulated more moderate
positions. Douglas Wilson's Moscow, Idaho ministry (Christ Church /
Canon Press / *Blog and Mablog*) represents an ongoing, somewhat
distinct expression of biblical-patriarchy teaching without Phillips's
specific covering framework. (Sources: Julie Ingersoll, *Building
God's Kingdom: Inside the World of Christian Reconstruction*, Oxford
UP, 2015; *Torres-Manteufel v. Phillips*, Bexar County, TX, filed
April 15, 2014. On successors: the NCFIC website (ncfic.org) and
Douglas Wilson's *Blog & Mablog* (dougwils.com) provide the movement's
own current articulations.)

Kristin Kobes Du Mez, *Jesus and John Wayne: How White Evangelicals
Corrupted a Faith and Fractured a Nation* (Liveright, 2020), p. 294,
situates the teaching within a broader post-1970s network in which
"Doug Wilson, John Piper, Mark Driscoll, James Dobson, Doug
Phillips, and John Eldredge all preached a mutually reinforcing
vision of Christian masculinity — of patriarchy and submission, sex
and power." Du Mez's synthesis is scholarly reception of the
movement in its patriarchy-and-masculinity dimension. Readers
weighing it as evidence against particular figures should note that
the six named figures hold materially different positions on the
specific question of the unmarried adult child: Phillips articulates
the covering / Umbrella doctrine (§4.1b, B.1.4); Piper holds View
2a with a strong complementarian marriage theology; Dobson holds
View 2a with family-systems accents; Wilson holds 1a-strong on the
daily-life axis, paternal-mediation on the Arrangement Question,
and a covenantal covering-transfer claim for unmarried daughters
(§4.1b lists him among the current carriers on the daughters-
component only, and *not* as an advocate of the systematic
umbrella / covering doctrine, which he has publicly refused);
Driscoll and
Eldredge lie largely outside the adult-child debate as such. The
network Du Mez names is real; collapsing it into a single position
on this paper's question would be a category error.

### B.1.5 R.C. Sproul Jr. — Family-Integrated Church Voice (deposed 2006)

Not one of the four movements above but part of the same pattern: R.C.
Sproul Jr. was deposed from the ministry by the Westminster
Presbytery of the Reformed Presbyterian Church General Assembly in 2006
for what the presbytery called "abuse of authority in an inexcusable
manner." He had been an influential voice in the family-integrated
church movement. He was subsequently reinstated in a different
denomination but faced further controversies in later years.

### B.1.6 The "Recovery of Puritan and Catholic Teaching" Claim

Proponents of View 1b often present the doctrine as a *recovery* of
older Reformed or Catholic household piety. On the paper's reading
of the primary sources, the claim does not hold in the form its
proponents state it; readers who read the same sources differently
should test the passages quoted below.

*Puritan sources.* William Gouge, *Of Domesticall Duties* (1622),
articulates a robust doctrine of household order — husband over wife,
parents over children, master over servants — grounded in the fifth
commandment. But Gouge treats parental authority over children as
*age-limited* in its directive form: the treatise puts the question
directly at Treatise 5 §62 — "Quest. What if children be growne to
yeeres, and not vnder their parents gouernment?" — and answers,
"There may be some difference both in some parts of subiection, and
also in the manner of subiection betwixt such children as remaine
vnder their parents gouernment, and such as are freed from it: but
yet so long as a childe hath a parent, of what age or estate so euer
he be, he oweth an honour vnto his parent." Directive subjection is
tied to the "vnder gouernment" state; the honor duty continues past
it. Marriage
is the ordinary transition point at which the daughter passes from
paternal to marital authority; there is no doctrine that a fatherless
or launched adult daughter must be re-covered by an elder. Richard
Baxter, *A Christian Directory* (1673), similarly treats the
"government of children" as ending with adulthood and marriage;
Baxter is emphatic that the honor commandment continues but treats
marriage-promises made while "the child was at age" as adult acts
that bind on their own account, rather than as acts requiring
continuing parental consent for validity. The Westminster Larger Catechism (Q.
129-130) treats superiors and inferiors generally without positing an
umbrella-of-protection doctrine of continuous covering. The Puritan
household was ordered and pious; it was not umbrella-doctrinal in the
twentieth-century sense.

*Catholic sources.* The Catechism of the Catholic Church (2214-2220,
citing the tradition) is explicit: "Obedience toward parents ceases
with the emancipation of the children; not so respect, which is
always owed to them" (CCC 2217, as quoted at §4.3.1). Thomas Aquinas,
*Summa Theologiae* II-II, q. 101, treats *pietas* (filial piety) as a
lifelong virtue; but *obedientia* to parents is treated at II-II q.
104 as bounded by the natural termination of paternal power and by
the higher law of God. Aquinas is specific at q. 104 a. 5: Paul's
"in all things" (Col. 3:20) is read as jurisdictionally scoped, and
the article names the cases where the jurisdiction does not reach —
"servants are not bound to obey their masters, nor children their
parents, in the question of contracting marriage or of remaining in
the state of virginity or the like" (Dominican trans.; Latin text
in the Leonine edition via Corpus Thomisticum). The *doctor
communis* of the Catholic tradition thus places the paper's central
topic — the adult child's marriage decision — explicitly outside
continuing paternal directive authority, while condemning
*obedientia indiscreta* (undiscriminating obedience) as a vice
rather than a virtue. The 1983 Code of Canon Law (canons 1058, 1071,
1083) requires the ability to marry as an adult without parental
consent, and while parental blessing is strongly encouraged (canon
1071 § 1, 6°), it is not required for validity — a canonical position
established at the Council of Trent (Session 24, *Tametsi* decree,
Chapter I, 1563), which anathematized those who claimed that
marriages contracted by children without parental consent are
invalid. The Catholic tradition is emphatic on
lifelong *honor* and on *vocational discernment* under ecclesial
guidance; it does not teach an umbrella-of-protection doctrine of
continuous covering by a defined human authority.

The four modern strands are therefore, whatever their intended
continuity with older piety, a distinct twentieth-century doctrinal
development. This does not by itself falsify the doctrine, which
must be assessed on its own scriptural and theological claims (see
§4.1b). It does mean the "recovery" framing overstates the historical
warrant.

### B.1.7 The Actual Reformed and Puritan Warrant — for View 1a, Not 1b

A separate and prior tradition does bear on View 1a, and it should
not be lost inside the criticism of View 1b. The Reformed and Puritan
tradition of household worship — of the Christian household as "a
little church" — is a robust, textually-anchored, ecclesially-endorsed
pattern of pious multigenerational household life that is compatible
with View 1a but that is not the modern umbrella doctrine.

**Matthew Henry, *A Church in the House: A Sermon Concerning
Family-Religion* (London, preached 16 April 1704; printed for Tho.
Parkhurst).** Henry, the Presbyterian pastor at Chester and author of
the celebrated *Exposition of the Old and New Testament,* preached
this sermon in London and printed it "at the Request of Some who
Heard the Substance of it Preach'd." The core exhortation is that
"every house should be a little church" — that heads of households
lead daily Scripture reading, catechesis, and prayer with the whole
household present. Henry does not teach a covering doctrine, does not
teach that an adult unmarried child requires continuous submission
under an authority as a condition of God's protection, and does not
teach that departure from the paternal household is spiritually
dangerous per se. He teaches that the household — as long as it
persists as a household — is to be a worshiping unit. That is the
Puritan warrant relevant to View 1a: not authority-continuation but
worship-continuation.

**Cotton Mather, *Cares about the Nurseries* (Boston, 1702).** Mather
issued the work as two paired discourses: "The One, Offering Methods
and Motives for Parents to Catechise their Children, while yet under
the Tuition of their Parents. The Other, Offering Some Instructions
for Children, How they may do well, when they come to years of doing
for themselves." The paired shape of the work — parent-facing and
child-facing, "under the tuition of their parents" and "when they
come to years of doing for themselves" — makes explicit what the
older Reformed tradition assumed: that parental tuition has a
natural terminus (the child's years of "doing for themselves"), and
that both stages have their proper piety. The Puritan formation of
adult offspring is by pre-adult catechesis, not by post-adult
continuous covering. Mather also wrote the better-known *A Family
Well-Ordered* (1699), which similarly treats the reciprocal duties
of parents and children within an assumed household life-cycle.

**William Gouge and Richard Baxter** (already treated in §B.1.6):
Gouge's *Of Domesticall Duties* (1622) and Baxter's *A Christian
Directory* (1673) round out the picture. Both treat parental
authority over children as directive during the years of household
formation and honor-only after adulthood or marriage. Neither posits
a doctrine of continuous covering.

**What this warrant supports and does not support.**

The Reformed and Puritan household tradition supports:

- The household as a worshiping unit — daily Scripture, catechesis,
  prayer — for as long as the household persists.
- Deliberate, sustained parental catechesis of children through
  adolescence and into "years of doing for themselves" (Mather's
  phrase; Deut. 6:6-7).
- Multigenerational household life as an honorable and biblically
  witnessed pattern.
- Marriage as *an* ordinary transition point at which household
  membership is renegotiated (life-cycle service into another
  household was another; see §B.2.1 for the historical qualification).
- Fathers as active *disciplers,* not merely disciplinarians
  (Eph. 6:4).

It does not support:

- The umbrella doctrine — that adult believers require continuous
  covering by a defined human authority as a condition of spiritual
  protection.
- The claim that departure from the paternal household prior to
  marriage is per se sinful or spiritually dangerous.
- The claim that adult unmarried offspring owe ongoing directive
  obedience of the kind proper to minor children.
- Any doctrine that would nullify the honor Christ commands (Matt.
  15:4-6) by making household submission the means of that honor.

The Puritan warrant is real and it is different from the modern
umbrella doctrine. Both critics and defenders of View 1a will read
the tradition more clearly by keeping the two distinct.


### B.1.8 Current Carriers and Adjacent Voices

Two research notes migrated from §4.1b, preserved in full: the
search for currently active systematic carriers of the covering
doctrine, and the Plymouth Brethren / dispensationalist picture.

**A note on the current systematic carriers.** In preparing this
chapter the author searched, in print and online, for currently
active systematic defenses of the covering doctrine in the same
register as Nee, Gothard, or Phillips. The finding is fuller than
"the doctrine now lives only in older texts," and the paper
records the fuller finding so the reader is not left to infer an
absence that is not there. Four current carriers, each of a
different type, sit in the field, with two adjacent observations
about American mainstream-adjacent practitioners belonging in the
honest inventory though they do not themselves function as
carriers of the doctrine.

- *Exegetical, daughters-form, with a doctrinal-notoriety caveat.*
  William (Bill) Einwechter, a Reformed pastor and theonomist,
  wrote the most direct scholarly-register exegetical defense of
  the daughters-form of the claim: "What About the Unmarried
  Daughter?" (originally circulated in *Patriarch* magazine and the
  Vision Forum orbit), arguing from Numbers 30 and the father-of-
  the-virgin reading of 1 Corinthians 7:36-38 that the unmarried
  adult daughter remains under paternal authority until marriage.
  The caveat, which the paper names first in its
  name-the-asymmetry style: Einwechter is a theonomist whose 1999
  essay defending the continuing judicial validity of the
  Deuteronomy 21 penalty for the incorrigible son drew wide
  criticism — a doctrinal notoriety, distinct from personal
  misconduct but a real reason readers of his marriage essay will
  weigh it accordingly. Both essays share the assumption of the
  Mosaic judicial law's continuing magisterial reach; readers of
  the marriage essay can weigh it with that hermeneutic in view.
- *A living Nee stream, outside American evangelicalism.* Witness
  Lee's Living Stream Ministry (Anaheim, California; founded 1974)
  continues to print and teach Nee's *Spiritual Authority* as
  *Authority and Submission*. This is the deputy-authority
  doctrine's most sustained current systematic voice in print. It
  sits outside the American homeschool-movement genealogy that
  §B.1.3-§B.1.4 catalogues; it is theologically contested on other
  grounds (chiefly its local-churches ecclesiology and associated
  christological formulations), not on the Reality-leg data that
  attaches to the American movements. Treated in §B.1.1.
- *The Gothard Life Notebook material, continuing through
  informal-network transmission.* The chain-of-counsel doctrine
  Gothard developed in the IBLP *Basic Life Principles* seminar and
  the accompanying *Life Notebook* material has never depended on
  bookstore-shelf distribution; it moves through the seminar-to-
  notebook-to-household channel Gothard's ministry has always used.
  A first-person report from within IBLP-attender family networks
  (2026, in the author's possession) — "Eight Qualities Essential
  for Marriage" and its associated catechetical scripts forwarded
  to the author as current pre-marital counsel — indicates that
  the material continues to circulate in materially unchanged form.
  Treated in §B.1.3.
- *A daughters-component partial carrier, inside a framework that
  refuses the full covering doctrine.* Douglas Wilson's *Her Hand
  in Marriage: Biblical Courtship in the Modern World* (Canon
  Press, 1997) is the most rhetorically able current living
  defense of two specific components of the traditional claim:
  the father's continuing jurisdiction over the daughter's
  courtship (Exod. 22:16-17; Num. 30; the bride-price grammar)
  and the covering-transfer at marriage. On those two components
  it is stronger writing than any other current source. Wilson
  advances the argument, however, inside a framework that
  *explicitly rejects* the universal umbrella / covering mechanics
  of the Vision Forum articulation — he has publicly refused the
  daughters-at-home absolutism in print — and his position on
  sons is launch-shaped (*Future Men,* Canon Press, 1999).
  Wilson thus belongs in the honest inventory as a current
  carrier of the *daughters-component* of 1b, not as a
  systematic advocate of the whole covering doctrine. The paper
  cites *Her Hand in Marriage* on its exegetical merits in the
  Arrangement Question treatment (§5.3); its inclusion here is
  the same discipline applied to the carriers finding —
  engage the text and name the frame, rather than avoid the
  contested person. Wilson's public controversies lie on grounds
  unrelated to the covering doctrine and are not Reality-leg data
  for it; the paper engages the text. Treated further at §B.1.4.

Two adjacent observations belong in the inventory though they are
not themselves carriers of the covering doctrine:

- *The deliberate boundary case — American mainstream-adjacent
  voices who articulate a boundary against the full claim.*
  Voddie Baucham, Scott Brown, Jason Dohm, and Dwight Cover —
  practitioners who take the household-continuity pattern most
  seriously in the current American evangelical setting — are, on
  the daily-life axis, working in the 1a register (household as
  pattern and pastoral wisdom) rather than the 1b register
  (household as systematic doctrinal requirement). Baucham's
  father-as-family-shepherd framework — building the household
  discipleship ethos of *Family Driven Faith* (2007) into a
  head-of-household formal structure in *Family Shepherds:
  Calling and Equipping Men to Lead Their Homes* (Crossway, 2011)
  — is the paradigm case. Baucham's own critique of reducing
  fatherhood to material provision is direct: "Parenting,
  especially when it comes to fathers, has been reduced to a
  materialistic endeavor. A man with a job that takes him away
  from his wife and children and removes all possibility of
  spiritual influence now takes solace in the fact that he's
  'working to give them what really matters'" (*Family Shepherds*,
  p. 75). The right ordering, in Baucham's own framing, subordinates
  career to family identity: "Are you a doctor who has a wife on
  the side, or are you a husband and father who provides for his
  family through a job in the medical field?" (*Family Shepherds*,
  p. 96). The formulation is thus a household-shepherd doctrine
  rather than a covering doctrine: material provision matters but
  is not sufficient; spiritual shepherding of the household is the
  father's core duty. The doctrinal under-frame is a
  three-jurisdictions sphere-sovereignty: "God designed the world
  with three distinct institutions — the family, the church, and
  the civil government — each with specific jurisdictions. The
  church can no more tell a family how to run its affairs than it
  can tell the state how to run theirs. Certainly, the church has
  a responsibility to teach, admonish, warn, and guide. However,
  it may not govern the other jurisdictions" (*Family Shepherds*,
  p. 176) — a Kuyperian sphere-separation that distinguishes 1a
  from 1b (which reads the church-elder covering as continuing over
  the launched adult child). Baucham himself treats launch as the
  expected trajectory: his chapter sections "Preparing Your
  Marriage for the Empty Nest" and "Preparing Your Children for
  Marriage" (pp. 97-98) are consistent with a frame in which the
  family-shepherd's authority is bounded at the household-formation
  seam rather than extended over the launched adult; and his use of the "adult children" category
  at p. 175 places them in the 1 Tim. 5:4 reverse-provision
  direction (adult children caring for aging parents), a role that
  presupposes their own independent household-formation. Cover's
  Cop → Coach → Consultant chart (Christian Heritage Conference
  2025 talk, "Dads, Prepare and Launch Sons as Men of God";
  unpublished chart in author's possession, discussed at §5.0.5)
  explicitly argues that
  parental discipline should decline to 0% by adulthood, targeting
  "shoulder-to-shoulder, man-to-man respect" and the adult child
  as "fellow-heir of the grace of life." Brown and Dohm's National
  Center for Family-Integrated Churches teaching draws the same
  boundary in family-integrated-church register. These voices are
  the doctrine's current *deliberate boundary case,* practitioners
  of the household-continuity intuition who have positioned
  themselves in print against the full continuous-authority claim
  while running the outward shape.
- *Practice without doctrinal adjudication.* Steven and Teri
  Maxwell's Titus2 material (books, blog, and family conferences
  over the 1990s-2020s) runs the father-mediated courtship and
  adult-children-at-home practices at full strength without
  articulating a position on the systematic doctrinal claim in
  either direction — practice literature without the doctrine's
  adjudication. Their material is thus an honest data point about
  how the outward household-continuity shape can be run as
  devotional-practical piety detached from the doctrinal
  question, but it is not itself a carrier of the covering
  doctrine's articulation.

The reader is thus not left to infer that the absence of a
mainstream-bookstore systematic advocate means the position is
unheld or unarticulated; it is held, and it is articulated in
four current active carriers of different types — Einwechter's
exegetical essay defending the daughters-form, Living Stream
Ministry's continuation of Nee outside American evangelicalism,
Gothard Life Notebook material circulating through IBLP-attender
networks, and Wilson's daughters-component argument inside a
covering-doctrine-refusing frame. The mainstream American
practitioners closest to the intuition either draw the boundary
explicitly in print (Baucham, Cover, Brown, Dohm) or run the
practice without adjudicating the doctrine (Maxwells).

**Note on Plymouth Brethren and dispensationalist writers.** These
communities and voices are sometimes assumed to share 4.1b, but the
picture is more complex. The *Exclusive Brethren* lineage (Darby →
J.B. Stoney → F.E. Raven → J.T. Taylor Sr. and Jr. → Hales, now
organized as the Plymouth Brethren Christian Church) practices
strict community and family separation from outsiders, including
"withdrawing from" (a form of shunning) that can sever adult
family relationships — a practice the community itself grounds in
its reading of the separation texts (2 Tim. 2:19-22; 2 Cor.
6:14-17) and in Darby's founding principle (*Separation from Evil
God's Principle of Unity*, 1834), as ecclesial obedience rather
than family cutoff. Ngaire Thomas, raised in the Taylor-era
Exclusive Brethren and now outside the community, describes the
formation aim in her memoir *Behind Closed Doors* (Penguin NZ,
2004): "an independent spirit ... usually meant that a person had
begun to think for themselves rather than obeying the M.O.G.
[Man of God] — an undesirable trait that needed to be dealt with."
Craig Hoyle's *Excommunicated* (HarperCollins NZ, 2023) documents
the same pattern from another survivor's perspective. The
practical outcome — adult community members remaining under
communal and paternal authority — resembles View 1b in outward
shape, but the theological engine is *ecclesial-separation
doctrine* rather than Nee's *delegated-authority* teaching or
Gothard's *umbrella* teaching.

The *Open Brethren* (Anthony Norris Groves, Robert Chapman, George
Müller, and their heirs) have no doctrinal position on adult
unmarried children as such and in practice track the mainstream
evangelical launched-at-adulthood pattern (§4.2a) with strong
assembly involvement.

*Dispensationalist writers proper.* Modern American
dispensationalism *originated* in the Brethren movement — Darby's
sevenfold division of biblical history and his pre-tribulational
rapture teaching are its parent stock — but propagated across the
American evangelical world well beyond the Brethren, through the
Scofield Reference Bible (1909, revised 1917) and the Dallas
Theological Seminary tradition (Lewis Sperry Chafer, John
Walvoord, Charles Ryrie, J. Dwight Pentecost). These writers are
not Brethren, and neither is the most prominent contemporary
inheritor of the dispensational lineage, John MacArthur — pastor
of Grace Community Church in Sun Valley, California, and founder
of The Master's Seminary, whose soteriology is Reformed and whose
ecclesiology is independent and Baptistic. He is a "leaky"
dispensationalist in the classification the movement uses of
itself, not a Brethren.

None of these writers press the umbrella doctrine, and where they
engage the Ephesians 6:1 passage they tend to hold the age-limited
reading. MacArthur's *Ephesians* (Moody, 1986, at Eph. 6:1) reads
it explicitly: "*Tekna* (children) is a general term for children
and is not limited to a specific age group. It refers to any child
still living in the home and under parental guidance." The
present-tense imperative "demands a continuous obedience" — but
continuous *within* the age-scope of "child still living in the
home and under parental guidance." That is substantially the
status-bounded reading toward which the paper's own lexical lean
runs (§1.1.1, §1.2.1). The Scofield Reference Bible
(1917 note at Eph. 6:1) is a bare cross-reference to Colossians
3:20 with no substantive treatment of scope. Ryrie's *Basic
Theology* (Moody, 1986/1999) and study-Bible notes do not
thematize the age-limitation question. The silence itself is
informative: the twentieth-century American dispensational
tradition, wherever it engaged the passage, tended to hold the
age-limited reading, and where it did not engage the question it
did not press a continuous-authority claim.

## B.2 Modern Origins of View 2 (Launched at Adulthood)

Views 2a and 2b share a common historical arc. Both are products of
industrial and post-industrial reorganization of the household and
of a series of twentieth-century developmental-psychology and pastoral
frameworks. 2a and 2b diverge at the theological interpretation of
those developments, not at the developments themselves.

### B.2.1 The Pre-Industrial Household

The picture of a pre-industrial household as a stable unit in which
unmarried adult sons and daughters uniformly remained under the
paternal roof until marriage requires qualification, particularly for
the Northwest European and Anglo-American stream from which the
Reformed, Puritan, and evangelical traditions descend. The dominant
household-historical scholarship since John Hajnal's 1965 paper on
European marriage patterns — extended by Peter Laslett and the
Cambridge Group, and documented in exhaustive detail for England by
Ann Kussmaul's *Servants in Husbandry in Early Modern England*
(Cambridge, 1981) and for colonial New England by Edmund S. Morgan's
*The Puritan Family* (1944; rev. Harper & Row, 1966) — describes a
"Northwest European Marriage Pattern" (NWEMP) with three features
material to the present question. First, mean age at first marriage
in England and its colonies from at least the sixteenth century ran
in the mid-to-late twenties for both sexes — much later than the
Mediterranean or Slavic patterns. Second, between roughly ages
fourteen and marriage, a substantial fraction of young people —
Kussmaul's estimate (ch. 1) for early modern England is that servants
"constituted around 60 per cent of the population aged fifteen to
twenty-four," and the practice cut across social strata — left the
paternal household and lived within *another* household as servants
in husbandry, apprentices, or domestic servants, under the discipline
of the master and mistress of that household rather than of their
biological parents. Third, this out-of-household residence was
ordinarily terminated by marriage, at which point the young person
established a new independent household rather than returning to the
parental one.

Two theological implications follow, and both matter for the paper's
downstream reasoning. The first is that the historic norm the Reformed
and Puritan streams *actually* inherited and codified was not
"unmarried children remain under the paternal roof until marriage" but
something closer to "unmarried children are formed within a household
— either the parental household or another household of the same
covenanted community — under discipline, until marriage." Morgan
documents that Puritan New England assumed and legally regulated the
placement of unmarried teenagers into other households (see the
Massachusetts Bay orders of 1668 requiring town selectmen to place
"unruly" youth in service — reprinted in William H. Whitmore, ed.,
*The Colonial Laws of Massachusetts, Reprinted from the Edition of
1672,* Boston, 1889). The paternal household was one instance
of a broader category — the ordered household under a head — and the
practice of placing unmarried adults *outside* the parental home under
another head's authority was ordinary, not exceptional. Second: the
transfer point commonly appealed to in Chapter 4's Arrangement
discussion (§4.6) — that departure from the parental household
historically coincided with marriage — is true for one leg of the
young adult population (those who remained on the farm or in the
family trade until marriage) but not for the very large fraction who
left the parental household as adolescents for service in another
household. The pattern View 1a appeals to at §B.1.7 as "the historic
norm" is more accurately described as *one* historic norm within a
broader system in which household-of-formation and household-of-origin
were routinely different.

None of this settles the exegetical or theological question. What it
does is remove one implicit historical support from any view that
frames "remaining in the paternal household until marriage" as *the*
uniform pre-industrial pattern that the industrial and postindustrial
world merely disrupted. The historic Anglo-American practice was
already a mixed one, and the Reformed tradition regulated the mixture
under a household-discipline framework rather than a paternal-only
framework. The industrial and post-industrial disruption (§B.2.2) is
real and is what View 2 responds to; but the pattern it disrupted was
not the one View 1a often implicitly reconstructs. Sense (2) of
authority in §1.1.1 — formative office — was routinely exercised in
the historic pattern by a household head other than the biological
father; senses (3) and (4) — counsel and priestly office — remained
with the biological parents; and sense (1) — directive authority in
the moment — followed residence and covenant, not biology.

### B.2.2 Industrialization, Urbanization, and the College Track

Nineteenth- and early twentieth-century industrialization moved
productive work outside the household, opened wage-labor to unmarried
young adults, and made independent housing economically feasible. The
Servicemen's Readjustment Act (the GI Bill, June 22, 1944) subsidized
college education and low-interest home loans for returning servicemen
and reshaped the American household: by the mid-1950s an unprecedented
share of American young adults left the paternal household in their
late teens or early twenties, first for military service, then for
college, then for independent housing in the emerging suburbs. The
Federal Housing Administration (established 1934) and Veterans
Administration mortgage programs, combined with postwar suburban
tract construction (Levittown, 1947), made independent household
formation an economic default rather than an economic exception. What
had been a launching pattern tied to marriage became, for the first
time in Western history, a launching pattern tied to age-of-majority.

### B.2.3 Developmental Psychology — Erikson and Arnett

Erik Erikson's *Childhood and Society* (1950) proposed a life-cycle
model in which adolescence and young adulthood are characterized by
*identity vs. role confusion* — a stage in which the young person
must construct an identity distinct from parents in order to enter
adult intimacy and generativity. Erikson's model gave a developmental
framing to what postwar suburbanization was already producing: an
extended period between the end of parental authority and the
formation of the new household, treated as a *necessary* stage rather
than an incidental delay. The framework was elaborated by James
Marcia's identity-status research in the 1960s-1970s and became
standard in American developmental psychology.

Jeffrey Jensen Arnett's "Emerging Adulthood: A Theory of Development
from the Late Teens Through the Twenties" (*American Psychologist* 55,
May 2000; expanded in *Emerging Adulthood: The Winding Road from the
Late Teens Through the Twenties*, Oxford UP, 2004, second ed. 2015)
proposed that the period between roughly 18 and 29 has become a
distinct developmental stage in industrialized societies, characterized
by identity exploration, instability, self-focus, feeling in-between,
and a sense of possibility. Arnett explicitly framed emerging
adulthood as *not*-yet-adulthood — a period in which the person is
neither adolescent nor fully adult, and in which the developmental
task is self-authorship. The framework was welcomed by some as
descriptive; it has been critiqued (by Christian Smith and others,
see B.3) as *prescriptive* of the very pattern it purports to
describe.

### B.2.4 Focus on the Family and the Evangelical Adaptation

Focus on the Family was founded by James Dobson in 1977. Dobson's
early books (*Dare to Discipline*, 1970; *The Strong-Willed Child*,
1978) addressed the parenting of minor children; his later work
(*Bringing Up Boys*, 2001; *Bringing Up Girls*, 2010) and Focus's
ongoing radio and print counsel to parents of adult children addressed
the launching question directly. Dobson's counsel warned against
both extremes — hovering parents who cannot release their adult
children, and disengaged parents who abandon them at eighteen — and
articulated the position now catalogued as View 2a: directive
authority ends at adulthood; honor, counsel, and moral obligation
continue. Gary Chapman's *The Five Love Languages* (1992) and
subsequent works on adult relationships extended similar counsel to
the marriage and family sphere. The counsel typical of American
parachurch youth ministries (Young Life, Cru / Campus Crusade, Youth
for Christ) during the same decades reinforced the launch-at-18
pattern within an evangelical framework of continuing honor.

### B.2.5 The Boomerang Generation and the Delayed Launch

Beginning in the 1990s and accelerating after the 2008 financial
crisis, American young adults began returning to (or not leaving) the
parental household in unprecedented numbers. Pew Research Center
reported that in September 2020, for the first time since the Great
Depression, a majority of U.S. adults aged 18-29 lived with their
parents (Fry, Passel, and Cohn, "A Majority of Young Adults in the
U.S. Live with Their Parents for the First Time Since the Great
Depression," Pew Research Center, September 4, 2020). This economic
shift has partially recomposed the launched-at-adulthood pattern —
either through economic necessity or through a re-evaluation of the
practice — without in itself resolving the theological question of
what authority attaches to household residence.

### B.2.6 Where 2a and 2b Diverge

The material and developmental history is common to both. 2a
interprets the emerging pattern as a legitimate *shift of mode* —
directive authority naturally ends when economic and vocational
independence begin; honor, counsel, and moral obligation continue.
2b interprets the emerging pattern as a *covenantal
release* — the adult child answerable directly to Christ, the
parent's office becoming priestly (intercession, counsel,
watchfulness) rather than directive. A third interpretation — the
person as sole authority over his or her own life, residual
parental claims elective — is the Abandonment drift (§5.5), not a
faithful view. The theological divergence lies at exactly this
point, not at the economic or developmental one.

### B.2.7 The Secular Career-Track Parallel and Delayed Marriage

The pattern of delayed marriage paired with adult-woman frustration
documented in §4.1a Challenges and (for the harder 1b / umbrella
form) at §4.1b's Reality-leg discussion (Recovering Grace and West
2024 at §4.1b; Kobes Du Mez 2020 at §B.1.4) has a striking secular
parallel that
shows the pattern is not peculiar to Christian patriarchy.

Since the 1970s, the US median age at first marriage has risen
sharply. The US Census Bureau (Historical Marital Status Tables,
Table MS-2, "Estimated Median Age at First Marriage, by Sex: 1890
to Present," census.gov/data/tables/time-series/demo/families/
marital.html) reports:

- 1970: men 23.2 years, women 20.8 years
- 2023: men 30.2 years, women 28.4 years

A rise of seven to eight years for both sexes in half a century. A
parallel literature — journalistic, sociological, and memoir — has
followed the trajectory of the women involved.

*Sylvia Ann Hewlett's* founding text of the modern discussion,
*Creating a Life: Professional Women and the Quest for Children*
(Talk Miramax, 2002), reported that 33% of high-achieving career
women aged 41-55 were childless, rising to 49% among "ultra-
achievers" earning over $100,000. The accompanying *Harvard
Business Review* article, "Executive Women and the Myth of Having
It All" (April 2002), crystallized the finding: "Nowadays, the
rule of thumb seems to be that the more successful the woman, the
less likely it is she will find a husband or bear a child."

*Lori Gottlieb's* much-discussed *Atlantic* essay "Marry Him! The
Case for Settling for Mr. Good Enough" (March 2008) argued the
regret side of the same trend from the inside: "My advice is
this: Settle! ... Marriage ultimately isn't about cosmic
connection — it's about how having a teammate, even if he's not
the love of your life, is better than not having one at all." Her
subsequent book *Marry Him* (Dutton, 2010) expanded the argument.

*Kate Bolick's* *Spinster: Making a Life of One's Own* (Crown,
2015) opens: "Whom to marry, and when will it happen — these two
questions define every woman's existence, regardless of where she
was raised or what religion she does or doesn't practice" (p. 1).
Bolick's book alternates memoir and cultural history and treats
prolonged singleness sympathetically.

*Rebecca Traister's* *All the Single Ladies: Unmarried Women and
the Rise of an Independent Nation* (Simon & Schuster, 2016)
provides the historical framing: for the entire history of the
United States "the median age at first marriage for women had
remained between twenty and twenty-two." Traister documents that
in 2009 the proportion of American women who were married dropped
below fifty percent for the first time.

**What this comparative note establishes for the SCAR analysis.**
The pattern of delayed marriage paired with adult-woman frustration
— including the specific "old maid" fear that L.J. records at
Recovering Grace (§4.1b Challenges) and that Gottlieb articulates
in her own
voice — appears in *both* the over-protective direction (§4.1a
drifting into the Clutch, §4.1b's Clutch drift, §5.5) *and* the
launched-and-adrift direction (§4.2a drifting toward autonomy;
§4.2b drifting into the Abandonment, §5.5; and the career-first
secular pattern reaching the same downstream outcome by a wholly
different route). The two directions arrive at the same downstream
outcome by opposite drifts of opposite views. Faithful 1a, 1b, 2a,
and 2b all name and resist the drift that would produce the
delayed-marriage-and-frustration cohort; the Reality-leg data
records the shape of what happens where the drift is not resisted.
This is a Reality-leg observation neither of the launched-at-adulthood
views nor either of the household-continuity views can safely
ignore: whichever way the launch is arranged, formation patterns
in which marriage is deferred by default — rather than either
pursued as a formative goal or set aside for a discerned calling
(§4.3) — produce the same downstream cohort by different routes.

## B.3 Empirical Studies on Launched Emerging Adults

The following empirical literature bears on the "Reality" leg of the
SCAR quadrilateral, particularly with respect to Views 2a and 2b.

*Methodological caveats.* The studies below are sociological and
psychological, not theological. They can describe *what happens* to
young adults in various launching patterns; they cannot by themselves
adjudicate *what should* happen. The empirical picture is also
predominantly American, predominantly Protestant, and predominantly
of the post-1980 cohorts; extrapolation to other cultural contexts
is limited. The findings are cited here as one input to the SCAR
"Reality" leg, not as conclusive. One symmetry note: the attrition
and outcome findings for launched cohorts (§§B.3.1-B.3.4) carry
methodological limits of the same kind §B.3.5 flags for the
household-continuity data — self-report instruments, cohort
effects, the absence of comparison groups raised under the
alternative patterns, and no counterfactual identification of the
household variable itself. The caveats run in both directions, not
only against the smaller literature.

### B.3.1 Christian Smith and the National Study of Youth and Religion (NSYR)

The National Study of Youth and Religion, directed by Christian Smith
(University of Notre Dame) with funding from the Lilly Endowment,
followed a nationally representative cohort of American teenagers
into emerging adulthood over more than a decade. Three books:

- *Soul Searching: The Religious and Spiritual Lives of American
  Teenagers* (with Melinda Lundquist Denton, Oxford UP, 2005). Study
  of American 13-17-year-olds. Introduced the term *Moralistic
  Therapeutic Deism* (MTD) as the functional lived theology of most
  American teenagers regardless of their nominal tradition:
  God exists and wants people to be good; the central goal of life
  is to be happy and feel good about oneself; God does not need to
  be particularly involved in life except when needed to resolve a
  problem; good people go to heaven when they die.
- *Souls in Transition: The Religious and Spiritual Lives of Emerging
  Adults* (with Patricia Snell, Oxford UP, 2009). Follow-up study of
  the same cohort at ages 18-23. Documented substantial religious
  attrition during the launch: emerging adults raised with strong
  parental religious formation retained faith at higher rates than
  those without, but even in the strongest formation groups,
  substantial minorities departed. The functional theology of the
  cohort remained MTD-shaped.
- *Lost in Transition: The Dark Side of Emerging Adulthood* (with
  Kari Christoffersen, Hilary Davidson, and Patricia Snell Herzog,
  Oxford UP, 2011). Documented five *problematic* domains in the
  emerging-adult cohort: confused moral reasoning, routine
  intoxication, materialistic life goals, regretted sexual
  experiences, and civic and political disengagement. Smith and
  colleagues were careful to distinguish these findings from a moral
  panic — most emerging adults are not in acute crisis — while
  arguing that the cultural apparatus supporting emerging adulthood
  is producing genuinely worse outcomes on these axes than earlier
  cohorts.

### B.3.2 David Kinnaman and Barna Group

David Kinnaman, *You Lost Me: Why Young Christians Are Leaving Church
… and Rethinking Faith* (Baker Books, 2011), synthesized Barna Group
studies of American church-raised young adults ages 18-29 and
identified six broad reasons for disaffiliation (Kinnaman's own
chapter labels): churches seem *overprotective* (ch. 5), *shallow*
(ch. 6), *antiscience* (ch. 7), *repressive* — particularly on
sexuality — (ch. 8), *exclusive* (ch. 9), and *doubtless* — meaning
unwelcoming of honest doubt (ch. 10). Kinnaman's *Faith for
Exiles* (with Mark Matlock, Baker Books, 2019) followed with a
smaller but positive study of "resilient disciples" who retained
Christian practice through the launch. Kinnaman and Matlock
identify five co-equal practices; the third — "forge meaningful,
intergenerational relationships" — is characterized as one of the
defining features of the resilient-disciple group, whose
cross-generational church-community ties are reported to be
substantially deeper than those of the prodigal / nomad /
habitual-churchgoer cohorts.

### B.3.3 Jean Twenge — *iGen* and the Post-Smartphone Cohort

Jean Twenge, *iGen: Why Today's Super-Connected Kids Are Growing Up
Less Rebellious, More Tolerant, Less Happy — and Completely
Unprepared for Adulthood* (Atria, 2017), documented statistical
shifts in the cohort born approximately 1995-2012: delayed
independence markers (driver's license, dating, working, drinking,
sexual initiation), increased screen use, and — most controversially
— sharply increased rates of depression, anxiety, and suicidality
correlated with the mass adoption of the smartphone around 2012.
Twenge's causal claims (particularly the smartphone-mental-health
link) have been contested in the literature; the descriptive claim
that this cohort is markedly less independent at each life-stage
than immediately preceding cohorts is broadly accepted. Twenge's
*Generations* (Atria, 2023) extends the analysis across six
generational cohorts.

### B.3.4 What the Empirical Picture Does and Does Not Show

The empirical picture is *consistent with* the concern that the
launched-at-adulthood pattern, particularly as it drifts toward
the *Abandonment* (§5.5), produces measurable costs in religious
retention, moral
formation, and (per Twenge) mental health. It does not by itself
show that any of the alternative views — 1a, 1b, or any answer to
the Arrangement Question — produce demonstrably better outcomes on
the same measures at scale, since comparable longitudinal studies of
adult children raised under those patterns are lacking. Kinnaman's
finding that multi-generational integrated community predicts
resilience is suggestive of View 1a (in either its covering or
kingdom-directed accent). Christian Smith's findings on the
durability of
parental religious formation are consistent with every faithful
view's insistence on the primacy of the household. The empirical literature
supports the *diagnostic* seriousness of the challenges (§4.2a and
§4.2b "Challenges" sections) without prescribing a particular
alternative.

### B.3.5 What Positive Data Exists for 1a-Shaped Communities

Because the surveyed longitudinal studies (Smith, Kinnaman, Twenge)
were designed around mainstream-evangelical and general-population
samples, they under-sample the communities where View 1a is the
lived norm. What positive data exists is smaller-N, tradition-
specific, and best treated as *suggestive* rather than probative.
It is nonetheless part of the Reality leg and is owed to the reader.

*A note on figures.* Where specific percentages are quoted, they
are drawn directly from the named primary reports (Kraybill et al.
2013 for Amish retention; the 2018 Cardus reports *From the
Classroom to the Workplace* and *Involved and Engaged* for the
homeschool figures). Earlier-wave figures (2011, 2014) and figures
drawn from the parallel *social-ties-and-relationships* thematic
report have not been individually pinpoint-verified for this draft;
formal work should consult the specific report and wave. The
concept-level claims — that household-continuity communities exist
as a distinct measurable population, and that the studies of them
are mixed rather than uniformly favorable — hold at the concept
level independent of any particular figure. This scoping applies
to §B.3.5 only; §§B.3.1–B.3.4 summarize well-known published
theses of Smith, Kinnaman, and Twenge and do not carry the same
caveat.

*Old Order Anabaptist retention.* Sociological studies of Old
Order Amish and conservative Mennonite communities (Donald
Kraybill, *The Riddle of Amish Culture*, rev. 2001; Kraybill,
Johnson-Weiner, and Nolt, *The Amish*, Johns Hopkins, 2013,
Chapter 9 "Retention and Defection") report a broad band of
adult-child retention rates — roughly 80-95 percent depending on
affiliation and settlement, with Nolt and Meyers reporting a
present northern Indiana rate of about 95 percent (up from 80
percent in the 1920s-30s) and the more liberal New Order
defecting at ~40 percent while the traditional Andy Weaver
group loses ~2.6 percent (Kraybill et al. 2013, p. 163 with
notes at p. 439) — figures substantially above the retention
rates reported
in mainstream-evangelical longitudinal studies, though measures
and denominators differ across studies (see pre-note above). These communities live a
variant of View 1a in a strong form: unmarried adults ordinarily
remain in or adjacent to the parental household and community;
vocational formation is intra-community; marriage arises within a
bounded courting network. The retention finding does not by itself
vindicate any particular exegesis, but it establishes that
household-continuity communities can be measured and that their
measurable outcomes on retention are not weaker than the
mainstream alternative.

*Christian homeschool graduates — a mixed record.* The Cardus
Education Survey (Ray Pennings et al., 2011, 2014, 2018) is one of
the few large-N studies of formation outcomes by schooling type.
Its record for religious homeschool graduates specifically — a
subgroup with n = 124 in the 2018 U.S. wave, small enough that
Cardus themselves flag chance-fluctuation risk — is genuinely
mixed, and the paper reports it as such.

On the positive side, the 2018 CES thematic report *Involved and
Engaged* finds that "the likelihood of donating to a religious
congregation is higher among all religious school graduates,
especially among Protestant school and religious homeschool
graduates," and that religious school graduates (homeschool and
Protestant school alike) are "also more likely than public school
graduates to donate money or goods to other types of religious
organizations or causes as well." Mission and social-service trip
participation is also higher among religious homeschool graduates
than public school graduates, though the homeschool effect there
is largely accounted for by demographic and family background
variables. Cardus's evidence for higher *early-marriage* formation
is best attested for evangelical Protestant Christian *school*
graduates, not homeschool graduates specifically; the 2018
*Involved and Engaged* report does not present marriage-timing
findings for the homeschool subsample.

On the countervailing side, *Involved and Engaged* also finds that
"Protestant and religious homeschool graduates are less likely to
report that they volunteer for a neighbourhood or civic
organization," that religious homeschool graduates report a lower
average number of civic organizations, that they are "70 percent
less likely" than public school graduates to volunteer for
international issues or causes, and that once family-background
controls are applied, total-volunteer-hour differences across
school sectors are not statistically significant. The 2018 CES
report *From the Classroom to the Workplace* found that
respondents homeschooled for religious reasons had completed on
average "just over one year less of schooling than public school
graduates," were "about four times more likely to leave schooling
directly after graduating from high school," were "over twice as
likely to be unemployed" than public school graduates, and reported
household income "about $10,000 lower on average" than public
school graduates; the odds of a religious homeschool graduate
attaining an advanced degree were "about 60 percent less than an
average public school graduate."

The honest summary is that religious homeschool graduates are
strong on *religious-institutional* giving and mission-trip
participation, roughly comparable to public school graduates on
total volunteer hours once family background is controlled, and
below public school graduates on secular civic volunteering,
international-cause volunteering, years of education,
advanced-degree attainment, employment, and household income. The
picture is not "homeschool graduates do better" or "worse" as a
totality; it is that homeschool formation ties a graduate more
tightly to congregational and religious-cause life, and less
tightly to secular civic and labor-market outcomes. Brian Ray's surveys through
the National Home Education Research Institute (NHERI) report
patterns favorable to homeschooling; NHERI is a
homeschool-advocacy-affiliated research shop, which the paper flags
alongside the ordinary self-selection caveat (families who choose
homeschooling differ from families who do not, so what is measured
is outcomes for the population as such, not the counterfactual
effect of the schooling choice itself; and homeschooling is here a
proxy for the formation-intensive household, not a direct measure
of adult household continuity). Overall the Cardus/NHERI
record supports the claim that these communities produce
measurable strengths in congregational and formation outcomes,
alongside measurable deficits in secular civic and labor-market
outcomes, without supporting an unqualified claim of educational
or occupational advantage.

*Catholic communities with strong household catechesis.* Reports
from the Center for Applied Research in the Apostolate (CARA,
Georgetown) on Mass attendance and vocations across Catholic
sub-populations consistently find that adult retention in the
faith tracks the strength of household-of-origin practice more
closely than most other single variables studied. This is
consistent with (though not exclusive to) View 1a's insistence on
the household as the primary catechetical unit.

*A note on first-person 1b goods testimony.* In preparing this
appendix the author searched for a substantial body of
first-person testimony from adults raised under a systematic
continuous-authority pattern (1b proper, in the Nee / Gothard /
Phillips register rather than the moderate 1a register) who
describe the experience as good — the sort of memoir literature
that would parallel West 2024 or the Recovering Grace archive but
in the affirmative direction. What surfaced was scattered — brief
testimonies within apologetic literature for the position, comments
sections, homeschool-network publications — rather than the
book-length first-person accounts that would carry the same
evidentiary weight as the critical memoirs. The paper records the
search and its outcome so the asymmetry in the appendix's
testimony citations is understood as a real feature of the
available literature rather than a selection choice. That the
critical memoirs are more substantial than the affirmative
memoirs is itself Reality-leg data, but it is not by itself
adjudication: memoirs are written by those with a story that
demands writing, and the peaceful continuation of an ordinary
household seldom produces one.

*What the positive data does and does not do.* Taken together, the
Anabaptist, homeschool, and Catholic-catechetical evidence
supports one modest claim: household-continuity communities
(1a-shaped) produce measurable formation strengths that the
mainstream-evangelical baseline does not consistently produce.
The evidence does *not* isolate the distinctive claim of View 1b —
that continuing directive authority as a *systematic requirement*
is the operative variable — and the paper does not present it as
if it did. Whether View 1b as such adds to or subtracts from
1a-shaped advantages is not answerable from the current empirical
literature. Honest use of the Reality leg here requires the paper
to report both the positive findings for 1a-shaped communities and
the countervailing findings within the same body of studies, and
to name the *absence* of a comparable finding for 1b as a distinct
systematic claim.
