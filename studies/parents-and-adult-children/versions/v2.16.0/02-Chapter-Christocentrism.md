# Chapter 2 — Christocentrism

## Overview (Christocentrism)

What did Jesus teach and demonstrate about the parent-child relation?
The evidence falls into four groups: Jesus's own conduct as a son;
His teaching that discipleship supersedes family loyalty; His defense
of the honor commandment against religious traditions that evaded it;
and His care for His mother from the cross.

## 2.1 Jesus at Twelve — Subject to Parents, Yet Not Only to Them

> **Luke 2:49-52** — And he said unto them, How is it that ye sought
> me? wist ye not that I must be about my Father's business? And they
> understood not the saying which he spake unto them. And he went down
> with them, and came to Nazareth, and was subject unto them: but his
> mother kept all these sayings in her heart. And Jesus increased in
> wisdom and stature, and in favour with God and man.

*Observations.* At twelve — the age of bar-mitzvah threshold in later
Judaism — Jesus asserts a higher allegiance ("my Father's business")
without denying His parents' authority ("was subject unto them"). The
sequence matters: the higher allegiance is not deployed to break the
lower one; it is affirmed *and* the ordinary subjection is preserved.
For a boy still at home, both hold.

The passage carries a further weight not always noticed. When Jesus
speaks of "*my* Father's business" (τοῖς τοῦ πατρός μου, *tois tou
patros mou*), he is not
speaking of Joseph. He is asserting, in Joseph's very hearing, that
his ultimate belonging is to *another* Father's household. Joseph is
not slighted; Joseph is not repudiated; Jesus goes down with them,
and is subject. But at twelve, the boy is already testifying that
the paternal household of formation and the paternal household of
ultimate belonging are not the same. This double-household frame — a
boy's earthly household of formation and a Son's heavenly household
of belonging — will govern everything Jesus says about family for
the rest of the Gospel record.

## 2.2 Jesus's Departure for Ministry

Jesus is called "the carpenter" (Mark 6:3) and "the carpenter's son"
(Matt. 13:55). His formation for eighteen years between Luke 2:52
and Luke 3:23 is the formation of Joseph's household — carpentry,
Torah, synagogue, obedience to father and mother. Joseph is not
mentioned during the public ministry and is presumed to have died
before it began. Jesus, as eldest son, inherited responsibility for
Mary and his siblings until his departure for public ministry.

By approximately age thirty (Luke 3:23), Jesus has left Nazareth to
itinerate publicly. The Gospels report no ceremony, no announcement,
no explicit release by Mary. He simply goes. He does not return to
live at home. His mother sometimes travels to meet him; he does not
return to be a son under her roof.

*Observations.* Whatever the biblical pattern is for a son's leaving,
Jesus's own conduct exemplifies leaving before marriage for the sake
of the Father's business. This does not itself construct a doctrine
of pre-marital independence — Jesus is unique — but it demonstrates
that leaving before marriage for kingdom cause is not sinful, not
rebellious, and not dishonoring. And it shows one further thing: the
Son who left the carpenter's household did not leave it into
autonomy. He left it *into another Father's household* — the same
Father he had confessed at twelve. Every subsequent word Jesus
speaks about houses, families, and leaving is spoken *from within*
that other household.

### 2.2.1 But the Independence Was Real

It would be dishonest to smooth over the shape of those three years.
By every ordinary daily-life metric, Jesus during his public ministry
looked *independent*.

> **Matthew 8:20 / Luke 9:58** — And Jesus saith unto him, The foxes
> have holes, and the birds of the air have nests; but the Son of
> man hath not where to lay his head.

He had no fixed home. No wife. No settled income (he was supported by
the women of Luke 8:1-3). His mother sometimes had to travel to
find him. He did not return to be a son under Mary's roof.

> **Mark 3:20-21** — And the multitude cometh together again, so
> that they could not so much as eat bread. And when his friends heard
> of it, they went out to lay hold on him: for they said, He is beside
> himself.

His own family — οἱ παρ᾽ αὐτοῦ (*hoi par' autou*), "his own" —
thought he had lost his
mind and set out to physically bring him home. This is the reality
in which Mark 3:31-35 ("Who is my mother?") should be read. The
redefinition of family in that passage is not a serene theological
observation. It is spoken in the very moment his family is trying to
retrieve him from what they took to be a breakdown.

> **John 7:5** — For neither did his brethren believe in him.

Through the ministry years, Jesus's own brothers did not believe him.
Only after the resurrection do we find them among the disciples
(Acts 1:14; James emerges as leader of the Jerusalem church;
Jude writes an epistle). The hinge of that turn is a particular
resurrection appearance: "after that, he was seen of James" (1
Cor. 15:7). Paul preserves the record of a risen appearance to
Jesus's own brother — the middle term between the unbelief of
John 7:5 and the leadership of Acts 15 — and locates the
reconciliation of the natural household with the household of
faith in the risen Christ's specific initiative toward his own
brother.

*Observations.* The daily-life independence was real. Jesus's family
relationship during ministry was strained — misunderstood by his
brothers, worried over by his mother, retrieved-for at one point as a
mad relative. The subsequent theological confession (that he was
leaving *into* the Father's household) is retrospective and
Christocentric; it is not what a Galilean neighbor watching the
carpenter's son wander off would have said in real time.

Three things must therefore be held together:

- **Yes** — by daily metrics, Jesus's ministry years show a son
  living genuinely apart from his father's household, with real
  family strain that was not resolved until after the resurrection.
- **Yes** — theologically and finally, he was under the Father, in a
  household of disciples and supporting women (Luke 8:1-3), and the
  strain was healed at the cross (John 19:26-27) and the upper room
  (Acts 1:14).
- **Also yes** — the transition itself was not a clean break. At
  the wedding at Cana (John 2:1-11) — the only Gospel scene of the
  adult Jesus responding to a maternal request before the cross —
  he honors his mother without command-obedience, timing governed
  by the Father ("mine hour is not yet come"). Immediately after,
  "he went down to Capernaum, he, and his mother, and his brethren,
  and his disciples: and they continued there not many days" (John
  2:12). The natural household still travels with him at the very
  beginning of the ministry. Departure into the Father's household
  proved compatible, in Jesus's own life, with the natural
  household still moving alongside him during the transition.

For the present question, this yields a cautious observation. When a
young adult leaves the paternal household for a genuine calling, the
appearance of independence — and even real family strain — may not
by itself be evidence of rebellion or of individualist drift. The
Father's-house pattern (§2.7 below) can look, from the outside,
much like autonomous departure. Discernment is required.

The distinguishing marks are visible over time, not in the moment:
- Continued honor of parents even when apart (John 19:26-27).
- Concrete provision when the seam is reached (John 19:27b, "and
  from that hour that disciple took her unto his own home").
- No repudiation of the biological family. Reconciliation and
  incorporation follow (Acts 1:14).
- The departure is *into* discernible discipleship, not merely
  away from home.

Where these marks are present, apparent independence can be faithful
even when it looks — to observers, and sometimes to the parents
themselves — indistinguishable from ordinary leaving.

## 2.3 "Who Is My Mother?" — The Reordering of Kinship

> **Mark 3:31-35** — There came then his brethren and his mother, and,
> standing without, sent unto him, calling him. And the multitude sat
> about him, and they said unto him, Behold, thy mother and thy
> brethren without seek for thee. And he answered them, saying, Who is
> my mother, or my brethren? And he looked round about on them which
> sat about him, and said, Behold my mother and my brethren! For
> whosoever shall do the will of God, the same is my brother, and my
> sister, and mother.

*Observations.* Jesus does not deny His mother; He redefines the
primary family. The doer of the will of God is *more truly* His
brother, sister, mother than the biological relation. This is a
seismic reordering: the household of faith is prior to the household
of blood. Every subsequent Christocentric text must be read with this
reordering in mind.

> **Luke 11:27-28** — And it came to pass, as he spake these things,
> a certain woman of the company lifted up her voice, and said unto
> him, Blessed is the womb that bare thee, and the paps which thou
> hast sucked. But he said, Yea rather, blessed are they that hear
> the word of God, and keep it.

*A second, independent attestation of the same reordering.* Luke
supplies a distinct pericope with the same shape as Mark 3:31-35:
the biological relation is not denied — the woman's blessing of
Mary's womb is not contradicted — but the obedience-relation is
ranked above it. The doer of the word is *more blessed* than the
womb that bore Christ. That the same move appears in two
independent settings, once framed by biological family members
outside and once by a stranger in the crowd, doubles the attestation
and confirms that the reordering is not a one-time saying but a
settled feature of Christ's teaching on kinship.

## 2.4 The Hard Sayings

> **Matthew 10:37** — He that loveth father or mother more than me is
> not worthy of me; and he that loveth son or daughter more than me
> is not worthy of me.

> **Luke 14:26** — If any man come to me, and hate not his father, and
> mother, and wife, and children, and brethren, and sisters, yea, and
> his own life also, he cannot be my disciple.

*Observations.* Semitic idiom uses "hate" in the sense of decisively
prefer-less. The point is comparative and total: no earthly loyalty
may compete with Christ. This is a boundary condition on every family
arrangement. A household ordered so that filial devotion (or paternal
control) rivals devotion to Christ has failed the Christocentric test.

> **Matthew 19:29** — And every one that hath forsaken houses, or
> brethren, or sisters, or father, or mother, or wife, or children,
> or lands, for my name's sake, shall receive an hundredfold, and
> shall inherit everlasting life.

> **Mark 10:29-30** — Verily I say unto you, There is no man that
> hath left house, or brethren, or sisters, or father, or mother, or
> wife, or children, or lands, for my sake, and the gospel's, But he
> shall receive an hundredfold now in this time, houses, and brethren,
> and sisters, and mothers, and children, and lands, with
> persecutions; and in the world to come eternal life.

> **Luke 18:29-30** — Verily I say unto you, There is no man that
> hath left house, or parents, or brethren, or wife, or children, for
> the kingdom of God's sake, Who shall not receive manifold more in
> this present time, and in the world to come life everlasting.

*Observations.* Christ's teaching is explicit: leaving father and
mother for the sake of His name is licit, is honored, and is
promised recompense. The recompense in Mark's version is emphatically
communal — "houses, and brethren, and sisters, and mothers, and
children" — restored *in this present time* within the household of
faith. This is the textual foundation on which the pre-Nicene
consecrated life (Ch. 3), Catholic and Orthodox monasticism, the
Anabaptist voluntary-service tradition (Ch. 4 §4.4.1), and every
form of vocational household transfer (Ch. 4 §4.6) rest. The
promise does not annul the honor commandment; it establishes that
adult leaving for Christ's sake is not merely permitted but blessed,
and that the church becomes the extended family in the receiving.

**The accusation Jesus bore.** One further datum belongs with the
hard sayings. When Jesus's opponents wished to discredit him, the
vocabulary they reached for was the rebellious-son statute's own
charge-list: "a man gluttonous, and a winebibber" (Matt. 11:19;
Luke 7:34; cf. "a glutton, and a drunkard," זוֹלֵל וְסֹבֵא,
*zolel ve-sove*, Deut. 21:20). The Son whose sayings relativize
every household bond was himself smeared as the covenant
household's worst case — the son who deserves stoning — and the
curse-text that immediately follows the statute, on the man
hanged on a tree (Deut. 21:22-23), is the text Paul applies to
his crucifixion (Gal. 3:13). The full typology — the prophets'
application of "stubborn and rebellious" to national Israel, and
the statute's trajectory from the city gate to the cross — is set
out at §3.1.2 (point 6). Its bearing here is narrow but real: the
hard sayings were spoken by one who was paying, in reputation and
then in death, the rebellious son's price — while being, on the
data of this chapter (§2.1, §2.6), the one son who perfectly
honored both his earthly household and his Father.

## 2.5 The Defense of the Honor Commandment

> **Mark 7:9-13** — And he said unto them, Full well ye reject the
> commandment of God, that ye may keep your own tradition. For Moses
> said, Honour thy father and thy mother; and, Whoso curseth father
> or mother, let him die the death: But ye say, If a man shall say to
> his father or mother, It is Corban, that is to say, a gift, by
> whatsoever thou mightest be profited by me; he shall be free. And
> ye suffer him no more to do ought for his father or his mother;
> Making the word of God of none effect through your tradition which
> ye have delivered.

*Observations.* Jesus explicitly forbids any religious device that
uses appeal to God to evade material care of parents. This is
directly relevant. An adult child cannot deploy "the Lord's call"
to abandon responsibility for aging parents. The vertical devotion
(Matt. 10:37) does not annul the horizontal duty; it re-motivates it.
(See also §1.2.2 on honor's content of material provision.)

*Second-Temple background — Sirach 3:1-16.* The most extended
ancient Jewish exposition of the fifth commandment applied to
adult children is Sirach 3, the likely background of Jesus's own
critique of the *korban* device. Ben Sira presses honor into
concrete adult obligation and — uniquely in surviving Second-Temple
literature — extends it explicitly into the parent's decline:

> **Sirach 3:12-16** (KJV Apocrypha) — My son, help thy father in
> his age, and grieve him not as long as he liveth. And if his
> understanding fail, have patience with him; and despise him not
> when thou art in thy full strength ... He that forsaketh his
> father is as a blasphemer; and he that angereth his mother is
> cursed of God.

The passage supplies three points relevant to the paper. First, it
establishes that the fifth commandment was understood in Ben Sira's
generation as governing *adult* care of the *aged* parent — the
picture in view is not the minor obeying but the strong son helping
the failing father. Second, it names the parent's cognitive decline
as the specific pastoral case where the son's honor is most
tested — "if his understanding fail, have patience with him" — a
qualification the Torah does not draw but which is deeply congenial
to Jesus's own Mark 7 sharpening of the commandment. Third, the
korban dispute in Mark 7 becomes intelligible against Sirach's
background: Jesus is defending not a novel expansion but the
standing Second-Temple reading of the commandment against a legal
device that had grown up around it. Sirach is a deuterocanonical
book — accepted as Scripture by Roman Catholic and Eastern Orthodox
communions, held as good and useful reading but not canonical
Scripture by most Protestants (Article 6 of the Thirty-Nine
Articles, Westminster Confession 1.3, and the traditional
Reformation position). This paper cites it as Second-Temple
background for the honor commandment, and reserves *Scripture-leg*
appeal to the Old and New Testament canon.

## 2.6 Care for Mary from the Cross

> **John 19:26-27** — When Jesus therefore saw his mother, and the
> disciple standing by, whom he loved, he saith unto his mother,
> Woman, behold thy son! Then saith he to the disciple, Behold thy
> mother! And from that hour that disciple took her unto his own home.

*Observations.* Jesus's last act toward his family is provisional
care for his mother. He arranges her ongoing care through the household
of faith (the beloved disciple), not through the ἀδελφοί
(*adelphoi*, "brothers") named in
Matt. 13:55 and John 7:5[^adelphoi-note] (who at that point were
not yet believers, John 7:5). This is
Christocentric practice of Mark 7 and 1 Timothy 5 in a single
gesture: honor of mother, exercised through the church.

[^adelphoi-note]: Whether these ἀδελφοί are Mary's biological sons
by Joseph (the Helvidian reading held by most Protestants), Joseph's
sons by a prior marriage (the Epiphanian reading held by Eastern
Orthodoxy), or cousins in the extended household (the Hieronymian
reading held by Roman Catholicism) is a long-contested question that
this study does not attempt to settle. The point pressing here is
independent of the identification: the persons in view were not at
that point believers, and Jesus therefore did not arrange his
mother's care through them.

## 2.7 The Father's House — Where All Household Questions Lead

The double-household frame introduced at Luke 2:49 is completed at
John 14. Every question about earthly household in this analysis
finds its final horizon here.

> **John 14:1-3** — Let not your heart be troubled: ye believe in
> God, believe also in me. In my Father's house are many mansions:
> if it were not so, I would have told you. I go to prepare a place
> for you. And if I go and prepare a place for you, I will come
> again, and receive you unto myself; that where I am, there ye may
> be also.

*Observations.* Jesus, on the night of his betrayal, gathers his
disciples' attention around a single image — *the Father's house* —
in which there are *many mansions*, into which He is preparing to
receive them. The Greek is worth registering: μοναί (*monai*,
"dwelling places" or "rooms" — a noun the New Testament uses only
here and at John 14:23, of the Father and Son making their *abode*
with the believer) pictures not primarily many separate mansions
on distinct properties, but *many rooms in one house* — the
observation carried in the title of McCaffrey's monograph on the
passage, cited below. The Father's household in the age to
come is a *shared dwelling* in which each of the redeemed has a
place.

*A reading many readers will bring: the bridegroom preparing a
place.* A widely circulated teaching hears John 14:2-3 through
Jewish marriage custom: the betrothed bridegroom returns to his
father's compound to prepare a dwelling for his bride, then comes
to fetch her. The teaching bundles two things of very different
evidential standing, and they should be separated. The
*attested* half is the patrilocal household pattern: in the
multi-generation *bet 'av*, a marrying son ordinarily brought his
bride into his father's compound, where rooms were added as the
family grew (Gen. 24:67 — Isaac brings Rebekah "into his mother
Sarah's tent"; on the compound archaeology, Lawrence Stager, "The
Archaeology of the Family in Ancient Israel," *BASOR* 260, 1985),
and marriage proceeded in two stages — binding betrothal, an
interval in which both parties prepared (*m. Ketubot* 5:2 allots
the betrothed virgin twelve months "to prepare herself," and the
same to the man), then the fetching of the bride to the groom's
home. The verb of John 14:3 — παραλήμψομαι (*paralēmpsomai*, "I
will receive [you] unto myself") — is the verb used of taking
home a wife (Matt. 1:20, 24), and John's Gospel elsewhere carries
a deliberate bridegroom motif (John 3:29; see Jocelyn McWhirter,
*The Bridegroom Messiah and the People of God*, SNTSMS 138,
Cambridge, 2006). The *unattested* half is the sermon's
load-bearing detail — that the groom's father alone inspected the
prepared chamber and set the wedding day (commonly linked to
Matt. 24:36) — which has no primary-source attestation in the
Mishnah, Talmud, or Second Temple literature and appears to be a
twentieth-century homiletic reconstruction (popularized by Zola
Levitt, *A Christian Love Story*, 1978), apparently derived from
Matt. 24:36 rather than from attested custom. Johannine scholarship divides
three ways on the primary background of the passage: the temple
("my Father's house" at John 2:16; James McCaffrey, *The House
With Many Rooms: The Temple Theme of Jn 14,2-3*, Analecta Biblica
114, 1988), permanent family belonging (John 8:35 — "the servant
abideth not in the house for ever: but the Son abideth ever" —
with the μοναί of 14:2 echoed at 14:23, where Father and Son make
their μονή with the believer), and the bridal resonance just
described. On any of the three, the argument of this section
stands unchanged; on the bridal reading, the Genesis 2:24 seam
gains a further resonance — in the patrilocal world the "leaving"
of marriage was never departure into the bounded self but
transfer into another father's house, which is the meta-household
point below.

Every household question of the present analysis — where the adult
unmarried child lives, under whose authority, when he leaves, when
he stays, whether the church becomes his family — is answered
proleptically in this eschatological household. The direction of
all earthly household ordering is toward *the Father's house*, and
the church in this age is the beginning of that household, not a
competing one.

The New Testament makes this connection explicit.

> **Ephesians 2:19** — Now therefore ye are no more strangers and
> foreigners, but fellowcitizens with the saints, and of the household
> of God (οἰκεῖοι τοῦ Θεοῦ, *oikeioi tou Theou*).

> **Hebrews 3:6** — But Christ as a son over his own house; whose
> house are we, if we hold fast the confidence and the rejoicing of
> the hope firm unto the end.

> **1 Peter 4:17** — For the time is come that judgment must begin
> at the house of God.

*Observations.* The church is not merely *like* a household; she is
called ὁ οἶκος τοῦ Θεοῦ (*ho oikos tou Theou*) — *the* household of
God, Christ as Son
over that house, ourselves as the house. The kinship texture of
Chapter 1 §1.6 is not sentimental; it is ontological. The believer
who leaves his father's carpenter shop for Christ leaves *into*
this house, not out of a house into an open field.

*Bearing on the present question.* Jesus's teaching brings three
things together that no single earthly view of adult children and
parents can hold apart.

- **Formation.** The earthly household (Joseph's carpenter shop,
  the father's or mother's roof, the parental instruction of
  Proverbs) is the ordained sphere of formation until the son or
  daughter is ready to leave.
- **Belonging.** The Father's house is the ultimate belonging of the
  believer, present already in the household of God (the church),
  consummated in "In my Father's house are many mansions."
- **Transition.** Whether the departure is into marriage (Gen. 2:24),
  into consecrated singleness (Matt. 19:12), into vocational
  household transfer (Ch. 1 §1.4.6), into military service
  (Ch. 1 §1.4.8), or into service of the covenanted community
  (Ch. 1 §1.6.3) — the transition is ordinarily *out of* one
  household *into* another, not into isolation — even the
  wilderness callings
  (John the Baptist, "in the deserts till the day of his shewing,"
  Luke 1:80; the desert tradition, §5.0.3) are received as into the
  Father's own
  household rather than into the bounded self. The Father's house is the
  meta-household, and the earthly seams (parental → military,
  parental → marriage, parental → church-family) are subordinate to
  it. (The household-embedded readings press this pattern hardest;
  View 2a records the dissent — launched adulthood under Christ, on
  its account, requires no earthly receiving household to be
  faithful, §4.2a. The paper does not adjudicate between them.)

This frame is not supplied by secular developmental accounts of
the transition, which treat departure as a movement from the
parental household into the bounded self. On the pattern the
biblical exemplars trace (§1.3.1, §1.4.6-1.4.8), the young adult
leaves one household for another —
military, apprenticeship, vocation, marriage, or the ecclesial
family — at an appointed seam, on the way home to the
Father's house.

## 2.8 Jesus's Priorities Extracted

Assembling the evidence:

1. **The Father's house is the horizon.** Every household question
   is finally answered in "In my Father's house are many mansions"
   (John 14:2; Eph. 2:19; Heb. 3:6; 1 Pet. 4:17). The church in this
   age is the beginning of that house.
2. **The kingdom is first.** No earthly bond, including the deepest
   filial bond, may rival allegiance to Christ (Matt. 10:37; Luke
   14:26).
3. **The kinship of faith is primary.** The household of God reorders,
   without destroying, the household of blood (Mark 3:31-35).
4. **Honor of parents is inviolable.** No devotion or tradition may
   nullify it (Mark 7:9-13).
5. **Care for parents is concrete.** It is arranged, materially, even
   at the moment of dying (John 19:26-27).
6. **Adult leaving for kingdom cause is licit — and is ordinarily
   *into* another household, not into isolation.** Jesus's own life
   demonstrates it (Luke 2:49; Luke 3:23; Mark 3:31-35;
   Matt. 19:29).
7. **Sonship subject to parents is preserved as long as the child is
   under their roof.** Luke 2:51.
8. **The adult Son's obedience is formed Godward through suffering,
   not through household subordination.** "Though he were a Son, yet
   learned he obedience by the things which he suffered" (Heb. 5:8).
   The New Testament's clearest apostolic depiction of an adult Son
   *learning obedience* locates that learning in the Son's
   suffering relation to the Father, not in continuing subordination
   to a human parent. Whatever cases the four views make for the
   unmarried adult child, the christological datum is that Christ's
   own mature ὑπακοή was filial submission to the Father, formed
   through suffering.

These eight data points together sketch the Christocentric material.
Each of the four Chapter 4 views reads them differently — Views 1a/1b
weighting #7 (subject-to-parents-while-under-roof), 1b as the
ordinary directive mode and 1a as the commended household pattern
for the unmarried adult; Views 2a and 2b weighting #6 (adult
leaving for kingdom cause) and #8 (mature obedience formed Godward)
as the ordinary pattern for the disciple. Chapter 2 sets the data
out; Chapter 4 develops the differing readings.

## 2.9 What the Christocentric Data Does Not Settle

- Jesus was celibate and never faced the specific question of the
  unmarried adult daughter under the father's roof.
- Jesus did not explicitly teach on Numbers 30.
- Jesus did not draw an explicit line between obedience (proper to
  minors) and honor (proper to adults). Views 2a and 2b read His
  own life and His use of the honor commandment as together implying
  the distinction; View 1a reads the same materials as compatible
  with the honor-and-consultation structure its commended household
  sustains, and View 1b as compatible with a
  directive-following mode continuing to marriage. Chapter 2 does
  not adjudicate.

These silences are important; they keep the door open for multiple
faithful applications.
