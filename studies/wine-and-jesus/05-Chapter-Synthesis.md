# Chapter 5: Synthesis — Where the Evidence Points, and How to Live It

## Overview

The four SCAR components have been examined. What emerges when they are brought together?

Chapter 1 walked through Scripture in seven categories of evidence. The pattern was consistent across the canon: wine appears as gift and blessing (Ps 104; Deut 14:26; Isa 25:6); wine is present at feast and covenant, from Melchizedek to Cana to the Last Supper; drunkenness is universally condemned; abstention is honored as a personal vocation, never as universal law; the vocabulary does not cleanly distinguish fermented from unfermented substance; and several passages remain pastorally ambiguous (1 Tim 3, Rom 14) in ways that leave room for prudential judgment.

Chapter 2 turned to Jesus. Cana presupposes fermented wine (the master-of-the-feast comment requires it). Jesus's self-report at Matthew 11:19 states it (the *oinopotēs* accusation has no purchase without actual wine-drinking). The Last Supper takes it for granted (Passover cups were fermented wine in every rabbinic and biblical witness). Jesus taught nothing prohibitive about wine as substance; his teaching on drunkenness tracked the general biblical pattern; and his priorities — the love of God and neighbor, the unity of his body — sit above every substance-question by an order of magnitude.

Chapter 3 turned to the pre-Nicene church. The received evidence is remarkably uniform: every source the church received as authoritative that names the eucharistic cup names it as wine — the Didache, Ignatius, Justin, Irenaeus, Cyprian. The one recorded departure (the *Aquarii* of third-century North Africa) was corrected by their own metropolitan, Cyprian of Carthage, on the ground that Christ instituted the cup with wine and departing from wine is departing from Christ. Clement of Alexandria's extensive pastoral treatment permits moderate wine to all except the young and the intemperate; ascetic wine-abstention is honored as a personal vocation; the Encratite movement that made wine-abstention a universal Christian rule was condemned as heretical.

Chapter 4 turned to the modern history. The two-wine theory — the reading that Scripture's approved "wine" is unfermented and its condemned "wine" is fermented — is a nineteenth-century American Protestant construction. It emerged in the temperance movement, was popularized by Nott, Patton, and Lees & Burns, and consolidated in Bacchiocchi's 1989 *Wine in the Bible*. Its lexical foundation was set aside by the refereed literature — no sustained defense has appeared there since roughly 1980. Its practical companion — grape-juice communion — spread through American Protestantism after 1869 through a combination of Welch's technology, the 1876 WCTU pressure, and the 1880 Methodist Discipline; it never took root in the sacramental traditions that anchor communion substance to Christ's institution.

Chapter 4.5 interrupted the argument to present the prohibitionist case at its strongest — twelve arguments the Independent Fundamental Baptist, Wesleyan Holiness, and Seventh-day Adventist traditions have advanced from Scripture, from Christology, and from pastoral testimony. That chapter did not respond; it presented. This chapter responds.

The four components converge; the prohibitionist case has been heard; this chapter draws the lines together.

---

## 1. Areas of agreement across the SCAR components

**Jesus drank fermented wine.** This is affirmed by Scripture (the Last Supper narratives; the Matthew 11:19 self-report), by the Christocentric priority (his participation in ordinary Jewish drinking culture is a fact of the record, not an inference), by antiquity (every pre-Nicene source assumes it), and by the academic consensus on ancient Near Eastern viticulture (the technology and economy of first-century Palestine made fermented wine the ordinary table drink). No SCAR component supports the reading that Jesus abstained from alcoholic wine, and no SCAR component supports the reading that his miracle at Cana produced an unfermented beverage.

**Drunkenness is prohibited.** This is universal across the four components. Scripture warns against it repeatedly (Prov 20:1; 23:29-35; Isa 5:11; Eph 5:18; Gal 5:21). Jesus warned against it (Luke 21:34). The pre-Nicene fathers preached against it (Clement, Tertullian, Cyprian). Every modern Christian tradition retains the prohibition, whatever their communion practice. There is no live Christian teaching that condones drunkenness.

**Wine as a substance is not itself sinful.** This is the fourth component all four SCAR legs support, though the two-wine theory contests it. Scripture treats wine as gift (Ps 104:15), covenant sign (Melchizedek, Passover), and eschatological image (Isa 25:6; Matt 26:29). Jesus made it, drank it, and blessed it at the meal that founded the Christian eucharist. The pre-Nicene church put it on every communion table it commemorated. The academic case for the substance-verdict is decisive: fermented wine was the biblical writers' normal referent for *yayin* and *oinos*, and unfermented juice was a marginal preservation category in the ancient world, not the default substance-meaning of the vocabulary.

**Abstention is honored as a vocation.** The Nazirite (Num 6), John the Baptist (Luke 1:15), Clement of Alexandria's admiration for the ascetic life, and modern monastic and personal-conviction abstainers all fall within this pattern. Scripture never universalizes the vocation; the pre-Nicene church honored it as personal calling; and modern Christian traditions from Catholic monastic orders to Adventist health teaching preserve it as valid personal witness.

## 2. Response to the prohibitionist case

Chapter 4.5 presented the twelve strongest prohibitionist arguments at their fullest. The synthesis here responds to each, in the same order, without dismissing the concerns each argument protects.

**1. The christological objection to Cana (Prov 23:31).**

*1a. Proverbs 23:31 in its own context.* The reading of Proverbs 23:31 as a universal command against ever looking upon fermented wine cannot be sustained by the surrounding text. Proverbs 23:29-35 as a whole is a Wisdom warning against *tarrying long* over wine and *going to seek* it — the shape of habitual excess. "Look not thou upon the wine when it is red" is diagnostic metonymy, not universal prohibition; the passage's own next line concerns the drinker who will "seek it yet again" (v. 35), the voice of the addict. If v. 31 were a universal ban on seeing red wine, it would also ban the Passover cup (customarily red wine, per later Jewish practice), the daily libation of *shekar* in the sanctuary (Num 28:7 — the prohibitionist re-reading of *shekar* as unfermented syrup is answered at 2d below), and every wine-blessing passage the same Wisdom tradition contains. The Christological argument then fails: Jesus at Cana is not violating Proverbs 23:31 as understood by the same Wisdom tradition that gave Proverbs; the master-of-the-feast's remark ("thou hast kept the good wine until now" — John 2:10) presupposes fermented wine on both sides of the customary comparison.

*1b. The kalos/agathos refinement.* Bacchiocchi's lexical refinement — in his own words, "This is supported by the very adjective used to describe it, namely *kalos*, which denotes that which is morally excellent, instead of *agathos*, which means simply good" — does not survive examination of Koine usage. The two adjectives overlap heavily in the first century (BDAG glosses *kalos* for objects as "fine, choice, in good condition"), and the master of the feast's whole speech is about *taste order at a feast*, not moral assessment: hosts serve the *kalos* wine first "and when men have well drunk, then that which is worse." "Worse" (*elassō*, inferior) is a quality-scale term; its opposite in the sentence is quality-*kalos*, not virtue-*kalos*. Reading a moral adjective into a sommelier's remark is the kind of wordbook exegesis James Barr's *Semantics of Biblical Language* (1961) dismantled.

*1c. The methyskō usage survey.* Nor does the "drink freely" rendering of *methysthōsin* rescue the argument — and here the Greek literary record can be consulted directly. Six observations, the fourth and fifth from the classical corpus and its best first-century Jewish witness. First, the usage survey: the KJV renders every other New Testament occurrence of the *methyskō/methyō* family with "drunk", "drunken", or "drunkard" (Matt 24:49; Luke 12:45; Acts 2:15; 1 Cor 11:21; Eph 5:18; 1 Thess 5:7; Rev 17:2, 6; cognate noun *methysos*, "drunkard": 1 Cor 5:11; 6:10) — John 2:10 is the sole softening, and at Revelation 17:2 the same verb in the same tense and voice (aorist passive; subjunctive at John 2:10, indicative there) is rendered "made drunk". Second, Bacchiocchi's own authority concedes the point: the Preisker sentence he quotes begins "*methuo* and *methuskomai* are mostly used literally in the NT for 'to be drunk' and 'to get drunk'" — Preisker's observation about John 2:10 is that the verb carries *no ethical judgment* there, which is a claim about the narrator's moral commentary, not about the guests' sobriety. Third, the Parkhurst counter-examples backfire — both of them: at Genesis 43:34 the underlying Hebrew is *shakar* — the drunkenness root, the same root as *shekar*, "strong drink" — so the LXX's *emethysthēsan* there is translating a Hebrew verb whose lexical meaning is intoxication, not disproving it; and Parkhurst's second exhibit, Song of Solomon 5:1 ("drink, yea, drink abundantly, O beloved"), sits on the very same root — the KJV's "drink abundantly" renders the Hebrew imperative *shikru*, "be intoxicated" — so both of the lexicon's proof-texts for a sober sense of *methyskō* are translations of the Bible's own drunkenness verb. (Parkhurst's lexicon is an eighteenth-century work much beloved of the temperance commentators precisely for this gloss.) Fourth, the classical corpus itself. Across Greek literature the family names the intoxication end of a sobriety scale: Theognis twice opposes μεθύων to νήφων, "drunken" to "sober" (Thgn. 478, 627, per LSJ); Heraclitus's drunk man "is led by a beardless boy, stumbling, not knowing where he steps" (fr. 117); Herodotus's Persians deliberate their gravest matters μεθυσκόμενοι and reconsider them sober (1.133); Plato's Poros passes out in Zeus's garden "drunk with nectar" (*Symp.* 203b). The verb's extended senses are saturation metaphors — Homer's ox-hide "drunk with oil" (*Il.* 17.390), a city "drunk with freedom" (*Rep.* 562d) — and LSJ's "be filled with food" extension (Hos 14:8 LXX, of *grain*) belongs to that same saturation family, not to a sober-wine-drinking sense. The decisive classical datum is the exchange at Xenophon, *Cyropaedia* 1.3.11, where Astyages asks of Cyrus's father, πίνων οὐ μεθύσκεται; — "when he drinks, does he not get drunk?" (Miller, Loeb) — and the boy answers that Cambyses merely quenches his thirst and takes no harm. The question is only askable, and the answer only meaningful, because the verb names the *resulting state*, not the act of ample drinking. If *methyskomai* meant merely "drink one's fill", the whole exchange would be incoherent.

Fifth, Philo of Alexandria — a Greek-speaking Jew of the New Testament's own century, who devoted a treatise section to precisely this verb (*De Plantatione* 140-166). Philo reports that "to get drunk is a matter of a twofold nature, one part of it being equivalent to being overcome with wine; the other, to behaving foolishly in one's cups" (§142, Yonge) — and then states expressly of *oinousthai* and *methyein*: "**both these words intimate a taking of too much wine**" (§155, emphasis added). Philo's milder sense is being mellow with wine, not drinking innocuously; his wise man "when he is intoxicated, becomes more good-humoured than when he is sober" (§166). Neither of the two senses the first century knew will carry unfermented juice.

Sixth, and decisively: even granting the generic-remark point and the softest possible rendering, the master's punchline still requires wine that dulls discrimination. "Serve the good wine first, the worse after the guests have drunk freely" is only a *rule of banqueting* if what they drink freely blunts their ability to tell good from worse. Grape juice in any quantity leaves the palate sober. The stock-in-trade of the hired banquet master presupposes fermented wine — whichever particular party he is describing.

*1d. The slander-reading of Matthew 11:19.* The companion argument — "winebibber" is enemy slander, so the verse proves nothing about Jesus's practice — fails on the structure of the saying itself. Hostile witnesses exaggerate, but exaggeration needs a substrate: the crowd's charge against John ("he hath a devil") attached to his *observable* desert asceticism, and the charge against Jesus ("gluttonous, a winebibber, a friend of publicans and sinners") attached to his *observable* practice of eating and drinking at shared tables — the friendship with publicans, at least, being a charge the Gospels confirm as sober fact. A drinking-charge manufactured against a visible abstainer, standing in explicit contrast to an actual Nazirite, would have carried no rhetorical purchase; the saying's whole antithesis ("John came neither eating bread nor drinking wine... The Son of man is come eating and drinking" — Luke 7:33-34) is built on a real difference in practice, and Luke's wording names the difference: *wine*. Bacchiocchi's social-lifestyle idiom concedes the substance of the point — the tables Jesus shared were tables where wine was the drink, which is why the "eating and drinking" idiom could carry the "winebibber" barb at all. The Deuteronomy 21:20 formula-reading — the slander-reading's strongest form — does not escape this logic; it sharpens it. Grant that "a gluttonous man, and a winebibber" invokes the rebellious-son formula: even a stock formula is *selected for fit*. The crowd had two ready charges and matched each to the visible pattern — the demoniac-charge for the desert ascetic, the drunkard-formula for the man at table with publicans; a capital-offense formula hurled at a visible abstainer would have ridiculed the accusers, not the accused. And the Greek-Bible carrier of the pairing cuts the same way: Proverbs 23:20-21 — where the *sove/zolel* roots enter the LXX as οἰνοπότης — is a Wisdom warning aimed at *actual observed drinkers* ("Be not among winebibbers"), so the idiom's own biblical home presupposes the conduct it names. And the formula-reading can reinterpret only the crowd's words, not the Lord's: the antithesis — "John came neither eating bread nor drinking wine… The Son of man is come eating and drinking" — is *Jesus's own framing* of the difference between the two ministries, and Luke's wording names wine as the term of contrast. A formula cannot be read back into Jesus's own sentence. And the not-all-kinds analogy (Jesus drank, but not necessarily *fermented* wine, just as he ate, but not unclean food) assumes what it must prove: clean and unclean foods were a revealed, named, legislated distinction; the fermented/unfermented wine distinction is precisely the category the two-wine theory has failed to establish (response 2). The slander-reading and the sect argument (response 11) are consistent with each other; what they share is a single fatal exposure: each must explain away not the crowd's words but Jesus's own antithesis with John, which presupposes a real and visible difference in practice — a difference Luke names as wine.

**2. The lexical case for two wines.**

*2a. The lexical core.* The four pillars of the two-wine theory are addressed in Chapter 4. The decisive counter-evidence is Hosea 4:11, which pairs *yayin* (ordinary wine) and *tirosh* (new wine) in the same warning as substances that both "take away the heart". The LXX translators — working two centuries before Christ, in Alexandria, with the same Hebrew text — rendered both *yayin* and *tirosh* as *oinos* in the majority of occurrences. The vocabulary distinction the theory requires was not drawn by the ancient Hebrew or Greek readers. The archaeological consensus (Chapter 4 §5) confirms that fermented wine was the overwhelming default of the ancient Mediterranean world; unfermented preserved substances existed but were a marginal category.

*2b. The "wine is a mocker" grammar.* As for the grammatical exhibit of Proverbs 20:1 — "wine is a mocker" as personified agency of the substance itself — personification is the Wisdom literature's ordinary poetic instrument (wisdom cries in the streets, wealth makes itself wings, bread eaten in secret is pleasant), and the verse's own second colon assigns the failure to the person: "whosoever is *deceived thereby* is not wise." The mocking is what wine does *to the deceived*, not a property that condemns the substance in the hands of the wise; the same book sets Proverbs 31:6-7 and Wisdom's own banquet — "drink of the wine which I have mingled" (Prov 9:5) — beside the warning without contradiction.

*2c. Bacchiocchi's Hosea 4:11 defense.* The defense deserves its own answer, since his *tirosh* survey is genuine philology. Three points. First, his own supporting material concedes the case for later usage: the Talmud passage he himself quotes — "he who indulges in wine becomes poor" — is the b. Sanhedrin 70a wordplay on *tirosh* (verified verbatim in Appendix C), where the rabbis read *tirosh* as a potable, intoxicating drink and draw the line between "a moderate amount" and "excessively". Second, the ancient translators he cites testify against him: the LXX and Vulgate render *tirosh* at Hosea 4:11 as *methysma* and *ebrietas* — "intoxicant", "drunkenness" — which is how the verse's earliest readers took it. Third, his concessive fork ("the verse would indict wine irrespective of quantity") proves too much: by identical logic, Psalm 104:15 — "wine that maketh glad the heart [*lebab*] of man" — commends wine irrespective of quantity, and the two verses speak of the same organ. Either both texts are quantity-indifferent absolutes (and Scripture contradicts itself), or both describe what wine characteristically does in use and abuse respectively — which is the moderationist reading. The apostasy-context point may readily be granted: the triad names things that capture Israel's devotion. But wine and *tirosh* capture the heart *through what they are*; the verse's diagnostic power presupposes the substance's potency.

*2d. The shekar-as-saccharine etymology.* The etymology fails on the philology the study has already assembled. *Shekar* is cognate with Akkadian *šikaru*, the ordinary Mesopotamian word for barley beer — a fermented drink — not with any sugar-word; the LXX transliterated it as *sikera* rather than translating it precisely because it named a class of fermented drinks with no one-word Greek equivalent; Luke 1:15 pairs *sikera* with *oinos* in the Nazirite formula, where abstention from a sweet syrup would be no consecration at all; and the Hebrew verb from the same root, *shakar*, means "to be drunken" in every lexicon (it is the verb of Gen 43:34, as shown above, and of Song 5:1; see Appendix A). The nineteenth-century sugar etymology was philology in the service of a conclusion.

*2e. The Ephesians 5:18 "wherein" parse.* The reading that locates the *asōtia* in the wine itself ("be not drunk with wine, wherein is excess" — ἐν ᾧ read with οἴνῳ as antecedent) is grammatically possible and exegetically wrong, for three reasons. First, the antecedent: Greek regularly uses ἐν ᾧ with a whole *clause* as antecedent, and that is how the commentators and the modern translations take it here — the dissoluteness is in the *getting drunk* (μὴ μεθύσκεσθε οἴνῳ), not in the vineyard's produce; hence the standard renderings "which leads to debauchery" (NIV) and "for that is debauchery" (ESV) — the relative and the demonstrative alike taking the clause, not the noun — rather than "in which [wine] debauchery resides". Second, the parallel: Paul's sentence opposes two *states* — "be not drunk… but be filled with the Spirit" — a contrast that works between drunkenness and Spirit-fullness, and collapses if one side is a substance and the other a state. The Spirit is not the counter-substance to wine; being filled is the counter-condition to being drunk. Third, the word: *asōtia* elsewhere in the New Testament names a manner of living, not a chemical property — the "riot" of the prodigal's country (Luke 15:13, adverb), the "riot" the elder's children must not be accused of (Titus 1:6), the "excess of riot" the pagans run to (1 Pet 4:4). A lifestyle-word takes a conduct-antecedent. If Paul had believed the dissoluteness resided in the substance, the counsel of 1 Timothy 5:23 — "use a little wine" — would have him prescribing bottled vice to his own delegate.

*2f. The dilution argument.* This argument deserves the most respectful answer of all, because it is the one abstentionist argument that concedes everything this study has established — and the concession should be received as such: on Stein's and Geisler's ground, Jesus drank fermented wine, Scripture blesses it, and the substance-question is settled. What remains is a *prudential* argument about modern strength, and as prudence it has real merit. But it cannot carry a prohibition, for four reasons. First, wine as actually drunk in each era demonstrably intoxicated. In the Greco-Roman and patristic world of water-diluted wine, drunkenness was drunkenness on the *mixed* krater. The whole sympotic literature is a literature of men getting drunk on diluted wine; the refereed survey of the practice is Everett Ferguson, "Wine as a Table-Drink in the Ancient World," *Restoration Quarterly* 13 (1970), whose findings carry particular weight here because Ferguson is a patristics scholar of the Churches of Christ — a juice-communion tradition with no moderationist stake. The Corinthians got drunk at the Lord's own table (1 Cor 11:21) in a milieu where Justin shows the mixed cup as the Christian norm; the rabbinic Passover cups were themselves mixed (the Mishnah's debates concern the mixing ratio); and Cyprian classifies the mixed eucharistic cup as the kind of substance that "inebriates" ("water cannot inebriate anybody"). In the Old Testament world, drunkenness came by unmixed or spiced wine — Hebrew "mixing" ran the opposite direction from Greek: the "mixed wine" (*mimsak*) of Proverbs 23:30 is wine fortified with spices to *heighten* potency, where Greco-Roman mixing diluted with water to lessen it, and Isaiah 1:22 treats *watering* wine as an image of degradation. Noah, Lot, and Nabal fell by that stronger substance while the same substance-vocabulary carried Psalm 104's blessing; which yields a further point in the study's favor: if the Old Testament's ordinary wine was, if anything, *undiluted*, then the undiluted category itself sat on the biblical table without categorical prohibition — Deuteronomy 14:26 puts even *shekar* on the covenant table for human drinking, and Numbers 28:7, though it describes an altar libation rather than a beverage, classifies the same undiluted substance as fit for the LORD's own offering. In neither era does strength move a substance between moral categories that Scripture assigns by use. Second, the arithmetic cuts less deeply than it appears: wine at 2:1 dilution still runs roughly four to five percent — beer-strength, not tonic — and even at the steepest ratios the abstentionist literature cites from Athenaeus's catalog (3:1 and higher), the residue remained intoxicating in the ancients' own estimation: the catalog sits inside book 10 of the *Deipnosophistae*, Athenaeus's great parade of drunkards; it includes half-and-half among the customary mixes; Plutarch's explanation of the five-three-four proverb itself names three-to-two and two-to-one as the drinker's mixes (*Quaest. conv.* 3.9) — the strong end of the range, not the weak; Zaleucus still found the unmixed cup worth a law; and Cyprian's "water cannot inebriate anybody" presupposes that the mixed cup can. Nor was the undiluted category forbidden: the *shekar* of Deuteronomy 14:26 was bought with tithe silver and drunk at the covenant feast. The moral category is use versus abuse, not percentage. Third, the argument proves moderation, not abstention: if the concern is potency, the remedy the argument itself suggests is the ancient one — dilute, or drink less, or choose lower-strength — which is moderation indexed to strength, exactly the discipline Clement of Alexandria taught ("it is best to mix the wine with as much water as possible"). The ancients themselves indexed dilution to potency: Homer's Maron gave Odysseus a wine so strong that "when he drank it he mixed twenty parts of water to one of wine" (*Od.* 9, Butler) — and when Odysseus served that same wine *unmixed* to the Cyclops, it laid the monster out. Strong wine mixed generously was civilization; strong wine taken neat was the undoing of Polyphemus. A Christian who waters his wine stands in the oldest tradition there is; a rule that he must not touch it does not follow from the ratio. Fourth, the pastoral conclusion the argument actually licenses — take modern strength seriously, drink accordingly — is one this study affirms without reservation in section 6 below.

*2g. The "unanimity of the winners" historical argument.* The historical companion deserves a more respectful answer, and receives one. The minority streams are real: the Aquarii, the Encratites, the Ebionites, the ascetic meals McGowan catalogues. The study's claim is therefore stated precisely: every pre-Nicene source *the church received as authoritative* that names the cup names it as wine. What the minority streams cannot supply is what the abstentionist needs: none of them abstained on the two-wine theory's ground. The Encratites rejected wine as intrinsically unclean (with marriage and flesh — a cosmological asceticism, condemned on those grounds); the Aquarii feared the morning smell of wine in persecution conditions, on Cyprian's own account; the Ebionites' water rite is reported only in Epiphanius's fourth-century account, of a movement whose second-century attestation already includes rejection of Paul and of the virgin birth. A minority that abstains for reasons the abstentionist himself rejects is not a suppressed witness to his reading. And the steel-man's own sources confirm the shape: the streams McGowan catalogues are ascetic-meal traditions bound up with the rejection of marriage and meat as much as wine — Encratite and apocryphal-Acts circles whose whole cuisine of renunciation the modern abstentionist does not adopt — and they were contested by their contemporaries in the second and third centuries, which is precisely the situation Cyprian's letter presupposes. Finally, the "winners" in question include the earliest datable evidence itself — Paul's Corinthians drunk at the supper in the AD 50s, before any canon-forming power existed to do the winnowing.

*2h. The wineskins inversion.* Bacchiocchi's wineskins inversion (Chapter 4.5 §2) fails on the parable's own words. Jesus states the mechanism explicitly: "Neither do men put new wine into old bottles: *else the bottles break*" (Matt 9:17). On Jesus's own description, the substance generates pressure — that is what would burst the old skins. If the "new wine" were boiled or filtered must that would never ferment, *no* skin would burst, old skins would serve perfectly well, and the parable's entire contrast collapses; there would be nothing for the new skins to accommodate. New skins were chosen precisely because fresh leather stretches with slow continuing fermentation where brittle old leather splits. Bacchiocchi's empirical premise (that fermentation gas would burst even new skins) requires the primary, violent stage of fermentation — but wine was racked into skins after primary fermentation in vats, when only slow secondary fermentation remained. And Luke's appended saying — "No man also having drunk old wine straightway desireth new: for he saith, The old is better" (Luke 5:39) — compares aged wine favorably to young wine, a comparison meaningless for grape juice, which does not improve with age; it spoils.

**3. Habakkuk 2:15 and the anti-serving reading.** The verse's own grammar targets the host who "makest him drunken also, *that thou mayest look on their nakedness*" — an exploitative purpose, not the incidental risk of intoxication in ordinary hospitality. The broader reading (any serving of alcohol risks drunkenness) is a legitimate *pastoral* concern that Romans 14 addresses, but Habakkuk 2:15 does not itself teach universal abstention. And Scripture directly authorizes serving both wine and strong drink to one's household in the covenant feast: "Thou shalt bestow that money for whatsoever thy soul lusteth after … for wine, or for strong drink … and thou shalt eat there before the LORD thy God, and thou shalt rejoice, thou, and thine household" (Deut 14:26). The Torah does not read Habakkuk 2:15 against itself.

The companion — the universalized Romans 14:21 — must be answered with equal care, because everything it quotes is Scripture; what it adds is a premise Scripture does not supply. The verse is conditional by its own grammar: it is good not to drink wine *"whereby thy brother stumbleth"* — the goodness attaches to the situation of actual stumbling, and Paul's surrounding argument keeps it situational ("Hast thou faith? have it to thyself before God" — Rom 14:22; "destroy not him with thy meat, for whom Christ died" — 14:15, a *particular* him). The universalizing premise — someone somewhere can always see you, so the condition is always met — proves more than its users hold: the same verse, in the same breath, makes it equally "good neither to eat flesh," so the universalized reading mandates vegetarianism by identical logic. The Independent Baptist and Southern Baptist deployments of the verse take the wine half and quietly drop the flesh half; the Seventh-day Adventist tradition comes closest to consistency — its health-reform counsel embraces the meat conclusion as well, though on stewardship grounds (§8) rather than from Romans 14, and as encouraged counsel rather than membership rule — which shows what consistency would cost. And Paul himself refused the universalization in the one case we can check: the man who wrote Romans 14:21 also wrote "use a little wine for thy stomach's sake" to a young minister whose abstention was publicly visible (1 Tim 5:23) — counsel impossible if visibility alone made every drink a stumbling-block. Voluntary abstention for a brother's sake remains a beautiful exercise of liberty (this study's §6 commends it); a standing rule built on a hypothetical observer is the lever-inversion this chapter's §3 warns against.

**4. 1 Thessalonians 5:22 (appearance of evil).** The Greek phrase *apo pantos eidous ponērou apechesthe* is variously translated. Modern translations render "every form of evil" or "every kind of evil"; *eidos* here names species or kind (so the modern versions); even where the word denotes visible form, the sense is "every visible form of [actual] evil," not "whatever might appear evil to an observer." The verse commands abstention from actual evil in its various forms. Its context (1 Thess 5:14-22) is a list of general spiritual disciplines — hold fast the good, prove all things, avoid evil — not a witness-management principle. The reading that generates universal wine-abstention from this verse is a translation-and-application move, not a straightforward reading of Paul's own instruction.

**5. Ezekiel 44:21 and universal priesthood.** The Ezekiel restriction is *task-specific*: priests, on duty, in the inner court, must not drink wine. The universal-priesthood argument extends this to all Christians at all times. But the same priests who could not drink in the inner court poured out *shekar* as libation before the LORD in the tabernacle service (Num 28:7). Their off-duty life included wine like any Israelite's; their vocational discipline attached only to the moment of sanctuary judgment. The New Testament's universal priesthood (1 Pet 2:9) is a spiritual mediation-language, not a literal claim that every Christian is permanently on cultic duty. The task-specific structure of the Old Testament ban does not universalize; the abstention pattern remains *vocational*, honored where it appears (Chapter 1 §5).

The royal twin fails the same way. Lemuel's mother forbids wine to the king *in the seat of judgment* — "lest they drink, and forget the law, and pervert the judgment of any of the afflicted" (Prov 31:5) — a task-specific discipline exactly parallel to the priestly one; and the very same oracle continues, two verses later, "Give strong drink unto him that is ready to perish, and wine unto those that be of heavy hearts" (Prov 31:6). The one text the kings-and-priests syllogism draws on distinguishes *who* and *when*, not *whether*. Israel's actual kings drank wine without censure outside drunkenness — David's own royal administration kept a standing officer "over the increase of the vineyards for the wine cellars" (Zabdi the Shiphmite, 1 Chr 27:27), and Ziba brought David's fleeing household "a bottle of wine … that such as be faint in the wilderness may drink" (2 Sam 16:1-2); and the "kings and priests" of Revelation 1:6 is doxological identity-language — the same book pictures the redeemed at a wedding supper (Rev 19:9), not on a perpetual duty-roster. If the double office prohibited wine, it would equally prohibit marriage and sleep to any Christian on the ground that on-duty priests kept vigils; offices confer callings, not the permanent activation of every duty-specific rule.

**6. Deuteronomy 21:20 and severity.** The composite portrait Deuteronomy 21:20 addresses — *stubborn and rebellious, will not obey, glutton and drunkard* — is a case of settled character over sustained time. The passage does not attach the death penalty to any drinking; it attaches the penalty to the person who has become a drunkard as a marker of his rebellion. The severity of God's assessment of drunkenness is real. It does not extend to God's assessment of moderate drinking; if it did, Melchizedek's bread and wine, Jesus's participation in the wedding at Cana, and Paul's counsel to Timothy would all fall under the same severity. The passage teaches the seriousness of *settled drunkenness as covenantal rebellion*, not the moral status of moderate use.

**7. Nazirite and Rechabite universalized.** This is the Encratite argument. The pre-Nicene church encountered it directly in the second century and rejected it as heresy (Chapter 3 §5). What God commends in the Rechabites is their obedience to their ancestor (Jer 35:14, 18) — the substance of the commendation is *filial fidelity as a rebuke to Israel's covenantal faithlessness*, not the specific content of the abstention. If the substance of the commendation were the abstention itself, the rebuke would misfire: Israel had never been commanded by God to abstain, so their fault could not be failure to keep an abstention-rule. The rebuke only works as an a-fortiori about obedience — the Rechabites kept a mere ancestor's word; Israel would not keep God's. The Rechabites' distinctive is the vertical transmission of their ancestor's teaching, not its horizontal content. And John the Baptist is described in Luke 1:15 with the Nazirite formula precisely because his abstention is *distinctive*, a marker of his prophetic vocation set alongside Jesus's ordinary participation (Matt 11:18-19).

**8. Ellen G. White's health-reform theology.** Health-reform teaching is a legitimate personal discipline. As stewardship of the body-as-temple (1 Cor 6:19), abstention from wine, tobacco, or heavy meat is a valid Christian choice — the study honors this in sections 1 and 5 as vocational abstention. What health-reform teaching cannot do is universalize its discipline as a biblical rule binding all Christians. The New Testament recognizes vocational disciplines (fasting, celibacy, monastic patterns) as callings for some, not norms for all. The Adventist tradition's stewardship theology is coherent and Christian; its extension into a universal rule against wine is where the exegetical case falls short.

**9. Modern pastoral testimony.** The pastoral counsel of Wilkerson, Van Impe, MacArthur, and Piper is *pastoral counsel* — grounded in ministry contexts with specific pastoral challenges. It should be received as such: valuable wisdom for the communities and moments it addresses, not universal Christian rule. The pastor of a congregation with significant addiction ministry may legitimately teach abstention as pastoral counsel. The same pastor may not teach that Scripture, apart from pastoral context, commands universal abstention. The two positions are distinct; conflating them is the temperance-tradition move Chapter 4 traced. And the tradition's own most careful voice draws exactly this line from inside the abstention camp: Piper's 1981 double answer — abstain, yes; require abstention for membership, no ("My answer to the first question is yes, and my answer to the second question is no") — led him to strike the total-abstinence clause from his own church's covenant while keeping the counsel in his own life. The strongest pastoral abstention testimony on record thus itself testifies that counsel must not become rule.

**10. "The fruit of the vine" at the Last Supper.** The phrase *gennēma tēs ampelou* is Jewish liturgical idiom, preserved intact in the *kiddush* blessing over wine to this day: "Blessed art thou, O Lord our God, King of the Universe, who createst the fruit of the vine". The formula is not chemistry-language distinguishing fermented from unfermented substance; it is the standard Jewish blessing over the substance of the Passover cup. The Mishnah's *Berakhot* 6:1 (Appendix C) confirms this: "Over wine one recites: Who creates fruit of the vine [*borei p'ri ha-gafen*]." *Fruit of the vine* is the blessing formula for wine specifically, not a distinct substance. Jesus's continuity-language — "until that day when I drink it new [*kainon*] with you in my Father's kingdom" (Matt 26:29) — presupposes the same substance in both the present cup and the eschatological one. Chapter 2 §3 develops this argument in detail. The eucharistic tradition of the pre-Nicene church, unanimous on the point (Chapter 3), confirms the reading.

**11. Jesus as member of a wine-abstaining Jewish sect.** The claim, examined in full in Appendix C, has no evidentiary base. The Qumran community (the best-attested candidate for a rigorist Jewish sect closest to the imagined Jesus-Essene identity) drank *tirosh* — new wine — at its own regulation meal (1QS VI:4-6; on the deliberate-*tirosh* rejoinder, see Appendix C); sect-affiliation with the Essenes would not entail abstention. Only the Therapeutae — an Egyptian philosophical community, not a Palestinian sect — prescribed wine-abstention, and no source connects Jesus to them. The Ebionites' water-eucharist (Epiphanius, *Panarion* 30.16) belongs to a Jewish-Christian movement attested from the second century (Irenaeus, *Adv. Haer.* I.26.2, c. AD 180) whose distinctive practices developed over time — the water-eucharist is reported only by Epiphanius c. AD 375 — and is contradicted by the earliest layer of Christian evidence (Didache, 1 Corinthians 11); the analogous water-only practice, when it surfaced among the third-century Aquarii, was corrected by mainstream pre-Nicene Christianity (Cyprian, *Ep.* 62). The Hegesippus tradition about James being a Nazirite from birth, even if accepted at face value, *distinguishes* James from Jesus rather than assimilating them — if James took a lifelong Nazirite vow and Jesus did not, that tells us the Jesus family knew both patterns and Jesus chose the non-Nazirite path. And Matthew 11:19 is decisive on Jesus's own testimony: his own hostile witnesses complain that he came "eating and drinking" in explicit contrast to John the Baptist's Nazirite abstention. What the hostile witnesses volunteer about Jesus is the very thing the abstaining-sect claim cannot accommodate. Mainstream historical-Jesus scholarship (Vermes, Sanders, Wright) uniformly rejects the sect-affiliation reading. The claim persists because it is pastorally useful, not because the evidence supports it.

**12. The officer-qualification argument (μὴ πάροινον).** The two-tier reading — deacons limited to little wine, overseers forbidden wine altogether — rests on an etymological parsing of *paroinos* ("beside wine") that the word's actual usage does not support. In classical and Koine Greek *paroinos* means "drunken, inebriated" — LSJ glosses it as equivalent to *paroinikos*, "drunk, inebriated" (Lysias 4.8; Antiphanes), gives μάχαι πάροινοι, "drunken brawls" (Anacreontea), and the adverb παροίνως, "drunkenly"; Lucian pairs πάροινος with μέθυσος, "drunkard" (*Timon* 55). The word names the quarrelsome behavior of a man in his cups — which is why Paul's list sets μὴ πάροινον next to "no striker... not a brawler" (1 Tim 3:3). The overseer must not be a wine-brawler; the deacon must not be devoted to much wine; the two phrases describe the same sobriety of character from two angles, not two tiers of substance-law. And the categorical reading collapses on Paul's own letter: two chapters later he commands Timothy — the very apostolic delegate charged with administering those leadership qualifications — "drink no longer water, but use a little wine for thy stomach's sake" (1 Tim 5:23). On the categorical reading of 3:3, Paul disqualifies his own delegate. Nor does the "medicinal exception" reading rescue it: Timothy's prior water-drinking shows personal practice (ascetic choice or prudence about water), not law — Paul *corrects* it, which he could not do if abstention were the standard his own qualification list had just imposed; and the prescription is still wine, prescribed by the apostle, which a categorically forbidden substance could never be (no rabbi prescribes pork for anemia). Bacchiocchi's suggestion that the medicinal wine was unfermented imports the two-wine lexicon already answered above — the two-wine advocate escapes the self-disqualification only by reading the *oinos* of 5:23 as unfermented juice, which returns the argument to the lexical case (response 2): the *methy*-family usage, the LXX bridging, and the same letter's own vocabulary give no warrant for the substitution. The νηφάλιος datum fails the same usage test its *paroinos* twin fails: in the Pastorals the word sits in virtue-lists beside "of good behaviour" and "faithful in all things" — sober-mindedness, as every modern version renders it. And the two-datum categorical reading generates a double implausibility in 1 Timothy 3 itself: the same overseer would be told "wineless" twice in adjacent clauses (νηφάλιος, 3:2; μὴ πάροινος, 3:3) while the same church's deacons five verses later are told merely "not much wine" — an implausible calibration; and in Titus, the aged women, held "likewise" (ὡσαύτως) to the aged men's νηφάλιος standard (2:2-3), are forbidden only *much* wine — an odd sequel if the men's standard meant literal winelessness. The pastoral practice of ministerial abstention (common in many traditions, and honorable) is a prudential discipline, not the meaning of *paroinos* or *nēphalios*.

---

**Additional evidence from the Second Temple Jewish world.** Appendix C also surveys the broader Dead Sea Scrolls and rabbinic material on wine. That evidence strengthens the study's argument, without complicating it. The Qumran sect drank *tirosh* at every regulation meal (1QS VI:4-6) and imagined the messianic banquet as a bread-and-new-wine meal (1QSa 2:17-22). The Mishnah requires four cups of wine at every Passover, mandatory even for the poorest (*Pesachim* 10:1). The Talmud debates wine's dangers extensively (Sanhedrin 70a-b) but never proposes covenantal abstention. Sirach 31:25-31 delivers the classical moderationist formulation three centuries before Paul writes 1 Timothy 5:23. The Christian moderationist position is not a Hellenistic accommodation of a stricter Jewish original; it is the direct inheritance of the Second Temple Jewish moderationist consensus.

## 3. Tensions that remain

Even after Chapter 4.5 has been answered, some tensions do not resolve cleanly.

**Individual conscience.** Romans 14 opens the door explicitly: "It is good neither to eat flesh, nor to drink wine, nor any thing whereby thy brother stumbleth, or is offended, or is made weak" (Rom 14:21). Paul's teaching presupposes that eating meat and drinking wine are intrinsically permitted, but that a Christian free-in-conscience may voluntarily abstain out of love for a weaker brother. The passage does not tell us how far that dynamic reaches in any specific culture or moment. It authorizes voluntary abstention; it does not mandate it. And it does not tell us when the "weaker brother" argument becomes a lever that inverts the freedom into a bondage.

**The pastoral concern behind the prohibitionist case is real.** Alcoholism is a genuine ministry problem. Recovery is a genuine ministry priority. Communities with high addiction burdens have a real interest in what appears on the communion table and at fellowship gatherings. The prohibitionist tradition's *exegetical* case cannot be sustained, but its *pastoral* concern deserves to be taken seriously by every congregation. The mature sacramental responses (Catholic *mustum*, Lutheran individual accommodation, ELCA whole-assembly flexibility) show that pastoral seriousness and biblical fidelity are not exclusive.

**Denominational latitude.** The doctrine-of-the-sacraments split described in Chapter 4 is real. Where a tradition holds a symbolic view of communion, the substance question is prudential; where a tradition holds a sacramental view, the substance is not negotiable. The two theologies produce different answers on the substance question because they are answering different underlying questions. This study takes no view on the doctrine of the sacraments as such; it takes a view only on the biblical and historical fact that fermented wine is what the church universally used until modern American temperance produced an alternative.

**The moral seriousness of wine.** Even for readers who accept the study's exegetical conclusions, a warning remains. Wine is a substance capable of harm. Scripture's warnings against drunkenness are strong for a reason. Modern moderation-defending literature can slip into a defensive posture that undersells the biblical warnings; that too would be a mistake. The right posture, the study argues, is the one Clement of Alexandria modeled: wine is a gift of God, drink it in moderation and in season, keep the drunk-song test as your daily measure, and honor those who out of vocation choose to abstain.

## 4. What the pattern is

Every SCAR component points the same direction. Scripture teaches that wine is God's gift and drunkenness is sin. Jesus lived it: he made, drank, and blessed wine in an ordinary Jewish culture, and warned against being caught unaware by drunkenness. The early church walked in his pattern: wine at the eucharistic cup, moderation at the ordinary table, condemnation of excess, honor for personal vocation. Modern academic scholarship has re-established this same pattern against the temperance-era rereading that displaced it in much of American Protestantism.

The pattern is *use-with-thanksgiving*. It is the pattern of 1 Timothy 4:4-5 — every creature of God is good, and nothing is to be refused when it is received with thanksgiving. It is the pattern of Deuteronomy 14:26 — take the tithe money and buy what your soul desires, wine or strong drink or whatever, and rejoice before the Lord. It is the pattern of Jesus at Cana — the good wine kept until now, the messianic sign in ordinary substance. It is the pattern of Justin's Sunday service — bread and wine and water brought together to the president of the brethren.

The pattern the two-wine theory offers instead — *use-only-what-cannot-abused* — is not the biblical pattern, and when the pre-Nicene church met the categorical form of it in the Encratites, it rejected it. It is a nineteenth-century modification, born of legitimate pastoral concern, sustained by a lexical claim that does not survive scholarly examination.

---

## 5. Multiple solutions in practice

Different communities honor the pattern in different ways. The study takes no side among the following options; each has integrity in its own frame.

**Continued sacramental practice.** Catholic, Orthodox, Lutheran (LCMS/WELS), Anglican, and continental Reformed traditions retain fermented wine as the eucharistic substance. This is the practice of the church for its first eighteen hundred years, preserved by these traditions with theological reasoning that this study does not evaluate. Their continuity with the pre-Nicene practice is complete.

**Pastoral accommodation within sacramental practice.** The Catholic *mustum* provision for clergy unable to consume alcohol (since 2003); the Lutheran and Anglican patterns of discreet individual accommodation; the ELCA's whole-assembly flexibility in some contexts — these preserve wine as the norm while responding to particular pastoral needs. This is the most nuanced practice, and arguably the closest to Paul's counsel in Romans 14.

**Continued temperance practice.** The Methodist, Baptist, Restorationist, Holiness, and Pentecostal traditions that adopted grape juice in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries retain it today. Their communion tables are grape juice; their teaching typically condemns wine as such, following the temperance tradition. This study argues that the exegetical basis for that teaching cannot be defended; it does not argue that the communities themselves are outside genuine Christian fellowship. Their communion is real communion; their concern for their addiction-vulnerable members is genuine pastoral care.

**Recovery of the older pattern.** The recent shift toward moderate wine use in Reformed and evangelical circles (Gentry, Wilson, and others) represents an attempt to recover the pre-temperance pattern. When done with pastoral care — not as a declaration against communities that keep the temperance practice, but as a recovery of what was normal for nearly two millennia — it stands within the biblical and historical trajectory this study has traced.

**Vocational abstention.** Some Christians will hear the fullest examination of the evidence and still, out of personal calling, choose to abstain. This is honored, in the tradition of the Nazirite and John the Baptist and the ascetic strand of pre-Nicene Christianity. The study argues only that vocational abstention should be received as vocation, not universalized as rule.

The love-and-unity test the SCAR methodology insists on (John 13:34-35; John 17:20-23) applies here: any Christian community's practice succeeds when it promotes love among its members and preserves unity in the wider body of Christ. It fails when it makes the substance-question a wedge — a first-order dividing line where Christians who read the evidence differently are treated as compromised or unfaithful. Substance is not first-order in Jesus's own priority-structure; the love and unity of his body are.

## 6. Practical implementation

For a reader working out how to live inside this pattern, several practical guidelines emerge from the study.

**Take Scripture's warnings against drunkenness seriously.** They are not culturally contingent temperance-era exaggerations; they are the plain and repeated voice of both testaments. Moderation is the biblical standard, and it is a real standard: it can be lived, but it takes attention. And let moderation be indexed to strength: modern table wine is undiluted and stronger than the customarily mixed wine of antiquity — dilute, pour less, or choose lower-strength, in the tradition of Clement's own counsel to "mix the wine with as much water as possible."

**Read your own vocation honestly.** If you are, or have been, subject to intemperance in ways that make ordinary drinking a snare, abstain — with the church's blessing and in the tradition of Nazirite and monastic vocation. Personal abstention out of self-knowledge is a Christian virtue.

**Read your community's vocation honestly.** If you serve in a congregation with a substantial recovery ministry, or in a mission field where cultural drinking is at chronic excess, the pastoral wisdom Paul commends in Romans 14 may direct the community's practice toward abstention. That is a legitimate pastoral judgment. It does not require rewriting the lexicon.

**Do not universalize your particular practice.** A community that keeps wine at communion should not treat a community that keeps grape juice as sub-Christian. A community that keeps grape juice should not treat a community that keeps wine as compromised. And neither community should treat the substance question as a test of orthodoxy or of personal holiness. A community that adopts corporate abstention for pastoral reasons — high addiction burden, recovery culture, care for the weaker brother — is exercising legitimate prudence; the counsel here is only against elevating that local practice into a test of another community's fidelity to Christ. Both are working out the same underlying pattern — use with thanksgiving, moderation always, honor for those whose vocation calls for something different — in their own local shape.

**Test any practice by love and unity.** Where the substance-question is being used to divide Christ's body, the question has been mis-scaled. Where it is being lived quietly, with pastoral attention and mutual honor, the pattern is being kept.

## 7. The ultimate question

The study opened with a driving question: did Jesus drink alcoholic wine, and does Scripture teach that wine as a substance is sinful?

The answer, on the evidence assembled here, is clear on both counts. Jesus drank fermented wine — at Cana as its provider, at ordinary meals as an observable participant, at the Last Supper as its blesser. Scripture does not teach that wine as a substance is sinful; it teaches that wine is a gift of God, that drunkenness is a sin, and that abstention is a vocation available to any who receive it as calling.

The study's most important contribution, however, is not the answer to the substance-question. It is the *scale* at which the question sits. Wine is not a first-order Christian question. Jesus taught nothing about it beyond what his own life demonstrated, because it was not among the things he came to teach. The love of God and neighbor is first-order. The confession of Christ crucified and risen is first-order. The unity of his body is first-order. Where communion substance serves the first-order things, its exact composition — Catholic wine, Methodist grape juice, Catholic *mustum* accommodation, Adventist juice, PCA either-or — is prudential and pastoral. Where communion substance becomes the wedge that divides brothers, the question has been mis-scaled by every side that participates in the division.

This is what Jesus's priorities require. This is what Scripture teaches when read whole. This is what antiquity practiced. And this is the pattern by which a modern Christian community — of any denominational stripe — can live inside the wine question without letting it displace the questions that matter more.

## 8. Reader questions

Several questions this study cannot answer belong to the reader's own discernment.

*Should my church change its communion practice?* This is a matter for your church's own polity and pastoral wisdom. The study argues that the exegetical case for the two-wine theory cannot be defended, and that the pre-Nicene and universal-church practice was fermented wine. It does not argue that a community using grape juice should change immediately, or that a community using wine should refuse to accommodate members with cause. Read your own community's vocation carefully.

*What about children at communion?* Both practices — wine and grape juice — have live traditions of accommodating children. Catholic first communion is at the age of reason; children partake of both species where communion of both species is standard for their community. Protestant traditions vary. Neither is dictated by the substance-question as such.

*What about recovering alcoholics?* Recovering alcoholics have every right to receive grape juice (or the equivalent), and every mature Christian tradition provides for this. The Catholic *mustum* provision was designed exactly for this. In traditions that use wine, discreet individual accommodation is normal. Do not let the substance-question become a barrier to receiving communion for anyone whose recovery it might jeopardize.

*What about cultural drinking?* Modern Christian cultures vary enormously — from Mediterranean Catholic cultures in which daily wine at meals is unremarkable, to American evangelical cultures in which any drinking is regarded as compromise, to Nordic Lutheran cultures with their own regional patterns. Scripture and history do not settle the cultural question; they settle the substance-question. Live your culture with charity and self-knowledge, and let your ordinary drinking (if any) honor the biblical warning against excess.

*What if I still find wine morally suspect?* Read the evidence for yourself. Read Jesus at Cana and Matthew 11:19. Read Cyprian's letter. Read Clement of Alexandria's *Paedagogus* II.2. If, after honest engagement with the evidence, you still find personal abstention right for you, honor that as vocation. Do not universalize it as rule. And do not treat brothers and sisters who drink moderately in thanksgiving as compromised in their faith — Scripture and history do not support that judgment, and love does not permit it.

The overview chapter (Chapter 0) — placed first in the finished study but written last — puts the whole into narrative form for the reader whose primary interest is the pastoral question. The evidence is here. The pattern is clear. Wine is a gift; drunkenness is a sin; abstention is a vocation; love and unity are above every substance-question. On this the four SCAR components speak with one voice.
