# Appendix C: The Dead Sea Scrolls, Rabbinic Literature, and the Jesus-Sect Question

The pre-Nicene Christian evidence (Chapter 3) shows a church that drank wine at the eucharist, drank wine at love-feasts, and condemned drunkenness while permitting moderate use. The prior question is what the *Jewish* world of the first century looked like on the same substance. The Dead Sea Scrolls, Josephus, Philo, the Mishnah, and the Talmud together give an unusually complete picture. This appendix walks through that evidence and then addresses a specific claim — sometimes heard in the popular literature — that Jesus belonged to a Jewish sect that abstained from wine.

The picture, as this appendix will show, is uniformly moderationist. Wine is present at the sect's most sacred meals, at every Passover cup, at every blessing, at every wedding. Where austere Jewish groups appear — the Essenes at Qumran, the Therapeutae in Egypt, the Rechabite lineage — the evidence must be examined case by case, but none of them supply what a modern prohibitionist reading of Jesus needs.

Some passages here are marked with an editorial note when the exact wording of a translation could not be verified against a physical copy in the research pass; those notes are preserved for the reader's own verification.

## Part 1: What the Second Temple Jewish evidence contributes

### 1. Qumran and the Dead Sea Scrolls

**1QS (Community Rule) VI:4-6.** The Community Rule is the sect's own constitution. It prescribes communal meals presided over by a priest. The blessing formula for the meal:

> And they shall eat in common and pray in common and deliberate in common. Wherever there are ten men of the Council of the Community, there shall not lack a Priest among them. And they shall all sit before him according to their rank... And when the table has been prepared for eating, and the new wine [*tirosh*] for drinking, the Priest shall be the first to stretch out his hand to bless the first-fruits of the bread and new wine.
> — 1QS VI:2-6 (Vermes, *The Complete Dead Sea Scrolls in English*, 2011)

Two facts are decisive. First: the Qumran sect — the strictest, most separatist Jewish community of the Second Temple period, the group whose purity rules exceeded even the Pharisees' — drank *tirosh* at its regulation communal meal. Second: the drinking is not incidental but liturgical — a blessing precedes it, and the priest's precedence in the blessing marks the meal as ritual. The sect that most fully embodied Jewish sectarian rigor did not abstain from wine; it sanctified wine.

**1QSa (Rule of the Congregation) 2:11-22 — the messianic banquet.** Copied on the same scroll as 1QS, 1QSa is the sect's rule "for the whole congregation of Israel in the latter days" — the eschatological, messianic-age version of the meal:

> And when they shall gather for the common table, to eat and to drink new wine [*tirosh*], when the common table shall be set for eating and the new wine poured for drinking, let no man extend his hand over the first-fruits of bread and wine before the Priest; for he shall bless the first-fruits of bread and wine, and shall be the first to extend his hand over the bread. Thereafter, the Messiah of Israel shall extend his hand over the bread; and all the Congregation of the Community shall utter a blessing, each man in the order of his dignity. It is according to this statute that they shall proceed at every meal at which at least ten men are gathered together.
> — 1QSa 2:17-22 (Vermes 2011, pp. 159-160; wording verified against the Google Books preview of the 2011 Penguin edition)

This is one of the most important texts in the whole Second Temple corpus for the wine question. The messianic banquet — the meal at which the Priest-Messiah and the Messiah of Israel preside — is a meal of *bread and new wine*. Not water. The Hebrew term is *tirosh*, and the deliberate-*tirosh* rejoinder should be named and answered: an abstentionist could reply that Qumran's rules prescribe *tirosh*, never *yayin*, and that the sect's word-choice is itself a purity scruple — juice, not wine. Three things defeat the reading. First, the rabbinic tradition's own wordplay on this very word — b. Sanhedrin 70a, verified verbatim below — treats *tirosh* as a drink with a moderate/excessive axis: "If one merits and drinks a moderate amount he becomes a leader [*rosh*], whereas if he does not merit and drinks excessively he becomes poor [*rash*]" — an axis that does not exist for juice. Second, Hosea 4:11 pairs *tirosh* with *yayin* as substances that "take away the heart." Third, and practically: 1QS prescribes the blessing over *tirosh* "at every meal at which at least ten men are gathered" — a year-round rule. Fresh must exists for a few weeks after vintage; stored must ferments; keeping it unfermented year-round would require exactly the preservation apparatus (boiling, filtering, sulfuring) that nothing at Qumran attests. The community's *tirosh* is new wine — the wine of joy, the wine of harvest, in the vocabulary of Joel 2:24 and the blessing formulas.

The intertextual echoes to the Last Supper and to the Cana wedding at John 2 are direct. Jesus's Last Supper (Matt 26:29) blesses bread and cup with an eschatological horizon ("until that day I drink it new with you in my Father's kingdom"). The Qumran messianic banquet blesses bread and cup with the same horizon. Both use *tirosh* / *oinos neos* language. Whatever else 1QSa proves, it proves that a first-century Jewish sect's vision of the messianic age — the age of righteousness and covenant fulfillment — was structured around wine, not around its absence.

**CD (Damascus Document).** CD is the sect's second major rule text. Its wine-related content is limited and revealing. The one direct wine regulation prohibits selling wine to Gentiles for potential idolatrous use:

> No man shall sell clean beasts or birds to the Gentiles lest they offer them in sacrifice. He shall refuse, with all his power, to sell them anything from his granary or wine-press...
> — CD 12:8-10 (Vermes, *The Dead Sea Scrolls in English*)

The concern is separation, not abstention — the sect will not participate in Gentile pagan sacrifice by supplying its raw material. Wine is treated as legitimate produce; the restriction is on its commercial destination.

**Verdict for the Qumran corpus.** The community that gave us the most demanding Jewish sectarian purity code of the Second Temple period drank wine. It drank wine at its regulation communal meal, it envisioned wine at the messianic banquet, and its scattered restrictions on wine involve Gentile commerce, not personal use. No Qumran text prescribes abstention.

### 2. Josephus and Philo on the Essenes

Josephus's fullest description of Essene meal practice:

> The same priest, when he hath dined, says grace again after meat; and when they begin, and when they end, they praise God, as he that bestows their food upon them.
> — Josephus, *Wars of the Jews* 2.8.5 (Whiston)

And the crucial line on discipline:

> This is demonstrated by that perpetual sobriety they exercise, and the same settled measure of meat and drink that is allotted to them, and that such as is abundantly sufficient for them.
> — Josephus, *Wars* 2.8.5 (Whiston)

The Whiston translation reads "perpetual sobriety" — sobriety, not abstention; the English names the condition of one not intoxicated. (The underlying Greek of *B.J.* 2.133 has not been checked against a critical edition in this pass, so no claim is made here about its exact vocabulary.) Josephus is describing *moderation*, not *abstention*. The "settled measure of meat and drink" is a fixed portion, not a fixed absence.

Josephus does not name wine explicitly in this passage. The absence has been read both ways: some infer abstention from the silence; most infer that wine is the assumed drink and Josephus's silence reflects the routineness of it. The 1QS text above breaks the tie — the sect *does* drink wine at its regulation meal, so Josephus's "meat and drink" almost certainly includes wine.

Philo's Essenes (*Every Good Man Is Free* §§ 75-91) are the same group. Philo does not name wine in the Essene passage. Like Josephus, he emphasizes shared meal, communal simplicity, and frugality. He does not describe abstention as a rule.

**Philo's Therapeutae are a different matter.** In *On the Contemplative Life*, Philo describes an Egyptian Jewish contemplative community that did abstain from wine:

> They do not admit wine into their gatherings, but only the clearest water; cold water for the generality, and hot water for such of the old men as are accustomed to a luxurious life.
> — Philo, *On the Contemplative Life* §§ 73-74 (Yonge)

> For wine is the medicine of folly, and costly seasonings and sauces excite desire, which is the most insatiable of all beasts.
> — Philo, *On the Contemplative Life* § 74 (Yonge)

The Therapeutae are the one Second Temple Jewish community for whom substance-abstention is directly attested. But three things must be noted. First: the Therapeutae are *not* the Essenes; Philo describes them as a distinct, primarily Egyptian, primarily philosophical group. Second: their abstention is not framed as covenantal obligation but as *philosophical* discipline — Philo's rationale ("wine is the medicine of folly") is Platonic-Stoic, not scriptural. Third: they are geographically and demographically remote from Galilean-Judean Judaism; the New Testament shows no contact with them.

**Verdict on the Essene / Therapeutae evidence.** The Essenes practiced moderation, not abstention — their own foundational rule (1QS VI) prescribes ritual wine-drinking. Philo's Therapeutae did abstain, but they were an Egyptian philosophical community, not part of the Palestinian sectarian landscape Jesus inhabited. No Jewish group in the Palestinian evidence prescribed total wine-abstention on covenantal grounds.

### 3. The Mishnah

**Mishnah *Pesachim* 10:1 — the Passover cups.**

> On the eve of Passover close to *minha* [afternoon prayer] a person must not eat until it becomes dark. And even the poorest of Jews must not eat until he reclines. And they must give him no less than four cups of wine, and even if [he receives funds] from the charity plate.
> — m. Pesachim 10:1 (Koren-Steinsaltz / William Davidson Edition, Sefaria)

This is the load-bearing text for the substance question at the Last Supper. Four cups. Wine. Even the poorest man must have wine at Passover; the community's charitable obligation is *to provide the wine*, not to substitute juice or water. The rabbinic tradition does not know a Passover with grape juice.

The subsequent mishnayot of *Pesachim* 10 detail the ritual sequence: cup one before the meal, cup two after the *maggid* (telling), cup three after the meal (the *cup of blessing* — the eucharistic cup in Christian tradition), cup four with the concluding *hallel*. Every cup is wine.

**Mishnah *Berakhot* 6:1 — the blessing over wine.**

> What blessing does one recite over fruits?... Over wine one recites: Who creates fruit of the vine [*borei p'ri ha-gafen*]. Over bread one recites: Who brings forth bread from the earth.
> — m. Berakhot 6:1 (Koren-Steinsaltz / Davidson, Sefaria)

Jesus's "fruit of the vine" formula at the Last Supper (Matt 26:29 par.) is precisely this liturgical formula — *p'ri ha-gefen* → *gennēma tēs ampelou*. The blessing does not refer to "fruit of the vine" as a distinct substance from wine; it refers to *wine*. The rabbinic distinction between blessings is between *substances* (fruit of the tree / fruit of the ground / fruit of the vine / bread), and *fruit of the vine* is the blessing formula for wine specifically. Chrysostom, Justin, and the whole pre-Nicene Christian tradition inherit this understanding.

### 4. The Talmud

**b. Sanhedrin 70a — wine, Noah, and the Tree.** The Bavli discussion of Noah's drunkenness is one of the most extended rabbinic treatments of wine. (All quotations below re-verified verbatim against the William Davidson Edition via the Sefaria API, 2026-07-30.)

Rav Ḥanan on wine's purpose:

> Wine was created in the world only to comfort mourners in their distress, and to reward the wicked in this world so that they will have no reward left in the World-to-Come, as it is stated: "Give strong drink to him that is ready to perish, and wine to the bitter of soul" (Proverbs 31:6).
> — b. Sanhedrin 70a (Davidson)

The Gemara's exposition of Proverbs 23:31 — the very verse the prohibitionist tradition presses:

> What is the meaning of that which is written: "Look not upon wine when it is red" (Proverbs 23:31)? Look not upon wine that reddens the faces of the wicked in this world when they drink it, and whitens their faces, i.e., embarrasses them, in the World-to-Come.
> — b. Sanhedrin 70a (Davidson)

The Gemara's wordplay on *tirosh* — and note where the rabbinic line is drawn:

> The word for wine is written *tirash*, without the letter *vav*, but we read it as *tirosh*, with the letter *vav*. The matter can be explained as follows: If one merits and drinks a moderate amount he becomes a leader [*rosh*], whereas if he does not merit and drinks excessively he becomes poor [*rash*].
> — b. Sanhedrin 70a (Davidson)

The Davidson rendering makes the rabbinic frame explicit: the line runs between *a moderate amount* and *excessively* — the use/abuse distinction, not a substance distinction. And elsewhere in the Bavli, the famous gematria on wine and secrets:

> Wine [*yayin*] is given in letters of seventy, i.e., the numerical value of the letters in the word *yayin* is seventy, and secret [*sod*] is given in letters of seventy... When wine enters, secrets emerge.
> — b. Sanhedrin 38a (Davidson; parallel at b. Eruvin 65a)

On the Tree of Knowledge as a grapevine:

> As it is taught in a baraita: Rabbi Meir says: The tree from which Adam the first man ate was a grapevine.
> — b. Sanhedrin 70a (Davidson)

Two things stand out. First: the rabbis are ambivalent about wine — they see it as capable of both consolation (mourners) and destruction (Noah, Solomon, Meir's Adam). This is *exactly* the biblical pattern of Psalm 104:15 balanced against Proverbs 20:1. Second: not one voice in the whole Sanhedrin 70 sugya says "therefore Israel should not drink." The rabbis debate Adam's tree, Noah's fault, Solomon's fall — but they never propose that the covenant community should abstain. Their own wordplay on *tirosh* draws the line at *moderate* versus *excessive* drinking. Their solution to wine's dangers is halakhic regulation, not substance-prohibition.

**b. Sanhedrin 43a — the condemned man's wine cup.**

> The court gives one who is being led out to be killed a grain [*koret*] of frankincense in a cup of wine in order to confuse his mind and thereby minimize his suffering from the fear of his impending death, as it is stated: "Give strong drink to him that is ready to perish, and wine to the bitter in soul" (Proverbs 31:6).
> — b. Sanhedrin 43a (Davidson)

This is the rabbinic parallel to Mark 15:23 / Matthew 27:34 — the condemned received a wine-based analgesic. The mainstream Talmudic tradition (independent of any Christian influence) preserves the practice: wine, capable of blunting consciousness, given to those about to die. The substance is functionally identified as intoxicating — no "grape juice" reading is possible.

### 5. Sirach — the pre-rabbinic Jewish moderationist locus

Ben Sira (Sirach), written c. 180 BC and preserved in the LXX, is the closest pre-Christian analog to what will become the Christian moderationist position. Clement of Alexandria will later quote it at length in *Paedagogus* II.2 (see Chapter 3 §3).

> Do not try to prove your strength by wine-drinking, for wine has destroyed many. As the furnace tests the work of the smith, so wine tests hearts when the insolent quarrel. Wine is very life to human beings if taken in moderation. What is life to one who is without wine? It has been created to make people happy. Wine drunk at the proper time and in moderation is rejoicing of heart and gladness of soul. Wine drunk to excess leads to bitterness of spirit, to quarrels and stumbling. Drunkenness increases the anger of a fool to his own hurt, reducing his strength and adding wounds. Do not reprove your neighbor at a banquet of wine, and do not despise him in his merrymaking; speak no word of reproach to him, and do not distress him by making demands of him.
> — Sirach 31:25-31 (NRSV-CE; verified verbatim against BibleGateway, 2026-07-27)

The same substance-vs-abuse distinction that structures the New Testament, the pre-Nicene fathers, and the modern moderationist reading is already fully articulated in Ben Sira three centuries before Paul writes 1 Timothy 5:23.

### Summary — DSS/Rabbinic contribution to the wine question

The evidence is uniform. The Jewish world in which Jesus lived and taught, from the strictest sectarian community (Qumran) to the codified rabbinic tradition (Mishnah, Talmud) to the sapiential wisdom books (Sirach), drank wine as a matter of course and sanctified wine liturgically. The Qumran sect drank *tirosh* at every communal meal of ten or more and imagined the messianic banquet as a bread-and-new-wine meal. The Essenes were sober but not abstinent. Only the Therapeutae — an Egyptian philosophical community, not a Palestinian sect — abstained on Platonic-Stoic grounds. The Mishnah requires four cups of wine at every Passover, mandatory even for the poorest. The Talmud debates wine's dangers extensively but never proposes covenantal abstention. Sirach 31 delivers the classical moderationist formulation three centuries before Paul.

The DSS and rabbinic evidence *strengthens* the study's argument, without complicating it. The Christian moderationist position is not a Hellenistic accommodation of a stricter Jewish original. It is the direct inheritance of the Second Temple Jewish moderationist consensus. Jesus did not receive a Judaism that abstained from wine and then loosen it for his Gentile Church; he received a Judaism that drank wine liturgically at Passover, at Sabbath, at every regulation meal of a rigorist sect, and at the imagined messianic banquet — and he presided at the Cana wedding and the Last Supper in perfect continuity with that inheritance.

## Part 2: Was Jesus part of a sect that did not drink alcohol?

The claim is popular and persistent. It surfaces in health-reform Adventist literature, in "Jesus was an Essene" popular books (Karl Bahrdt 1784, Karl Venturini 1802, Barbara Thiering 1992, Elmar Gruber, Elizabeth Clare Prophet), in some Ebionite-revivalist circles, and in occasional stray remarks from otherwise mainstream teachers. The SCAR method requires the claim be presented in its strongest form before it is engaged. Chapter 4.5 §11 presents the claim; this section examines the evidence in detail.

### The candidate sects

**Essenes.** The evidence against Jesus being an Essene is decisive. First, *practice mismatch*: the Essenes' regulation communal meal (1QS VI, above) drank wine. So if Jesus had been an Essene, he would have drunk wine as a member of that sect — the sect-affiliation would not be the evidence of abstention. The argument shoots itself in the foot. Second, *personal mismatch*: the Essenes were largely celibate (Josephus reports a marrying order, *B.J.* 2.8.13), communal-property, sabbath-strict beyond even the Pharisees, and geographically concentrated at Qumran and a few other communities. Jesus was itinerant, ate with tax collectors, healed on the Sabbath, and worked no communal property. Third, *scholarly consensus*: Geza Vermes (*Jesus the Jew*, 1973) placed Jesus in the tradition of the Galilean *hasid* — the itinerant charismatic-pietist type — not the Essenes. E. P. Sanders (*Jesus and Judaism*, 1985) placed Jesus in mainstream apocalyptic Judaism. N. T. Wright (*Jesus and the Victory of God*, 1996) placed Jesus in a broadly prophetic tradition, distinct from Qumran sectarianism. It should be added that the strongest popular forms of the claim escape the 1QS wine evidence only by redefining the category — treating the Community Rule's wine provisions as non-normative, late redaction, or in need of re-translation. The move is circular: the identification of Jesus with the Essenes rests on the Qumran texts, and survives those texts' plain content only by setting the inconvenient portions aside.

**Ebionites.** The Ebionites are a Jewish-Christian movement attested from the late second century (Irenaeus, *AH* I.26.2, c. AD 180); their water-eucharist is first attested by Epiphanius in *Panarion* 30 (c. AD 375). Epiphanius describes their eucharistic practice directly:

> They too receive baptism, apart from their daily baptisms. And they celebrate supposed mysteries from year to year in imitation of the sacred mysteries of the church, using unleavened bread — and the other part of the mystery with water only.
> — Epiphanius, *Panarion* 30.16.1 (Frank Williams, NHMS Vol. 35; verified verbatim against archive.org `epiphanius-of-salamis-the-panarion-book-i-sects-1-46`, 2026-07-27)

Their water-eucharist is a genuine attested Christian practice. But the evidence against connecting it to Jesus is fivefold. First, *chronology*: the Ebionite movement is attested from the second century (Irenaeus, *Adv. Haer.* I.26.2), but the water-eucharist report comes only from Epiphanius c. AD 375 — 345 years removed from Jesus, with no earlier witness tying the practice to the movement's origins. Second, *contradiction with the earliest Christian evidence*: the Didache (c. AD 50-110) already gives thanks over the cup "for the holy vine of Thy son David"; Paul's Corinthians were getting drunk at the supper (1 Cor 11:21, AD 50s — impossible with juice); and the earliest explicit witness, Justin (*1 Apol.* 65), names the cup as wine. Third, *Ebionite heterodoxy on other fronts* (Paul-rejection, virgin-birth denial, vegetarianism) marks the group as an innovator, not a preserver. Fourth, *Cyprian's testimony* (Chapter 3 §2): the mid-third-century North African bishop treats the water-only cup as an aberration. Fifth, *the Hegesippus data on James actually cuts the other way*. Hegesippus (preserved by Eusebius, *H.E.* II.23) describes James the brother of the Lord as a Nazirite from birth:

> He was called the "Just" by all men from the Lord's time to ours, since many are called James, but he was holy from his mother's womb. He drank no wine or strong drink, nor did he eat flesh; no razor went upon his head; he did not anoint himself with oil, and he did not go to the baths.
> — Hegesippus, quoted by Eusebius, *H.E.* II.23 (Loeb, Kirsopp Lake; verified verbatim against archive.org `eusebius-ecclesiastical-history-loeb`, 2026-07-27)

But this account *distinguishes* James from Jesus rather than assimilating them. If James took a lifelong Nazirite vow and Jesus did not, that is evidence that the Jesus family knew both patterns and that Jesus chose the non-Nazirite path. Matthew 11:19 — Jesus's own testimony that he came *eating and drinking* — directly contrasts him with the Nazirite discipline his brother James took up. The two brothers walked different disciplines within one family.

The opposite reading — that the Hegesippus material evidences a shared **family** Nazirite lineage across John the Baptist, Jesus, and James — has a mainstream-press modern carrier in James D. Tabor (Chair of Religious Studies, UNC Charlotte), whose *The Jesus Dynasty* (Simon & Schuster, 2006) reads Hegesippus as historical evidence for exactly that pattern:

> [Hegesippus] wrote that James was "holy from his mother's womb" and like his kinsman John the Baptizer neither drank wine nor ate any meat. Hegesippus also reported that James wore the linen of a priest and constantly prayed in the Temple, kneeling so much of the time that his knees grew hard like a camel's.
> — Tabor, *The Jesus Dynasty* (New York: Simon & Schuster, 2006), 306

Tabor's move is to make Hegesippus's word "kinsman" — for the John-and-James relation — load-bearing: not asceticism as personal vocation but as *inherited kinship practice* within Jesus's extended family. Robert Eisenman had made a stronger and more radical form of the same case in *James the Brother of Jesus* (Viking, 1997) — a full 1000-page reconstruction that mainstream historical-Jesus scholarship (Vermes, Sanders, Wright) has not received. The study acknowledges the family-Nazirite reading here in **Tabor's** voice rather than Eisenman's, not because Tabor's is the stronger academic form of the argument but because it is the reading a general reader will most plausibly encounter — Simon & Schuster mainstream press, UNC professor, four-hundred-page popular history — and steel-man discipline requires engaging the version of the argument the reader is likeliest to have met, not the marginalized academic maximum.

The study's answer to Tabor stands where it did against the Ebionite reading: the New Testament's own witness (Matthew 11:19) explicitly *contrasts* Jesus's practice with John's Nazirite discipline; and the same Hegesippus source that distinguishes James by lifelong Nazirite vow implicitly locates Jesus outside that vow. That a family knew both patterns is unsurprising. That Jesus, of the whole family, chose the non-Nazirite path is what Matthew 11:19 reports the crowd noticing. Tabor's family-lineage reading elevates the shared inheritance; the study argues the more careful reading elevates the intra-family divergence.

**Nazoreans / Nazarenes.** The "Nazoreans" (or Nazarenes) appear as a term for early Jewish Christians (Acts 24:5). The name is homonymous with Nazirite (Hebrew *nazir* → Greek *naziraios*, the vow-taker; Matthew's *Nazōraios*, the townsman) but the two words derive from different Hebrew roots. Patristic writers occasionally confuse them. The Nazoreans as attested do not appear to have practiced substance-abstention from wine.

**Therapeutae.** Philo's Therapeutae did abstain from wine (see Part 1). But they were Egyptian, philosophical rather than covenantal, and geographically and organizationally isolated from Palestinian Judaism. Nothing in the New Testament connects Jesus to them.

**Rechabites post-70 AD.** The Rechabites' post-Temple continuity is attested in scattered sources — the Mekhilta names their descendants, medieval texts imagine a "Land of the Rechabites," and 19th-century travelers (Joseph Wolff in Yemen, 1839) report tribes claiming Rechabite descent. None of this is evidence that the New Testament church was Rechabite-descended.

### The New Testament evidence

Three New Testament data points are load-bearing.

**Matthew 11:19 / Luke 7:34 — the "winebibber" accusation.**

> The Son of man came eating and drinking, and they say, Behold a man gluttonous, and a winebibber, a friend of publicans and sinners.
> — Matthew 11:19

The Greek is *oinopotēs* — literally "wine-drinker" (with a pejorative shading, as in Proverbs 23:20 LXX). The accusation cannot land unless Jesus is *seen drinking wine*. His accusers are not accusing him of drinking grape juice — the accusation is only an accusation if the substance is fermented. Jesus in the very next clause does not deny the substance-charge but distinguishes his practice from John the Baptist's: "For John came neither eating nor drinking... The Son of man came eating and drinking." John the Baptist did abstain — Luke 1:15 announces him as a Nazirite from the womb. Jesus explicitly *contrasts* his own practice with John's. That is the New Testament's own testimony that Jesus was *not* a Nazirite.

**The Cana wedding (John 2:1-11).** Jesus produces 120-180 gallons of *oinos* — wine — at a wedding. The steward's testimony ("thou hast kept the good *oinos* until now") is the study's title verse. This is a public act. A wine-abstaining sect member does not do this; a wine-abstaining sect member would not attend a wine-serving wedding, let alone provide the supply.

**The Last Supper.** The Passover cups (per m. Pesachim 10, above) were wine. Jesus's "fruit of the vine" is the liturgical formula for the wine-blessing (per m. Berakhot 6:1, above). His eschatological horizon — "I will not drink of it until that day when I drink it new with you in my Father's kingdom" — presupposes the same substance in the present cup and the eschatological cup. And the eschatological cup, on the Second Temple Jewish imagination, is *tirosh* (1QSa 2:17-22, above).

### Why the claim persists

The claim persists for three reasons — none of them rests on the historical evidence.

First: pastoral usefulness. If Jesus abstained from wine, the temperance-era prohibitionist reading of the New Testament acquires a Christological anchor that plain lexical and historical exegesis does not give it.

Second: the halo of DSS mystery. The 1947-1956 Dead Sea Scrolls discovery had massive popular impact. Popular literature ("Jesus scrolls," "Essene gospels") capitalized on the mystique. Barbara Thiering's *Jesus the Man* (1992) was a bestseller precisely because it plugged Jesus into the Qumran narrative — but academic reviewers uniformly rejected her method.

Third: partial truth. Jesus and John the Baptist and the early Jesus movement did emerge from a Second Temple Jewish milieu that included the Essenes, and there are real shared features (baptism, apocalyptic urgency, communal ethics). Popular writers over-read these shared features into full sect-affiliation. Academic scholarship reads them as shared cultural inheritance, not sect-membership.

### Verdict

**Jesus was not a member of a wine-abstaining sect.**

The rigorist Jewish sect closest to the imagined Jesus-Essene identity (the Qumran community, the best-attested candidate) *drank wine* at its own regulation meal. Sect-affiliation with the Essenes would not entail abstention. The one Second Temple community that did prescribe wine-abstention (the Therapeutae) was Egyptian, philosophical, geographically remote, and not connected to Jesus by any source. The Ebionites' water-eucharist is a later Christian development, contradicted by the earliest layer of Christian evidence and treated as an aberration by mainstream pre-Nicene Christianity. The Hegesippus tradition about James being a Nazirite from birth, even if accepted at face value, *distinguishes* James from Jesus rather than assimilating them. The New Testament's own testimony — Matthew 11:19 ("the Son of man came eating and drinking… a winebibber"), Cana, the Last Supper — actively contradicts an abstaining-Jesus reading. Mainstream historical-Jesus scholarship (Vermes, Sanders, Wright) rejects the sect-affiliation reading. Tabor's family-lineage reading (Simon & Schuster, 2006, treated above) is the strongest mainstream-press modern carrier of the claim; Eisenman's *James the Brother of Jesus* (Viking, 1997) is the more radical academic proponent. On the historical-scholarly frame from ancient Israelite viticulture, the Rechabite and Nazirite groups appear as bounded exceptions rather than as evidence of a widespread abstention norm: Carey Ellen Walsh's Harvard Semitic Monograph notes that patriarchal-era household drinking ordinarily included women and children, with "the Rechabite children (Jer 35:8) and Nazirite youths (1 Sam 1:11)" as the explicit exceptions whose prohibitions "rest on grounds other than that of age" (Walsh, *The Fruit of the Vine*, 237 n. 40).

Steel-manned, the claim collapses under Matthew 11:19 alone. The crowd's "winebibber" accusation functions only as a contrast with John the Baptist's observable Nazirite-style abstention — a drinking-charge leveled against a visibly abstaining teacher would have carried no rhetorical purchase. Hostile witnesses may distort (Jesus was no glutton), but distortion needs a substrate: the accusation exaggerates observed ordinary drinking; it cannot be manufactured against a public abstainer standing beside an actual one — and even taken as the Deuteronomy 21:20 stock formula (Chapter 4.5 §1), the charge had to fit its target to sting (Chapter 5, response 1d).
