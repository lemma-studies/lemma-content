# Chapter 3: Antiquity --- The Early Church Witness

## Why the Early Church Matters

The earliest Christians — those within 1-2 generations of the apostles — read the same Scriptures, remembered the apostolic voice, and practiced their faith under persecution rather than political power. Their witness matters not for infallibility but for proximity.

**Important caution:** Absence of a teaching in early church writings may mean it wasn't apostolic teaching OR simply wasn't their urgent concern. Arguments from silence require humility.

This chapter examines what early Christians wrote about the end times. The pre-Nicene witness (before 325 AD) comes first and carries the SCAR Antiquity weight; it is followed by an explicitly labeled survey of how the tradition continued (Chrysostom through Aquinas) and by the rabbinic parallels. The chapter asks what these writers said about:
- The antichrist / man of sin
- Christ's return and the resurrection
- The timing of these events

## The Apostolic Fathers (c. 70-150 AD)

### Didache (c. 70-100 AD)

**Context:** Earliest Christian church manual, likely written in Syria. Chapter 16 is the eschatological conclusion --- the earliest post-apostolic teaching on the end times.

**Source:** *The Didache* (Teaching of the Twelve Apostles), Chapter 16 (ANF, Vol. 7, trans. M. B. Riddle)

> "Watch for your life's sake. Let not your lamps be quenched, nor your loins unloosed; but be ready, for you know not the hour in which our Lord comes."
>
> "For in the last days false prophets and corrupters shall be multiplied, and the sheep shall be turned into wolves, and love shall be turned into hate; for when lawlessness increases, they shall hate and persecute and betray one another, and then shall appear the world-deceiver as the Son of God, and shall do signs and wonders, and the earth shall be delivered into his hands, and he shall do iniquitous things which have never yet come to pass since the beginning."
>
> "Then shall the creation of men come into the fire of trial, and many shall be made to stumble and shall perish; but they that endure in their faith shall be saved from under the curse itself. And then shall appear the signs of the truth; first, the sign of an outspreading in heaven; then the sign of the sound of the trumpet; and the third, the resurrection of the dead; yet not of all, but as it is said: The Lord shall come and all His saints with Him. Then shall the world see the Lord coming upon the clouds of heaven."
> --- Didache, Chapter 16 (ANF, Vol. 7, trans. M. B. Riddle)

**Significance:** The Didache presents a sequential narrative: (1) apostasy and false prophets, (2) the "world-deceiver" appears, (3) "the creation of men" comes into the "fire of trial," (4) signs in heaven and the trumpet, (5) resurrection, (6) the Lord comes. Its instruction is endurance through the trial; it expresses no two-stage scheme and no separation of the church from the trial --- though, as a first-century catechism, it also does not frame the modern distinctions (church, Israel, tribulation saints) the later debate turns on.

---

### Ignatius of Antioch (c. 35-107 AD)

**Context:** Bishop of Antioch, martyred in Rome under Trajan. Wrote seven authentic letters on his way to martyrdom.

**Relevant passage (to the Trallians):**
> "Flee, therefore, those evil offshoots [of Satan], which produce death-bearing fruit, whereof if any one tastes, he instantly dies. For these men are not the planting of the Father. For if they were, they would appear as branches of the cross, and their fruit would be incorruptible. By it He calls you through His passion, as being His members. The head, therefore, cannot be born by itself, without its members; God, who is [the Saviour] Himself, having promised their union."
> --- Ignatius, *To the Trallians* 11 (ANF, Vol. 1)

**Significance:** Ignatius warns about false teachers on his way to martyrdom. His concern is present faithfulness and doctrinal purity, not eschatological timeline.

---

### Polycarp of Smyrna (c. 69-155 AD)

**Context:** Disciple of the Apostle John, bishop of Smyrna, martyred c. 155 AD. His letter to the Philippians reflects apostolic-era eschatological expectations.

**Relevant passage (to the Philippians):**
> "For whosoever does not confess that Jesus Christ has come in the flesh, is antichrist; and whosoever does not confess the testimony of the cross, is of the devil; and whosoever perverts the oracles of the Lord to his own lusts, and says that there is neither a resurrection nor a judgment, he is the first-born of Satan."
> --- Polycarp, *To the Philippians* 7 (ANF, Vol. 1)

**Significance:** Polycarp uses "antichrist" language (echoing 1 John) for those who deny Christ's incarnation. He connects the antichrist spirit to denial of resurrection and judgment --- the same future events at stake in the rapture timing question. No separation of rapture from Christ's final coming.

---

### The Shepherd of Hermas (c. 100-150 AD)

**Context:** Popular early Christian vision literature, widely read in ancient churches. Considered Scripture by some (but excluded from the final canon).

**Relevant passage (Vision 4, Chapter 2):**
> "Go, therefore, and tell the elect of the Lord His mighty deeds, and say to them that this beast is a type of the great tribulation that is coming. If then you prepare yourselves, and repent with all your heart, and turn to the Lord, it will be possible for you to escape it, if your heart be pure and spotless, and you spend the rest of the days of your life in serving the Lord blamelessly."
> --- Shepherd of Hermas, *Vision* 4.2 (ANF, Vol. 2)

**Significance:** The great tribulation is presented as *coming*, and escape is explicitly held out --- but as a *conditional* promise: "if you prepare yourselves, and repent with all your heart... if your heart be pure and spotless, and you spend the rest of the days of your life in serving the Lord blamelessly." The mode of escape (removal or preservation) is unspecified, and the same vision complicates it: in Vision 4.3 the golden part of the beast's colors is those "who continue steadfast, and are put through the fire, [and] will be purified by means of it." The conditional shape is closer to the partial-rapture pattern than to either pre-trib or post-trib.

## Ante-Nicene Fathers (c. 150-325 AD)

### Irenaeus of Lyons (c. 130-202 AD)

**Context:** Bishop of Lyons, student of Polycarp, wrote extensively against Gnosticism.

**Key passage on antichrist (*Against Heresies* 5.25-30):**

> "[H]e, being an apostate and a robber, is anxious to be adored as God; and that, although a mere slave, he wishes himself to be proclaimed as a king... setting aside idols to persuade [men] that he himself is God... so that he sits in the temple of God, showing himself as if he were God... he will endeavour in a tyrannical manner to set himself forth as God... And then shall the wicked one be revealed, whom the Lord Jesus shall slay with the spirit of His mouth, and destroy by the presence of His coming; whose coming [i.e., the wicked one's] is after the working of Satan, in all power, and signs, and portents of lies."
> --- Irenaeus, *Against Heresies* 5.25.1, 3

> "But when this Antichrist shall have devastated all things in this world, he will reign for three years and six months, and sit in the temple at Jerusalem; and then the Lord will come from heaven in the clouds, in the glory of the Father, sending this man and those who follow him into the lake of fire; but bringing in for the righteous the times of the kingdom."
> --- Irenaeus, *Against Heresies* 5.30.4

**The contested passage.** One sentence of Irenaeus is the standard patristic proof-text in pre-tribulation historical defenses, and it must be faced squarely:

> "And therefore, when in the end the Church shall be suddenly caught up from this, it is said, There shall be tribulation such as has not been since the beginning, neither shall be. For this is the last contest of the righteous, in which, when they overcome they are crowned with incorruption."
> --- Irenaeus, *Against Heresies* 5.29.1 (ANF, Vol. 1, trans. Roberts-Rambaut)

The pre-trib reading: the Church is "suddenly caught up" and *then* the unequaled tribulation is announced --- an ante-Nicene pairing of the church's removal with the tribulation. The responses: (a) Irenaeus's very next sentence calls that tribulation "the last contest **of the righteous**, in which, when they overcome they are crowned" --- the righteous are *in* the contest, not absent from it; (b) the whole of 5.29 concerns the Antichrist already at work; and (c) Irenaeus's own explicit sequence one chapter later (5.30.4, above) has the Lord come *after* the Antichrist's three-and-a-half-year reign. Readers should weigh the one sentence against the stated sequence.

**Significance:** Irenaeus provides extensive commentary on the antichrist, and 5.29.1 is the closest any pre-Nicene text comes to rapture language --- yet his own stated sequence places the Lord's coming after the Antichrist's reign, and his "last contest of the righteous" places the righteous in the tribulation. He expects believers to go through the tribulation and be preserved through it.

### Justin Martyr (c. 100-165 AD)

**Context:** Apologist, philosopher, martyred in Rome. Wrote *Dialogue with Trypho* and *First Apology*.

**Key passage (*Dialogue with Trypho* 81):**

> "And further, there was a certain man with us, whose name was John, one of the apostles of Christ, who prophesied, by a revelation that was made to him, that those who believed in our Christ would dwell a thousand years in Jerusalem; and that thereafter the general, and, in short, the eternal resurrection and judgment of all men would likewise take place."
> --- Justin Martyr, *Dialogue with Trypho* 81 (ANF, Vol. 1)

**Significance:** Justin expects believers to be present in the rebuilt, earthly Jerusalem of the millennium. The passage does not itself narrate the events leading up to it; like most second-century texts it is silent on any prior removal (see the argument-from-silence caution below).

---

### Tertullian (c. 160-225 AD)

**Context:** Latin Father from Carthage, highly influential in Western Christianity.

**Key passage (*On the Resurrection of the Flesh* 25):**

> "Again in the Apocalypse of John the order of the times is laid down. This order, while beneath the altar they cry aloud for vengeance and judgement, the souls of the martyrs have learned to wait for, so that first the world may drink up its own plagues from the vials of the angels, and that harlot city may receive from the ten kings the death it deserves, and the beast Antichrist with his false prophet may bring conflict upon the church, and thus, the devil having for a season been bound in the abyss, the prerogative of the first resurrection may be set in order from the thrones."
> --- Tertullian, *On the Resurrection of the Flesh* 25 (Evans translation, SPCK 1960)

**Significance:** Tertullian explicitly lays out a sequence: plagues, the harlot city's fall, then "the beast Antichrist with his false prophet may bring conflict upon the church" *before* the first resurrection. The church faces the antichrist.

---

### Hippolytus of Rome (c. 170-236 AD)

**Context:** Roman theologian, wrote extensively on eschatology.

**Key passage (*On Christ and Antichrist* 14-15):**

> "As Christ springs from the tribe of Judah, so Antichrist is to spring from the tribe of Dan... That it is in reality out of the tribe of Dan, then, that that tyrant and king, that dread judge, that son of the devil, is destined to spring and arise, the prophet testifies when he says, 'Dan shall judge his people, as (he is) also one tribe in Israel.'"
> --- Hippolytus, *On Christ and Antichrist* 14-15 (ANF, Vol. 5)

**Significance:** Hippolytus provides detailed exposition of antichrist but does not teach a pre-tribulation rapture.

---

### Cyprian of Carthage (c. 200-258 AD)

**Context:** Bishop of Carthage, martyred under Valerian. His *De Mortalitate* (On the Mortality, c. 252) is a consolation treatise written during the great plague that bears his name. One passage from it is a standard entry in pre-tribulation historical defenses:

> "...[we] may regard it as the greatest advantage to depart from it as quickly as possible... And do you not give God thanks, do you not congratulate yourself, that by an earlier departure you are taken away, and delivered from the shipwrecks and disasters that are imminent?"
> --- Cyprian, *On the Mortality* 25 (ANF, Vol. 5, trans. Ernest Wallis)

**Significance:** The pre-trib appeal hears in "an earlier departure... delivered from the... disasters that are imminent" an early expression of removal-before-wrath. The treatise's own context is plainer: it consoles Christians dying in a plague, and the "departure" is death --- "Many of our people die in this mortality, that is, many of our people are liberated from this world" (sect. 15); "we are passing over to immortality" (sect. 22). The nearest eschatological note is the virgins who die "not fearing the threats of the coming Antichrist" (sect. 15) --- an expectation of the antichrist's arrival, not of escape from it.

### Methodius of Olympus (d. 311 AD)

**Context:** Late ante-Nicene father (martyred c. 311), wrote on resurrection and eschatology.

**Key passage (*The Banquet of the Ten Virgins*, Discourse VI, ch. IV):** Methodius reads the parable of the ten virgins together with 1 Thessalonians 4 — and places the kingdom of Antichrist *before* the trumpet and the catching-up:

> "Now the slumbering and sleeping of the virgins signifies the departure from life; and **the midnight is the kingdom of Antichrist**, during which the destroying angel passes over the houses. But the cry which was made when it was said, 'Behold the bridegroom cometh, go ye out to meet him,' is the voice which shall be heard from heaven, and **the trumpet, when the saints, all their bodies being raised, shall be caught up, and shall go on the clouds to meet the Lord**... as also Paul intimates, that 'the Lord Himself shall descend from heaven with a shout, with the voice of the archangel, and with the trump of God: and the dead in Christ shall rise first'... 'Then we which are alive shall be caught up together with them.'"
> --- Methodius, *The Banquet of the Ten Virgins*, Discourse VI, ch. IV (ANF Vol. 6, pp. 330-331, trans. Clark; verified against the ANF 6 text)

The sequence is explicit: the Bridegroom tarries, the virgins sleep, midnight — the kingdom of Antichrist — falls, and *then* the cry, the trumpet, and the catching-up to meet the Lord. A pre-Nicene father reads Matthew 25 and 1 Thessalonians 4 as one event, after Antichrist.

**Significance:** A pre-Nicene synthesis of Matthew 25 and 1 Thessalonians 4 as a single event — the kingdom of Antichrist (the parable's midnight) comes first; the trumpet, the resurrection, and the catching-up to meet the Lord follow.

---

### Commodianus (c. 250 AD)

**Context:** Latin poet, wrote *Instructions of Commodianus*.

**Key passage (*Instructions* 44):**

> "From heaven will descend the city in the first resurrection... They shall come also who overcame cruel martyrdom under Antichrist... and they themselves marrying, beget for a thousand years... But from the thousand years God will destroy all those evils."
> --- Commodianus, *Instructions* 44 (ANF, Vol. 4, trans. Wallis)

**Significance:** Early witness to premillennialism (thousand-year kingdom) --- and the millennium's citizens include those "who overcame cruel martyrdom under Antichrist": the church passes through the antichrist's persecution before the first resurrection.

---

### Victorinus of Pettau (d. 304 AD)

**Context:** Bishop of Pettau (modern Ptuj, Slovenia), martyred under Diocletian. Wrote the **earliest surviving Latin commentary on the Book of Revelation** — the *Commentary on the Apocalypse*. Represents late ante-Nicene Latin premillennialism.

**Source:** Victorinus, *Commentary on the Apocalypse* (ANF, Vol. 7, trans. Wallis).

**Significance for the ordering question:** Victorinus provides a detailed sequential reading of Revelation. On Rev 20:11-14 and the events surrounding it, he treats the church as enduring the Antichrist's persecution, with Christ descending in glory to slay the Antichrist and raise the righteous dead for the millennial reign. On 2 Thess 2, Victorinus's Rev 13 commentary quotes Paul directly: "Paul, speaking of Antichrist to the Thessalonians, says: 'Whom the Lord Jesus will slay by the breath of his mouth'" — the Antichrist is destroyed at Christ's coming, not before. Victorinus stands in the same ante-Nicene ordering the earlier Latin fathers (Irenaeus, Tertullian, Hippolytus, Cyprian) share: Antichrist first, then Christ's coming, then the gathering / first resurrection.

---

### Lactantius (c. 250-325 AD)

**Context:** Christian rhetorician known as the "Christian Cicero," tutor to Emperor Constantine's son Crispus. His *Divine Institutes* (*Institutiones Divinae*, c. 304-311) is the most detailed systematic ante-Nicene Latin end-times exposition, with Book 7 devoted to eschatology.

**Key passage (*Divine Institutes* 7.19, ANF Vol. 7, trans. Fletcher):**

> "The world therefore being oppressed, since the resources of men shall be insufficient for the overthrow of a tyranny of immense strength, inasmuch as it will press upon the captive world with great armies of robbers, that calamity so great will stand in need of divine assistance. Therefore God, being aroused both by the doubtful danger and by the wretched lamentation of the righteous, will immediately send a deliverer. Then the middle of the heaven shall be laid open in the dead and darkness of the night, that the light of the descending God may be manifest in all the world as lightning... He shall descend with a company of angels to the middle of the earth... Now this is he who is called Antichrist; but he shall falsely call himself Christ, and shall fight against the truth, and being overcome shall flee; and shall often renew the war, and often be conquered, until in the fourth battle, all the wicked being slain, subdued, and captured, he shall at length pay the penalty of his crimes."
> --- Lactantius, *Divine Institutes* 7.19 (ANF, Vol. 7, trans. Fletcher)

**Significance:** Lactantius's sequence is fully consistent with the ante-Nicene consensus: (1) a tyrant/Antichrist arises and oppresses the world; (2) the righteous suffer and cry out; (3) God sends a deliverer; (4) Christ descends visibly with a company of angels; (5) the Antichrist is defeated across a series of battles; (6) wickedness is extinguished. **The sequence is Antichrist first, then Christ's visible descent — not the reverse.** Lactantius wrote this near the end of the last great persecution and at the dawn of the Constantinian era, and his framework is a systematic Latin restatement of what Irenaeus, Tertullian, Hippolytus, and Cyprian had already taught in shorter form.

### The Cerinthus Problem: How Premillennialism Was Discredited

One of the most consequential developments in eschatological history was the guilt-by-association between premillennialism and the Gnostic heretic **Cerinthus** (fl. c. 100 AD). Cerinthus taught a carnal millennium --- a thousand-year period of feasting, drinking, and sexual indulgence on earth. His vision became a weapon against *all* premillennialists, eventually contributing to the suppression of the view for over a thousand years.

**Gaius of Rome** (early 3rd century), quoted by Eusebius (*Church History* III.28.2):

> "But Cerinthus also, by means of revelations which he pretends were written by a great apostle, brings before us marvelous things which he falsely claims were shown him by angels; and he says that after the resurrection the kingdom of Christ will be set up on earth, and that the flesh dwelling in Jerusalem will again be subject to desires and pleasures. And being an enemy of the Scriptures of God, he asserts, with the purpose of deceiving men, that there is to be a period of a thousand years for marriage festivals."
> --- Gaius of Rome, quoted in Eusebius, *Church History* III.28.2 (NPNF, Series 2, Vol. 1)

**Dionysius of Alexandria** (mid-3rd century) described Cerinthus's millennium in even more explicit terms:

> "For the doctrine which he taught was this: that the kingdom of Christ will be an earthly one. And as he was himself devoted to the pleasures of the body and altogether sensual in his nature, he dreamed that that kingdom would consist in those things which he desired, namely, in the delights of the belly and of sexual passion; that is to say, in eating and drinking and marrying, and in festivals and sacrifices and the slaying of victims."
> --- Dionysius of Alexandria, quoted in Eusebius, *Church History* VII.25 (NPNF, Series 2, Vol. 1)

The bathhouse story illustrates how viscerally the apostolic generation rejected Cerinthus:

> "[Irenaeus] says, on the authority of Polycarp, that the apostle John once entered a bath to bathe; but, learning that Cerinthus was within, he sprang from the place and rushed out of the door, for he could not bear to remain under the same roof with him. And he advised those that were with him to do the same, saying, 'Let us flee, lest the bath fall; for Cerinthus, the enemy of the truth, is within.'"
> --- Eusebius, *Church History* III.28.6, recounting Irenaeus (NPNF, Series 2, Vol. 1)

**Why this matters for the ordering question:** Cerinthus's carnal millennialism gave ammunition to those who wanted to reject *all* forms of premillennialism. If the heretic Cerinthus taught a millennium, then --- so the argument ran --- the millennium itself must be heretical. This guilt-by-association proved decisive. When Augustine later rejected premillennialism (*City of God* 20.7), he specifically cited the "immoderate carnal banquets" version --- Cerinthus's version --- as what he was rejecting.

The result was that premillennialism, which had been the dominant eschatological view of the first three centuries, was effectively suppressed in Western Christianity. The Reformation confessions made it official:

**Augsburg Confession (1530), Article XVII:**

> "They condemn also others who are now spreading certain Jewish opinions, that before the resurrection of the dead the godly shall take possession of the kingdom of the world, the ungodly being everywhere suppressed."

**Second Helvetic Confession (1566), Chapter 11:**

> "We further condemn Jewish dreams that there will be a golden age on earth before the Day of Judgment and that the pious, having subdued all their godless enemies, will possess all the kingdoms of the earth."

**Important correction:** The Council of Ephesus (431 AD) is sometimes cited as having condemned chiliasm. Recent scholarship (notably Michael J. Svigel, "The Phantom Heresy: Did the Council of Ephesus (431) Condemn Chiliasm?," *Trinity Journal* 24 NS [2003]: 105-12) has demonstrated that this claim is **unfounded** --- the council addressed Nestorianism, not premillennialism. The suppression of chiliasm came through theological argument (Augustine) and confessional condemnation (Augsburg, Second Helvetic), not through ecumenical council.

**Significance:** The disappearance of premillennialism from c. 400-1600 AD was not because the early church rejected it, but because it was suppressed through guilt-by-association with a heretic and later by confessional decree. This matters for the ordering question: the earliest Christians (who *were* premillennial) consistently placed the antichrist *before* the gathering. The later amillennial framework preserved this ordering (antichrist first, then Christ's return) while reinterpreting the millennium.

### Additional Early Church Evidence

#### Epistle of Barnabas (c. 70-130 AD)

**Context:** Anonymous early Christian letter (not by the apostle Barnabas). Chapter 4 contains eschatological warnings; chapter 21 is the closing exhortation.

**Key passage (Epistle of Barnabas 4):**

> "The final stumbling-block (or source of danger) approaches, concerning which it is written, as Enoch says, 'For for this end the Lord has cut short the times and the days that His Beloved may hasten; and He will come to the inheritance.'"
> --- Epistle of Barnabas 4 (ANF, Vol. 1, trans. Roberts-Donaldson)

**Key passage (Epistle of Barnabas 21):**

> "The day is at hand, in which everything shall be destroyed together with the Evil One. The Lord is at hand and His reward."
> --- Epistle of Barnabas 21 (Lightfoot translation)

**Significance:** Barnabas expects God to "cut short the times" (echoing Matthew 24:22) and warns that destruction comes "together with the Evil One" --- no separation of believers from the tribulation.

---

#### Clement of Rome (c. 35-100 AD)

**Context:** Bishop of Rome, wrote to the Corinthians (c. 96 AD) --- one of the earliest post-apostolic documents.

**Key passage (1 Clement 23):**

> "Of a truth, soon and suddenly shall His will be accomplished, as the Scripture also bears witness, saying, 'Speedily will He come, and will not tarry'; and, 'The Lord shall suddenly come to His temple, even the Holy One, for whom you look.'"
> --- 1 Clement 23 (ANF, Vol. 1)

**Key passage (1 Clement 24):**

> "Let us consider, beloved, how the Lord continually proves to us that there shall be a future resurrection, of which He has rendered the Lord Jesus Christ the first-fruits by raising Him from the dead."
> --- 1 Clement 24

**Significance:** Clement expects the Lord to come "suddenly" to His temple --- a singular event, not a two-stage return. The resurrection is presented as the culmination, with Christ as "first-fruits." Pre-tribulational scholars read the same passage in the opposite direction, as patristic testimony to imminence: "the church fathers were right: the rapture of the church and the beginning of the day of the Lord could come at any moment" (Robert Thomas, TMSJ 13 [2002]: 191). The passage attests expectation; whether that expectation was of a one-stage or a two-stage coming is what the two readings dispute.

---

#### Papias (c. 60-130 AD)

**Context:** Bishop of Hierapolis, student of the apostles, wrote interpretations of the Lord's sayings.

**Key passage (Fragment from Papias, quoted by Irenaeus):**

> "The days will come, in which vines shall grow, each having ten thousand branches, and in each branch ten thousand twigs, and in each true twig ten thousand shoots, and in each one of the shoots ten thousand clusters, and on every one of the clusters ten thousand grapes... In like manner [the Lord declared] that a grain of wheat would produce ten thousand ears, and that every ear should have ten thousand grains."
> --- Papias, quoted in Irenaeus, *Against Heresies* 5.33.3 (ANF, Vol. 1)

**Significance:** Papias expected a literal millennium before the final consummation. This is important because he was a direct student of the apostles and represents the earliest eschatological expectations.

---

#### Pseudo-Ephraem (c. 4th-7th century AD)

**Context:** A sermon attributed to Ephraem the Syrian (306-373 AD) but of disputed authorship. Pre-tribulation rapture advocates sometimes cite this text as early evidence for their view.

**Key passage (*On the Last Times, the Antichrist, and the End of the World*):**

> "All the saints and elect of God are gathered together before the tribulation, which is to come, and are taken to the Lord, in order that they may not see at any time the confusion which overwhelms the world because of our sins."
> --- Pseudo-Ephraem, *On the Last Times, the Antichrist, and the End of the World*, sect. 2 (trans. Cameron Rhoades, in Timothy J. Demy and Thomas D. Ice, "The Rapture and an Early Medieval Citation," *Bibliotheca Sacra* 152 [1995]: 311)

**Significance:** This is the most frequently cited "early" text in favor of a pre-tribulation rapture. However, several cautions apply: (1) the authorship is disputed --- many scholars date it to the 6th-7th century, not to Ephraem himself (Demy and Ice, 312-13); (2) the same sermon later describes the righteous within the tribulation fleeing the serpent, which complicates a pre-tribulation reading (Demy and Ice, 314-15); (3) the sense of "gathered together" is itself contested: Robert Gundry (post-trib) reads it as an evangelistic in-gathering of souls into the church rather than a physical removal, while Paul Alexander --- a Byzantinist with no stake in the rapture debate --- understood the passage as a pretribulational transportation of the saints, "an alternative to the shortening of the time intervals" (Gundry, *First the Antichrist*, 188, with Ice's response, IJOBA 1 [2020]: 100-101; Alexander, quoted in Demy and Ice, 316). Whether genuine or pseudepigraphic, this text shows the ambiguity has deep roots.

---

#### John Chrysostom (c. 347-407 AD)

**Context:** Archbishop of Constantinople, greatest Greek preacher. His Homilies on 1 Thessalonians are the most detailed patristic commentary on the rapture passage.

**Key passage (Homily 8 on 1 Thessalonians, on 4:15-17):**

> "If He is about to descend, on what account shall we be caught up? For the sake of honor. For when a king drives into a city, those who are in honor go out to meet him; but the condemned await the judge within... We are carried upon the chariot of our Father. For He received Him up in the clouds, and we shall be caught up in the clouds. Do you see how great is the honor? And as He descends, we go forth to meet Him, and, what is more blessed than all, so we shall be with Him."
> --- Chrysostom, *Homilies on First Thessalonians*, Homily 8 (NPNF, Series 1, Vol. 13)

**Significance:** Chrysostom uses the *apantēsis* (meeting) imagery explicitly --- just as citizens go out to meet an arriving king and then escort him *into* the city, believers go up to meet Christ and accompany Him back. This is the classic post-tribulation reading: the rapture is not escape but a royal welcome procession. Chrysostom does not separate the rapture from Christ's visible return.

---

#### Augustine of Hippo (c. 354-430 AD)

**Context:** Bishop of Hippo, most influential Latin father. His *City of God*, Book 20 is the most developed eschatological framework in the patristic era and became the standard Western position for over a millennium.

**Key passage on the antichrist before Christ's return (*City of God* 20.19):**

> "That which there is no doubt he said is this, that Christ will not come to judge quick and dead unless Antichrist, His adversary, first come to seduce those who are dead in soul; although their seduction is a result of God's secret judgment already passed."
> --- Augustine, *City of God* 20.19 (NPNF, Series 1, Vol. 2)

**On the restrainer of 2 Thessalonians 2:6-7 (*City of God* 20.19):**

> "I frankly confess I do not know what he means."
> --- Augustine, *City of God* 20.19

**On the millennium as the present church age (*City of God* 20.7):**

> "He used the thousand years as an equivalent for the whole duration of this world, employing the number of perfection to mark the fullness of time."
> --- Augustine, *City of God* 20.7

**On the binding of Satan (*City of God* 20.8):**

> "The devil, then, is bound and shut up in the abyss that he may not seduce the nations from which the Church is gathered, and which he formerly seduced before the Church existed."
> --- Augustine, *City of God* 20.8

**On the final persecution (*City of God* 20.11):**

> "[W]herever the Church shall be --- and it shall be in all nations, as is signified by the breadth of the earth, --- there also shall be the camp of the saints and the beloved city, and there it shall be encompassed by the savage persecution of all its enemies."
> --- Augustine, *City of God* 20.11

**On the antichrist's duration (*City of God* 20.13 and 20.23):**

> "This last persecution by Antichrist shall last for three years and six months, as we have already said... [T]he kingdom of Antichrist shall fiercely, though for a short time, assail the Church before the last judgment of God shall introduce the eternal reign of the saints."
> --- Augustine, *City of God* 20.13; 20.23

**Significance:** Augustine is explicit: **antichrist first, then Christ's return**. This is the most important patristic statement on the ordering question. His sequence: (1) the millennium is NOW (the church age), (2) Satan will be loosed at the end, (3) antichrist persecutes the church for 3.5 years, (4) Christ returns to judge the living and dead, (5) general resurrection. There is no separate rapture event --- the gathering of believers occurs at Christ's single, visible return, *after* the antichrist's persecution. Augustine's framework dominated Western Christianity for over a thousand years and remains the Catholic and many Protestant churches' position today.

## The Restrainer Debate: Who Holds Back the Antichrist?

One of the most consequential questions in early church eschatology was the identity of the "restrainer" (*to katechon* / *ho katechon*) in 2 Thessalonians 2:6-7. Paul wrote: "And now ye know what withholdeth [*to katechon*, neuter] that he might be revealed in his time. For the mystery of iniquity doth already work: only he who now letteth [*ho katechon*, masculine] will let, until he be taken out of the way" (KJV).

The shift between neuter ("what restrains") and masculine ("he who restrains") generated two lines of interpretation: the neuter was read as a system or institution, and the masculine as a person. The dominant patristic answer --- that the restrainer was the Roman Empire --- carried enormous consequences. If Rome was holding back the antichrist, then the fall of Rome became an eschatological event.

### Tertullian: The Earliest Explicit Identification (c. 197 AD)

Tertullian is the first father to explicitly name the Roman Empire as the restrainer. He does so in two separate works, making the identification a settled conviction for him, not a passing suggestion.

**In his *Apologeticum* 32.1 (c. 197 AD), written as a defense of Christianity to Roman magistrates:**

> "There is also another and a greater necessity for our offering prayer in behalf of the emperors, nay, for the complete stability of the empire, and for Roman interests in general. For we know that a mighty shock impending over the whole earth --- in fact, the very end of all things threatening dreadful woes --- is only retarded by the continued existence of the Roman empire. We have no desire, then, to be overtaken by these dire events; and in praying that their coming may be delayed, we are lending our aid to Rome's duration."
> --- Tertullian, *Apologeticum* 32.1 (ANF, Vol. 3)

**In *De Resurrectione Carnis* 24 (c. 210 AD), he is more explicit:**

> "[U]nless the disruption come first (of this empire, he means) and the man of delinquency be revealed (that is, Antichrist), the son of perdition... who but the Roman state, whose disruption, being dispersed among ten kings, will bring in Antichrist?"
> --- Tertullian, *De Resurrectione Carnis* 24 (Evans translation)

**Significance:** Tertullian's logic is striking: Christians should *pray for Rome's survival* because Rome's fall triggers the end. This means early Christians understood themselves to be living within a providential framework where political stability restrained eschatological catastrophe. The antichrist could not appear while Rome stood.

### Cyril of Jerusalem: The Catechetical Teaching (c. 348 AD)

Cyril's *Catechetical Lectures* (Lecture 15) provide one of the most detailed patristic treatments of the restrainer, delivered to baptismal candidates as part of their formal instruction in the faith:

> "But this aforesaid Antichrist is to come when the times of the Roman Empire shall have been fulfilled, and the end of the world is now drawing near. There shall rise up together ten kings of the Romans, reigning in different parts perhaps, but all about the same time; and after these an eleventh, the Antichrist, who by his magical craft shall seize upon the Roman power; and of the kings who reigned before him, three he shall humble, and the remaining seven he shall keep in subjection to himself."
> --- Cyril of Jerusalem, *Catechetical Lectures* 15.12 (NPNF, Series 2, Vol. 7)

**Significance:** Cyril directly connects the restrainer to the Roman political order and links the antichrist's rise to Daniel 7:24 (the ten kings). This was not speculation --- it was catechetical instruction given to new converts.

### Chrysostom: The Most Detailed Discussion (c. 400 AD)

John Chrysostom's *Homily 4 on 2 Thessalonians* is the most extensive patristic treatment of the restrainer. He presents two competing interpretations and takes sides:

> "**Some indeed say, the grace of the Spirit, but others the Roman empire, to whom I most of all accede.** Wherefore? Because if he meant to say the Spirit, he would not have spoken obscurely, but plainly, that even now the grace of the Spirit, that is the gifts, withhold him. And otherwise he ought now to have come, if he was about to come when the gifts ceased; for they have long since ceased."
> --- Chrysostom, *Homily 4 on 2 Thessalonians* (NPNF, Series 1, Vol. 13)

Chrysostom then explains *why* Paul spoke in code:

> "But because he said this of the Roman empire, he naturally glanced at it, and speaks covertly and darkly. **For he did not wish to bring upon himself superfluous enmities, and useless dangers.** For if he had said that after a little while the Roman empire would be dissolved, they would immediately have even overwhelmed him, as a pestilent person, and all the faithful, as living and warring to this end."

And on the mechanism of restraint:

> "Only there is one that restrains now, until he be taken out of the way, **that is, when the Roman empire is taken out of the way, then he shall come. And naturally. For as long as the fear of this empire lasts, no one will willingly exalt himself, but when that is dissolved, he will attack the anarchy, and endeavor to seize upon the government both of man and of God.**"

**Significance:** Chrysostom's argument is threefold: (1) Paul spoke obscurely because naming Rome would have brought persecution; (2) the Spirit interpretation fails because the charismata had already ceased by Chrysostom's time and the antichrist had not come; (3) the Roman Empire's political order restrains anarchy, and the antichrist exploits the vacuum. This is remarkably political theology --- the antichrist is an opportunist who fills a power vacuum.

### Jerome: The "Traditional Interpretation of All Commentators" (406-407 AD)

Jerome confirms the Roman-Empire reading was the patristic consensus:

**In his *Letter 121* to Algasia (406 AD), Question 11:**

> *"Nisi, inquit, venerit discessio primum, quod Graece dicitur apostasia, ut omnes gentes quae Romano imperio subjacent, recedant ab eis, et revelatus fuerit, id est, ostensus... homo peccati."*
>
> "Unless, he says, the falling away (*discessio*) comes first --- which in Greek is called *apostasia* --- so that all the nations which are subject to the Roman empire withdraw from them, and the man of sin be revealed, that is, made manifest."
> --- Jerome, *Epistola* 121, Q. 11 (PL 22, cols. 1023ff; Latin text verified against the PL edition; translation ours --- no complete public-domain English of Letter 121 exists)

Jerome interprets the "falling away" not as religious apostasy but as the *political disintegration* of Roman imperial authority --- after which the man of sin is revealed.

**In his *Commentary on Daniel* 7:8 (407 AD):**

> "We should therefore concur with the traditional interpretation of **all the commentators** of the Christian Church, that at the end of the world, when the Roman Empire is to be destroyed, there shall be ten kings who will partition the Roman world amongst themselves."
> --- Jerome, *Commentary on Daniel* (PL 25; trans. Archer)

**Significance:** By 407, Jerome could say that the Roman-Empire reading was "the traditional interpretation of **all** the commentators." This is the closest thing we have to a claimed patristic consensus on the restrainer.

### The Dissenters: Theodore and Theodoret

Not all fathers agreed. **Theodore of Mopsuestia** (c. 350-428) and **Theodoret of Cyrrhus** (c. 393-458), both from the Antiochene school, identified the restrainer not as Rome but as **God Himself**. The masculine *ho katechon* = God; the neuter *to katechon* = God's providential decree. The antichrist comes only when God permits it.

This theocentric reading is notable because the Antiochene school was known for literal-historical exegesis, yet on this point they diverged from the dominant political reading. Their dissent was a minority position, but it would later find echoes in Augustine's agnosticism.

### Augustine: The Honest Agnostic (c. 426 AD)

Augustine surveys three options in *City of God* 20.19 and then declines to choose:

**(a) The Roman Empire:** "Some think that the Apostle Paul referred to the Roman empire, and that he was unwilling to use language more explicit, lest he should incur the calumnious charge of wishing ill to the empire which it was hoped would be eternal."

**(b) Nero redivivus:** "He alluded to Nero, whose deeds already seemed to be as the deeds of Antichrist. And hence some suppose that he shall rise again and be Antichrist."

**(c) The wicked within the Church:** "That the words, 'Ye know what withholdeth'... refer only to the wicked and the hypocrites who are in the Church, until they reach a number so great as to furnish Antichrist with a great people."

Then: **"I frankly confess I do not know what he means."**

**Significance:** Augustine's honesty is instructive. He reports the Roman-Empire reading as the leading view, but unlike Jerome, he does not endorse it. His restraint would prove prescient: the fall of Rome would soon force a reckoning with the political reading.

### The Crisis of 476 and the *Translatio Imperii*

If the Roman Empire was the restrainer, and the Western Roman Empire fell on September 4, 476 --- when Odoacer deposed Romulus Augustulus --- then the restrainer had been "taken out of the way." Where was the antichrist?

This created a genuine eschatological crisis. Tertullian had said Rome's dissolution into ten kingdoms would "bring in Antichrist." Cyril had said the antichrist comes "when the times of the Roman Empire shall have been fulfilled." Chrysostom had said "when the Roman empire is taken out of the way, then he shall come."

The crisis was resolved through two theological moves:

**(a) The Eastern Empire continued.** The Byzantine Empire survived until 1453 and claimed continuity with Rome. The restrainer had not fully been removed.

**(b) *Translatio imperii* --- the transfer of empire.** The idea that imperial authority was not destroyed but transferred to successor kingdoms. This became the dominant medieval solution.

**Adso of Montier-en-Der** (c. 950), a Benedictine abbot, wrote the most influential medieval treatise on the antichrist (*De Ortu et Tempore Antichristi*). He resolved the crisis explicitly:

> "Even though we see that the Empire of the Romans is for the most part destroyed, nevertheless, as long as the kings of the Franks, who possess the Roman Empire by right, survive, the dignity of the Roman Empire will not perish altogether."
> --- Adso of Montier-en-Der, *De Ortu et Tempore Antichristi* (c. 950; CC Cont. Med. 45)

The restrainer role transferred from Rome to the Carolingian/Frankish monarchy, and later to the Holy Roman Empire. This "Last Emperor" legend --- drawn from the 7th-century Syriac *Apocalypse of Pseudo-Methodius* --- held that a final Christian emperor would reign, then lay down his crown at Jerusalem, and only then would the antichrist appear.

Later still, **Thomas Aquinas** (c. 1265) transferred the restrainer role from the Empire to the **Roman Church** itself, arguing that the institutional Church now performed the restraining function. The historicist Reformers would invert this completely: the papacy was not the restrainer but the *antichrist* (see Appendix E).

### Why the Restrainer Matters for the Ordering Question

The restrainer debate reinforces the unanimous early church position on ordering:

1. **The restrainer must be removed first** --- whether Rome, God's decree, or something else
2. **Then the antichrist is revealed** --- the "man of sin" appears
3. **Then Christ returns** --- to "consume with the spirit of his mouth" (2 Thessalonians 2:8)

No patristic interpretation of the restrainer produces a pre-tribulation rapture. Every identification --- political, theological, or agnostic --- places the antichrist's revelation *before* Christ's return and the gathering of believers.

## The Eastern Orthodox Witness

The Eastern Orthodox tradition provides a continuous line of eschatological teaching that is often overlooked in Western discussions of the rapture question. Two sources are particularly important.

### John of Damascus (c. 675-749 AD)

John of Damascus's *An Exact Exposition of the Orthodox Faith* (Book IV, Chapter 26) provides the most systematic Eastern treatment of the antichrist. It functions as a summary of the patristic consensus for the Eastern church:

> "It should be known that the Antichrist is bound to come. Every one, therefore, who confesses not that the Son of God came in the flesh and is perfect God and became perfect man, after being God, is Antichrist... But in a peculiar and special sense he who comes at the consummation of the age is called Antichrist."
> --- John of Damascus, *De Fide Orthodoxa* IV.26 (NPNF, Series 2, Vol. 9)

On the antichrist's deceptive strategy:

> "And in the beginning of his rule, or rather tyranny, he assumes the role of sanctity. But when he becomes master, he persecutes the Church of God and displays all his wickedness."

On the sending of witnesses and Christ's return:

> "But Enoch and Elias the Thesbite shall be sent and shall turn the hearts of the fathers to the children, that is, the synagogue to our Lord Jesus Christ and the preaching of the apostles: and they will be destroyed by him. And the Lord shall come out of heaven, just as the holy apostles beheld Him going into heaven, perfect God and perfect man, with glory and power, and will destroy the man of lawlessness, the son of destruction, with the breath of His mouth."

**The sequence according to John of Damascus:**
1. Gospel preached to all nations
2. Antichrist arises (indwelt by Satan, feigns sanctity)
3. Antichrist persecutes the Church
4. Enoch and Elijah sent to turn Israel back to Christ
5. Antichrist destroys Enoch and Elijah
6. Christ returns from heaven and destroys the antichrist

**Significance:** John of Damascus writes in the 8th century, summarizing centuries of Eastern reflection. His sequence is identical to what the Didache laid out 600+ years earlier: antichrist first, persecution of believers, *then* Christ's return. The Orthodox tradition never developed a pre-tribulation rapture.

### Andrew of Caesarea (6th-7th century)

Andrew's *Commentary on the Apocalypse* is the standard Eastern Orthodox commentary on Revelation. It eclipsed all other Greek commentaries and remains authoritative for the Greek, Slavic, Armenian, and Georgian churches.

On the mark of the beast (Revelation 13):

> "He... will strive to place upon all the outline of the ruinous name of the apostate and deceiver, 'in their right hands,' in order to cut off the doing of right and good deeds, and likewise 'in their foreheads,' in order to instruct the deceived to be bold in deception and darkness."
> --- Andrew of Caesarea, *Commentary on the Apocalypse* (PG 106; trans. Constantinou)

On the thousand years (Revelation 20):

> "By the number one thousand years by no means is it reasonable to understand so many years... rather they are to mean many generations."

**Significance:** Andrew provides the first systematic non-premillennial reading of Revelation 20 from an Eastern perspective. Like Augustine in the West, he interprets the "thousand years" symbolically as the entire church age. But like all Eastern fathers, he places the antichrist's persecution of the church *before* Christ's return.

### Modern Orthodox Teaching

The Orthodox tradition has no single equivalent to the Catholic Catechism (CCC 675-677), but its teaching is consistent. **Philaret of Moscow's *Longer Catechism*** (1830), the most authoritative doctrinal standard of the Russian Orthodox Church, lists among the signs preceding Christ's coming: decreased faith, increased wickedness, worldwide gospel preaching, and "the coming of Antichrist" (Questions 234-235).

As Metropolitan Hilarion Alfeyev observes: "By contrast, New Testament and patristic eschatology is one of hope and assurance: it was Christ-centred rather than Antichrist-centred" (*The Mystery of Faith*, ch. "The End of History").

The Orthodox Church in America teaches that the Second Coming brings the Last Day, the great judgment, the general resurrection, and the transfiguration of all creation together --- not as separate chronological events (see Thomas Hopko, *The Orthodox Faith*, oca.org). This explicitly rejects any pre-tribulation rapture framework.

## Post-Nicene Summary

The post-Nicene fathers and the Eastern Orthodox tradition continued the pre-Nicene consensus without developing a pre-tribulation rapture doctrine:

1. **Christ returns at the end of the age** --- this is when the rapture/resurrection occurs
2. **Antichrist must come first** --- the "man of sin" in 2 Thessalonians 2 is revealed before Christ's return
3. **Believers will face tribulation** --- persecution and trials continue until Christ's return
4. **The restrainer must be removed** --- whether Rome, God's decree, or something else, the restrainer's removal triggers the antichrist's appearance
5. **The resurrection is at Christ's coming** --- not before

### Modern reception of the pre-Nicene consensus

The historicist summary above is not only the study's reading; it is how the same corpus is read today by scholars whose vocation is the pre-Nicene tradition itself. David Bercot --- editor of *A Dictionary of Early Christian Beliefs* (Hendrickson, 1998) and author of *Will the Real Heretics Please Stand Up?* --- puts the ordering conclusion this way in his lecture *The Millennium and Rapture*:

> "Well, were they pre-trib, mid-trib, post-trib? It sounds like from those passages that the early Christians, or at least the ones who wrote about it, who speculated on it, were mid-trib. It sounds like certainly none of them thought they would be raptured before tribulation because they were enduring horrible persecution during the time when they lived. So, they hardly thought it likely that they would be taken from the earth right when the great challenge came of the Antichrist. But they did think that many believers would be slain by the Antichrist and that others, as Tertullian puts, it's sort of an instantaneous death where they're taken up away from the earth and receive a different body."
> --- David Bercot, *The Millennium and Rapture*, Divine Secrets Lesson 4 (thehistoricfaith.com), retrieved 2026-08-06

Bercot's summary matches the study's read at the load-bearing point: no pre-Nicene writer thought the church would be raptured *before* the Antichrist's oppression. His characterization ("mid-trib") is a scoping match with the study's Chapter 4 mid-trib and pre-wrath treatments; the differences from strict post-trib are handled at Chapter 4's mid-trib section. (Bercot's own reference to Tertullian's "instantaneous death... they're taken up" points to *Against Marcion* V.12 and *On the Resurrection of the Flesh* 41, quoted verbatim in Appendix B.)

## Rabbinic Views on the End Times

The Jewish rabbinic tradition developed rich eschatological expectations. While different from Christian eschatology, examining what the rabbis taught illuminates the conceptual world in which the New Testament was written. Jesus's phrase "the beginning of sorrows" (Matthew 24:8) is a direct echo of the rabbinic *chevlei Mashiach* --- "birth pangs of the Messiah."

### The Seven-Year Cycle Before the Messiah

**Key passage (Babylonian Talmud, Sanhedrin 97a):**

The Talmud describes a sabbatical cycle of events preceding the Messiah:

> The Sages taught in a baraita: With regard to the seven-year period, i.e., the Sabbatical cycle, during which the Messiah, son of David, comes: During the first year, this verse will be fulfilled: “And I will cause it to rain upon one city and cause it not to rain upon another city” (Amos 4:7). During the second year of that period, arrows of famine will be shot, indicating that there will be famine only in certain places. During the third year there will be a great famine, and men, women, children, the pious, and men of action will die, and the Torah is forgotten by those who study it. During the fourth year there will be plenty but not great plenty. During the fifth year there will be great plenty and they will eat, and drink, and rejoice, and the Torah will return to those who study it. During the sixth year, heavenly voices will be heard. During the Sabbatical Year, wars, e.g., the war of Gog and Magog, will be waged involving the Jewish people. During the year after the conclusion of the Sabbatical Year, the son of David will come.
> --- Babylonian Talmud, Sanhedrin 97a (William Davidson edition)

**Significance:** The rabbis expected a structured period of tribulation *before* the Messiah --- including famine, wars, and heavenly signs. This sequence parallels Jesus's Olivet Discourse (Matthew 24:6-8) remarkably. The Messiah comes *after* the tribulation, not before.

### The Conditions for the Messiah's Coming

**Key passage (Babylonian Talmud, Sanhedrin 97a):**

> Rabbi Yitzḥak says: The son of David will not come until the entire kingdom will be converted to heresy.
> --- Babylonian Talmud, Sanhedrin 97a (William Davidson edition)

**Key passage (Babylonian Talmud, Sanhedrin 97b):**

A major dispute about whether repentance is a precondition:

> Rabbi Eliezer says: If the Jewish people repent they are redeemed, and if not they are not redeemed. Rabbi Yehoshua said to him: If they do not repent, will they not be redeemed at all? Rather, the Holy One, Blessed be He, will establish a king for them whose decrees are as harsh as those issued by Haman, and the Jewish people will have no choice but to repent, and this will restore them to the right path.
> --- Babylonian Talmud, Sanhedrin 97b (William Davidson edition)

The Talmud also contains a famous warning against date-setting:

> Rabbi Shmuel bar Naḥmani says that Rabbi Yonatan says: May those who calculate the end of days be cursed [tippaḥ], as they would say once the end of days that they calculated arrived and the Messiah did not come, that he will no longer come at all. Rather, the proper behavior is to continue to wait for his coming, as it is stated: “Though it tarry, wait for it.”
> --- Babylonian Talmud, Sanhedrin 97b (William Davidson edition)

### The Birth Pangs of the Messiah (Chevlei Mashiach)

**Key passage (Mishnah, Sotah 9:15):**

The Mishnah's description of the "footsteps of the Messiah" (*ikveta d'meshicha*):

> In the times of the approach of the Messiah, impudence will increase and high costs will pile up. Although the vine shall bring forth its fruit, wine will nevertheless be expensive. And the monarchy shall turn to heresy, and there will be no one to give reproof about this. The meeting place of the Sages will become a place of promiscuity, and the Galilee shall be destroyed, and the Gavlan will be desolate, and the men of the border shall go round from city to city to seek charity, but they will find no mercy. And the wisdom of scribes will putrefy, and people who fear sin will be held in disgust, and the truth will be absent. The youth will shame the face of elders, elders will stand before minors. Normal family relations will be ruined: A son will disgrace a father; a daughter will rise up against her mother, a daughter-in-law against her mother-in-law. A man’s enemies will be the members of his household. The face of the generation will be like the face of a dog; a son will no longer be ashamed before his father. And upon what is there for us to rely? Only upon our Father in heaven.
> --- Mishnah Sotah 9:15 (William Davidson edition)

**Significance:** This passage strikingly parallels Jesus's warnings in Matthew 24:10-12 about betrayal, hatred, and the love of many growing cold. The final line --- "And upon what is there for us to rely? Only upon our Father in heaven" --- echoes Jesus's call to watch and trust God.

### Sages Who Dreaded the Messiah's Coming

**Key passage (Babylonian Talmud, Sanhedrin 98b):**

Several sages explicitly said they did not want to witness the Messianic era because of the suffering that would precede it:

> Ulla says: Let the Messiah come, but after my death, so that I will not see him, as I fear the suffering that will precede his coming. Likewise, Rabba says: Let the Messiah come, but after my death, so that I will not see him. Rav Yosef says: Let the Messiah come, and I will be privileged to sit in the shadow of his donkey’s excrement. I am willing to undergo all the pain and disgrace associated with his arrival.
> --- Babylonian Talmud, Sanhedrin 98b (William Davidson edition)

> And so too Rabbi Yoḥanan said: Let the Messiah come, but after my death, so that I will not see him.
> --- Babylonian Talmud, Sanhedrin 98b (William Davidson edition)

**Significance:** The tribulation preceding the Messiah was so fearsome that multiple sages preferred not to experience it. Only Rav Yosef was willing to endure anything for the sake of seeing the redemption. This mirrors the pastoral question in rapture timing: are believers called to endure the tribulation or to escape it?

### The War of Gog and Magog and the Pangs of the Messiah

**Key passage (Babylonian Talmud, Pesachim 118a):**

The Talmud identifies key themes within the Hallel psalms (Psalms 113-118):

> The regular hallel contains these five matters: The remembrance of the exodus from Egypt, the splitting of the Red Sea, the giving of the Torah, the resurrection of the dead, and the pangs of the Messiah.
> --- Babylonian Talmud, Pesachim 118a (William Davidson edition)

> The pangs of the Messiah, as it is written: “Not to us, God, not to us, but to Your name give glory” (Psalms 115:1). And Rabbi Yoḥanan said: The verse “Not to us, God, not to us” and the entire psalm, including the verse “Why should the nations say, where now is their God?” (Psalms 115:2), is referring to the era of the enslavement of the kingdoms and the redemption of the Jewish people from their dominion. Some say that Rabbi Yoḥanan said: The verse “Not to us, God, not to us” is referring to the war of Gog and Magog, the catastrophes and wars that will befall the Jewish people in the end of days from which they will be delivered.
> --- Babylonian Talmud, Pesachim 118a (William Davidson edition)

**Key passage (Babylonian Talmud, Sukkah 52a):**

The rabbis also expected a "Messiah son of Joseph" (*Mashiach ben Yosef*) who would die in battle before the final Messiah comes:

> Rabbi Dosa and the Rabbis disagree concerning this matter. One said that this eulogy is for Messiah ben Yosef who was killed in the war of Gog from the land of Magog prior to the ultimate redemption with the coming of Messiah ben David. And one said that this eulogy is for the evil inclination that was killed. The Gemara asks: Granted, according to the one who said that the lament is for Messiah ben Yosef who was killed, this would be the meaning of that which is written in that context: “And they shall look unto Me because they have thrust him through; and they shall mourn for him, as one mourns for his only son” (Zechariah 12:10).
> --- Babylonian Talmud, Sukkah 52a (William Davidson edition)

**Significance:** The rabbinic tradition expected a final apocalyptic war and even a suffering Messianic figure who dies before the ultimate redemption --- deep Jewish background for the New Testament's expectation of tribulation, conflict, and deliverance.

### Shabbat Observance as Protection from Tribulation

**Key passage (Babylonian Talmud, Shabbat 118a):**

> Rabbi Shimon ben Pazi said that Rabbi Yehoshua ben Levi said in the name of bar Kappara: Anyone who fulfills the obligation to eat three meals on Shabbat is rescued from three punishments: From the pangs of the Messiah, i.e., the suffering that precedes the advent of Messiah, and from the judgment of Gehenna, and from the war of Gog and Magog.
> --- Babylonian Talmud, Shabbat 118a (William Davidson edition)

**Significance:** The rabbis conceived of the Messianic birth pangs and the war of Gog and Magog as distinct tribulations. Faithful observance provides protection --- a concept that resonates with the Christian debate about whether faithfulness brings *escape* from or *preservation through* tribulation.

### The Eschatological Sequence in the Amidah

**Key passage (Babylonian Talmud, Megillah 17b):**

The Talmud explains that the order of blessings in the daily Amidah prayer reflects the sequence of end-times events: gathering of exiles, then judgment of the wicked, then exaltation of the righteous, then rebuilding of Jerusalem, then the Messiah, then restoration of prayer, then Temple service, then resurrection of the dead.

> Once Jerusalem is rebuilt, the Messiah, scion of the house of David, will come.
> --- Babylonian Talmud, Megillah 17b

**Significance:** The gathering of exiles comes as part of the final sequence --- not as a separate earlier event. This is the closest rabbinic parallel to the rapture question, and the sequence places the gathering *within* (not before) the eschatological drama.

## Patterns and Observations

### What the Early Church Taught:

1. **Christ will return visibly** — no debate
2. **The antichrist/man of sin will be revealed** — widely expected
3. **Believers will face tribulation** — persecution expected, tribulation mentioned
4. **Resurrection of the dead** — core doctrine
5. **No clearly articulated pre-tribulation rapture** — the specific doctrine does not appear in pre-Nicene literature

### What the Early Church Did NOT Teach:

- A "rapture" as a separate event before the tribulation
- The church being taken to heaven before antichrist appears
- A specific timeline of 7 years, 3.5 years, etc.

### Timing of the Pre-Tribulation View

The pre-tribulation rapture is a distinctly modern doctrine:
- Emerged in the circle around Edward Irving and the Albury conferences (1826-1830), with mature pre-tribulational doctrine documented in the Irvingite meetings by 1832 (Baxter 1833); systematized by John Nelson Darby in the late 1840s (~1849-1850) — see Appendix F
- Spread through the Plymouth Brethren from the Powerscourt conferences (1833) onward, and carried into American evangelicalism by the Bible-conference movement, Scofield, and the popular dispensational literature
- Developed through the dispensationalist movement
- Became prominent in American evangelicalism in the 20th century

This is NOT a criticism — it may be a valid interpretation of Scripture that earlier Christians simply didn't develop. But it is historically accurate to note that the earliest Christians did not hold this view.

### Geographic and Temporal Patterns

- **East (Greek Fathers):** More focused on spiritual interpretation, less on timeline
- **West (Latin Fathers):** More literal interpretation, but still no pre-tribulation rapture
- **Development over time:** As the church aged, eschatological expectation evolved, but the core remained: Christ returns, antichrist opposes, believers are tested

---

## Summary

The pre-Nicene fathers provide a consistent picture:

1. **No pre-tribulation rapture doctrine** — This specific teaching does not appear in any pre-Nicene writer
2. **Antichrist expected** — Most wrote about the coming antichrist as a future reality
3. **Believers face tribulation** — The expectation was that Christians would face trials
4. **Christ returns at the end** — The return is singular, not divided into separate events

This does not settle the debate — early Christians could have been wrong on this point. But it does mean that the pre-tribulation rapture is a development, not a retrieval of early teaching.
